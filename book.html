As I Lay Dying

AuthorWilliam Faulkner
StyleSouthern Gothic, Stream of Consciousness
Year1930
Pages~267
Plot Synopsis

When Addie Bundren dies in rural Mississippi, she leaves her family with one strange, stubborn instruction: carry her coffin to Jefferson to be buried among her own people. What follows is a broken, sweltering odyssey — a wagon hauling a rotting body through flood and fire, a family fraying at the seams under grief, pride, and self-interest. Faulkner tells the story through fifteen different narrators, each chapter a fragment of consciousness, so the truth of the Bundrens only emerges in the gaps between what they each refuse to say. It's a novel about the distance between people who share a name and a house, and about what a body means once the person is gone.

Why You Should Read It

This is Faulkner at his most structurally daring — fifteen voices, including one dead woman's, stitched into a single journey, and once you find the rhythm of jumping between minds it reads faster than its reputation suggests. If you liked the stripped-down intensity of Blood Meridian or The Road, this is where a lot of that lineage starts: terse, brutal, unsentimental about family and land. It rewards close reading rather than punishing it — each narrator adds a piece you didn't know you were missing, and the final picture is darker and stranger than the setup lets on. At under 300 pages, it's the most approachable entry point into Faulkner's harder-edged style.
