Blood Meridian, or the Evening Redness in the West

AuthorCormac McCarthy
StyleRevisionist Anti-Western · Historical Epic
Year1985
Pages337
Plot Synopsis

In 1849 a nameless runaway from Tennessee — called only “the kid” — drifts into the Texas–Mexico borderlands and falls in with the Glanton gang, a company of scalp hunters contracted by Mexican governors to take Apache hair, and soon indifferent to whose hair they take. What follows is a year of riding through a scorched, biblical desert, rendered in prose that is King James thunder one sentence and forensic butchery the next. Presiding over the company is Judge Holden: hairless, enormous, monstrously erudite, a man who lectures on geology and law while cataloguing the world in order to own it, and who insists that war is God. The kid’s slow, nearly wordless refusal of the judge is the only moral pulse in a novel that otherwise declines to console anyone.

Why You Should Read It

You’ve already read The Road, so you know McCarthy’s voice pared down to ash — this is that same voice at full orchestral volume, decades before he learned restraint. It may also be the book that finally cracks Moby-Dick open for you: the same white-whale architecture, the same unknowable antagonist, the same willingness to stop the plot cold and stare into the abyss for a page — but in 337 pages rather than six hundred. Fair warning, it earns its reputation: the violence is relentless and never redeemed, there are no quotation marks, and McCarthy offers no handholds. Read it for the sentences; few American novels have ever put language on a page like this.
