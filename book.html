Blindness

AuthorJosé Saramago
StyleAllegorical Dystopian Fiction / Portuguese Modernism
Year1995
Pages352
Plot Synopsis

A man stopped at a traffic light goes suddenly, inexplicably blind — not into darkness, but into a blank, luminous white. Within days the affliction has swept an unnamed city, and the first of the infected are herded into a derelict mental asylum, guarded by soldiers who shoot anyone who approaches the gate. Inside, food runs short, order collapses, and a criminal faction seizes the rations and begins trading them for the bodies of the women. One woman — the doctor's wife — has inexplicably kept her sight, and says nothing, becoming the sole witness to what people do when no one can see them.

Why You Should Read It

Saramago writes in long, unbroken paragraphs with no quotation marks and no character names — just "the doctor's wife," "the boy with the squint" — and it takes about ten pages before that stops feeling like an obstacle and starts feeling like the only way the story could be told. The prose runs like a held breath, which suits a book about endurance: it is fundamentally a novel about how long people can keep going, and what they become on the far side of that. It is bleak, but it is not nihilistic — it argues, quietly and hard, that dignity is something you choose rather than something you're granted. If you've read Camus or McCarthy's The Road, this is the book that sits between them, and it earned Saramago the Nobel three years after publication.
