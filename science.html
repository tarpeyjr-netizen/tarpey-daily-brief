Space

This Week in Space

Astronomy is, at heart, the art of reading very faint handwriting. Almost everything we know about the cosmos arrives as a trickle of light or X-rays that has crossed unimaginable distances, and the real skill lies in building instruments steady and sensitive enough to make sense of it. This week offered a nice spread of that craft, from a new telescope proving it can hold perfectly still to a long-awaited glimpse of two stars caught in the act of feeding one another.

The steadiest news came from NASA's Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope, which has shown it can lock onto a target with the precision of aiming a laser pointer at a dime from about 150 miles away, and which gave its planet-imaging coronagraph its first look at the sky. A coronagraph is essentially a tiny artificial eclipse: it blocks a star's glare so that the dim planets orbiting it are not drowned out, much as you might raise a hand to shade your eyes from the sun to see a bird beside it. Meanwhile, astronomers using Japan's XRISM observatory watched, for the first time directly, a giant star's wind being captured by its compact companion, producing flares of X-rays as the stolen gas fell in. Closer to home, the joint European-Chinese Smile mission began science operations with eerie images of auroras rippling over the North Pole in broad daylight, and NASA announced that its PRIMA far-infrared telescope is moving to the next development phase as the first of a new class of mid-sized Probe Explorer missions.

Why should a curious non-scientist care? Because these are the tools that decide what we will be able to see for the next decade. Sharper pointing and better starlight-blocking mean that, before long, we may be taking pictures of worlds around other stars rather than merely inferring their existence, and watching stellar relationships play out in X-rays tells us how stars live, feed and die. The sky has not changed this week, but our ability to read it just got a little better.

Sunday Long Read

The Flask That Has Been Evolving Since 1988

Evolutionary Biology

On February 24, 1988, a young evolutionary biologist named Richard Lenski at Michigan State University did something that sounds almost too simple to be science. He took a single harmless strain of the gut bacterium E. coli, split it into twelve identical flasks, and fed each one a thin broth of sugar. The next morning he diluted a bit of each culture into fresh broth, and the morning after that he did it again. Nearly four decades later, his lab and its successors are still doing it. The twelve lineages have now lived through well over 80,000 generations, which, by the usual yardstick of human generations, is roughly the span since our own species left Africa, multiplied several times over.

The idea was to watch evolution happen in a bottle and, crucially, to be able to rerun it. Every 75 days, about 500 generations, the team froze a sample of every population. That freezer is a living fossil record, and unlike a fossil, it can be revived. If something interesting happens in generation 30,000, you can thaw the ancestors from generation 20,000 and replay the tape to see whether the outcome was destiny or a lucky accident. It is the closest biology has come to the thought experiment the late Stephen Jay Gould once posed: what would happen if you rewound the history of life and let it run again?

For years the answer looked mostly like destiny. All twelve populations grew faster and bigger, and they did so in a strikingly predictable pattern, with rapid improvement early on that slowed to a crawl, like a student who learns most of a language in the first few months. Yet the details beneath that smooth curve were different in every flask. Each lineage found its own mutations. Chance, it turned out, writes the specifics even when selection sets the direction.

Then, around 2003, came the flask that broke the pattern. The broth contains a little glucose, which the bacteria eat, and a lot of citrate, a molecule that is there only to help hold metals in solution. E. coli, as a species, famously cannot eat citrate when oxygen is around; this inability is even used as a standard lab test to identify the microbe. One morning a graduate student, Zachary Blount, noticed that one flask, called Ara-3, had turned unusually cloudy. Its bacteria were thriving on the citrate, a food source that none of their ancestors could touch. A single lineage had, in effect, discovered a new way of making a living.

The real question was whether this was a one-in-a-trillion mutation or something deeper. Because of the freezer, the team could find out. They revived Ara-3 from different points in history and let each sample evolve again, thousands of times over. The ability to eat citrate reappeared far more often in samples from after roughly generation 20,000 than in earlier ones. Something had happened in the meantime that did not give the bacteria the skill but made the skill easier to acquire. Evolutionary biologists call this potentiation, and it is a bit like laying the groundwork for a bridge before anyone has decided to build it. Later work traced the final step to a copying accident that duplicated a stretch of DNA and placed a citrate-transporting gene next to a switch that turned it on when oxygen was present.

This is why the story matters beyond one microbe. It suggests that big evolutionary novelties may not arise from a single dramatic leap but from a quiet buildup of earlier, seemingly unrelated changes, each of which looked like nothing at the time. History matters. Had Ara-3 not wandered down a particular path earlier, the flask might still be eating only glucose today.

The experiment has also had a bumpier public life than you might expect. In 2008 a prominent online encyclopedia run by creationists accused Lenski of fudging his results, and he responded with a famously patient, polite and devastating series of letters, which have become a small classic of scientific correspondence. The experiment survived that, and it survived Lenski's retirement. In 2022 stewardship passed to Jeffrey Barrick at the University of Texas at Austin, who has kept the daily ritual going and has added modern genome sequencing to the freezer's old treasures. Recent analyses of the sequence data show that genes tied to growth tended to adapt early while those involved in survival adapted later, and that evolution at the gene level slows as bacteria get better, though never to zero.

What remains unknown is whether it ever really stops. In theory a population in a constant environment should eventually hit a peak, where every possible mutation is a step downhill. In practice, after tens of thousands of generations, the bacteria are still getting better at living in the flask, and the mathematical models suggest the improvement could continue more or less forever, getting ever more gradual. That may be the quietest and most remarkable lesson in the flasks. Evolution is not a sprint toward perfection, and it is not a lottery. It is a patient process, shaped by chance and history, running every day on a bench in Texas, long after the person who started it stopped watching the clock.

Neuroscience

This Week in Neuroscience

The brain has a way of making every other organ look simple. It is three pounds of tissue running on roughly the power of a dim light bulb, and yet it composes symphonies, holds grudges and keeps your heart beating without being asked. Part of what makes it so hard to study is that we keep discovering it is not one thing. This week's headlines tug at that idea from several directions: the brain's plumbing, its fear circuitry, and even the sound of your voice as a window onto how fast it is aging.

Start with the plumbing. Researchers at The Ottawa Hospital and the University of Ottawa, writing in Neuron on September 30, reported that they reversed core autism-like behaviors in mice by repairing the blood vessels in the brain. Neurons get all the glory, but they are demanding tenants, and they rely on a responsive supply of blood the way a busy restaurant relies on deliveries arriving exactly when the kitchen needs them. In the mouse model, that delivery system was faulty; restoring healthy vessel function brought back normal calcium signalling in brain cells and eased repetitive behaviors, hyperactivity and motor learning problems. Mice are not people, and autism is far too varied to be explained by a single fix, but it is a striking reminder that the brain's supporting cast may matter as much as its stars. A day later, a Rutgers team led by John McGann published in Nature Communications on how the brain decides which smells, sounds or sights should trigger fear after a bad experience. Think of it as the brain's editing desk, working out which clues in a scene actually predicted the danger so that you do not flinch at everything. Understanding that selectivity matters, because anxiety and PTSD can be seen as the editor losing its sense of proportion.

Then there is the voice. On October 2, an international group associated with the Global Brain Health Institute described in Science Advances a computational “speech clock” that estimates biological aging and dementia risk from voice recordings alone. Speech is one of the most complicated things we do, coordinating memory, word retrieval, breath and tiny muscle movements, so subtle changes in timing and tone may be an early tripwire. Elsewhere, recent reports have pointed to long COVID possibly damaging dopamine-releasing neurons, a needle-thin flexible fiber that can record, stimulate and deliver drugs at once, and stem cell transplants that helped regenerate stroke-damaged tissue in mice. Those are early findings and several are still at the animal or proof-of-concept stage, so they deserve a raised eyebrow along with the excitement. A Stanford-led paper last month, arguing the human brain grew from two ancient nervous systems fused together, is the backdrop to all of it.

So what should a curious non-scientist take from the week? Mostly that the brain is looking less like a computer made of neurons and more like an ecosystem, with blood vessels, support cells, ancient circuits and modern ones all affecting one another. That is good news in a practical sense: more moving parts means more places to intervene, and a cheap tool like a voice recording might one day help catch trouble years before memory fails. The caveat, as always, is that the journey from a mouse or a lab algorithm to a clinic is long.

Epidemiology

This Week in Epidemiology

Epidemiology is, at heart, the art of counting things that nobody wants to be counted in: the sick, the exposed, the lost. It is detective work done at the scale of whole populations, where the clues are case tallies, hospital admissions and the slow geography of who got ill and where. This week those tallies told three very different stories at once, and together they sketch the shape of the public-health year so far.

The loudest is in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, where an Ebola outbreak caused by the Bundibugyo virus, a less familiar cousin of the Zaire strain behind the 2014 West Africa epidemic, keeps growing. The World Health Organization reported nearly 7,900 confirmed cases and about 3,800 deaths as of September 23, with two more health zones affected by September 25. Counts reported by outbreak trackers are higher still, topping 8,000 cases. That makes it the largest Bundibugyo outbreak ever recorded and the second largest Ebola outbreak in history. A case-fatality ratio near 48 percent means roughly one in two people infected has died. Epidemiologists watch how fast an outbreak spreads as closely as how big it is, and this one is among the fastest-growing on record. Nearer to home, measles is writing its own grim record. The CDC counted 3,659 confirmed U.S. cases for 2026 as of September 24, well above the 2,289 recorded in all of 2025 and the most since the disease was declared eliminated in 2000. About 95 percent of cases are tied to outbreaks, with Pennsylvania the hardest hit at more than 800 cases. There has been one confirmed death, and investigators are examining three more.

The common thread is that outbreaks are rarely a surprise to the data; they are a story of thresholds. Measles needs about 95 percent of a community to be immune to stay contained, so it behaves like a wildfire in dry brush: a few unprotected pockets are enough to let it run. Cholera shows the same logic with water in place of vaccines. A WHO report on September 29 found cholera deaths rose 30 percent in 2025 even as reported cases fell, with seven countries accounting for about 90 percent of the burden, and Zambia declared a fresh outbreak on September 30. On the food side, health agencies are tracking a hepatitis A cluster tied to frozen clams, with at least 37 cases across four states, and a Salmonella outbreak linked to broccoli sprouts. The same U.S. agencies declared the country's largest-ever cyclospora outbreak over. For a curious non-scientist, the takeaway is that none of this is mysterious. These are mostly old pathogens meeting gaps in vaccination, clean water or food safety, and the numbers published each week are how we find those gaps before they widen.

Mathematics

This Week in Mathematics

Mathematics has a peculiar kind of drama: a problem can sit untouched for half a century, looking for all the world like a locked door, and then somebody finds that the key was lying in a different room entirely. This week offered a fine example. Lisa Sauermann and Huy Tuan Pham announced a resolution of the Graham conjecture, a 55-year-old problem in combinatorics, and the reporting on it stresses how they got there: by harnessing randomness. It sounds paradoxical, using chance to prove something certain, but it is one of the most productive tricks in modern combinatorics.

The idea is a bit like proving that a good seating plan exists for a wedding by showing that a randomly shuffled plan works often enough to guarantee at least one winner. You never have to build the perfect arrangement by hand; you only have to show that the odds of it existing are not zero. It is also part of a busy run for graph theory, the study of dots joined by lines that underpins everything from social networks to circuit design. Earlier this month the Four-Color Theorem, the famous claim that any map can be shaded with just four colors so that neighbors never match, was reported to have received a rare new proof, and mathematicians also described a long-awaited “graph sandwich” result on a decades-old conjecture about understanding complex networks.

Hovering over all of it is the question of what machines are doing to the craft. This week commentary has focused on the latest AI-generated proofs and what they mean for working mathematicians, and a human audit of AI-written proofs has appeared on the arXiv preprint server. I could not read the underlying papers myself this morning, so treat the details with caution. The so-what for the rest of us is that mathematics is in an unusually fertile and argumentative moment: old problems are falling, the methods are changing, and the old human habit of checking each other’s work is more valuable than ever.

Robotics & Engineering

This Week in Robotics & Engineering

For decades the hard part of robotics was never the flashy bits. Lifting a heavy box is a matter of strong motors; folding a towel in a stranger's living room is a matter of judgment, touch and a tolerance for mess. The field's quiet obsession right now is exactly that gap between the lab and the real world, and this month's crop of results reads like a progress report on closing it.

The clearest example comes from Figure, whose Helix 2.5 system was tested on tidying toys, folding towels and making beds in 30 different homes it had never seen. The striking finding was how much it helped to teach the robot elsewhere first: pretraining on a large body of prior experience lifted complete-task success from 9 percent to 56 percent, with no extra fine-tuning for each new house. Think of a new hire who has worked in a hundred kitchens and can walk into a hundred-and-first without a training week. A separate research effort, Facet-0, tackles the other half of the problem, physical contact. By combining camera images, spoken instructions and the force and twist felt at the robot's wrist, it predicts not just where an arm will move but what will happen when it touches something, and it completed precision computer-assembly tasks 82 percent of the time versus 15 percent for the strongest baseline the authors tried. Seating a delicate connector is more like threading a needle by feel than by sight, and this is a robot learning to feel.

Then there is the less glamorous but arguably more important question of what happens when things go wrong. A new paper on humanoid “Safe-Stop” teaches a robot to judge, in the instant an emergency stop is pressed, whether it can still come to rest standing up; if so it uses a learned stopping routine, and if not it switches to a gentler, damped fall. It is the robotic equivalent of knowing whether to brake or to tuck and roll. Meanwhile Agility Robotics is pushing its Digit 5 toward working safely beside people, reportedly lifting around 22.7 kilograms (50 pounds).

The so-what for a curious non-scientist is that the story of robots has shifted from “can it do the trick” to “can it do the trick in your house, without breaking anything, including itself.” Generalization, touch and safe failure are the unglamorous ingredients that decide whether a humanoid stays a demo or becomes a coworker. Several of these results are early research or company-reported figures rather than independent trials, so treat the numbers as promising signals, not verdicts.

Oceanography

This Week in Oceanography

The ocean is the one place on Earth where the map is still being drawn. We have better images of Mars than of much of our own seafloor, and every time a research ship lowers a robot into the dark, the list of things we did not know gets longer. This week was more a chorus of small surprises than one giant headline, but the surprises share a theme: the sea is far more crowded, and far more interconnected, than it looks from the surface.

Start with the penguins. Researchers reported that gentoo penguins, the orange-billed birds that waddle across Antarctic postcards, are not one species but four, with a previously unrecognized one living on the remote Kerguelen Islands in the southern Indian Ocean. Think of it like discovering that what you thought was a single family recipe is really four cousins’ versions that have quietly drifted apart over generations. Meanwhile, marine taxonomists gathered in Punta Arenas, Chile, to work through specimens hauled up on Schmidt Ocean Institute expeditions to the Chile Margin and the South Sandwich Islands, a reminder that naming what lives down there is slow, patient detective work. NOAA’s Okeanos Explorer also wrapped up its American Samoa mapping and robot-dive expedition in Fiji, adding more seafloor to the atlas.

Then there is the invisible crowd. Recent work on Prochlorococcus, the tiny photosynthetic microbe that is probably the most abundant living thing in the sea, points to viral DNA tucked inside its own genome, a possible clue to how it stays so successful, like an immune system built partly from borrowed parts. Related studies on “ocean snow,” the drifting flakes of dead matter that carry carbon to the deep, suggest that hitchhiking bacteria can dissolve the mineral ballast that makes those flakes sink, which could weaken one of the ocean’s major ways of storing carbon. And on Friday a report from Systemiq and partners took stock of progress toward protecting 30 percent of the ocean by 2030, describing the gaps still in the way.

So what should a curious non-scientist take from it? That the ocean is not a blank blue backdrop but a living machine whose smallest parts, microbes, viruses and sinking specks of debris, help regulate the climate we live in, and whose largest, familiar animals still hold secrets. A note on sourcing: several of these items came from news summaries rather than the full papers, and a couple of them are a little older than a week, so treat the details as a first look and follow the original studies for the fine print.

Batteries & Electricity

This Week in Batteries & Electricity

Battery science has always been a game of chemical accounting: how many electrons can you coax out of a given lump of material, and how many times can you do it before the material gives up and falls apart. Most headline "breakthroughs" nudge that ledger by a few percentage points. This week's news is more interesting because it plays with the ledger in three completely different ways at once — one team found electrons that were supposedly already spoken for, one country decided to bet billions on chemistries other than the lithium-ion recipe everyone already trusts, and one carmaker proved you can build a genuinely new battery without digging a single new ounce of metal out of the ground.

Start with the electrons. In a standard lithium-sulfur battery, each sulfur atom trades two electrons during charging and discharging — that's the deal chemists have worked with for decades, and it's already appealing because sulfur is cheap and can theoretically hold far more energy than the graphite used in ordinary lithium-ion cells. Researchers at the University of Maryland, working with Vanderbilt University and Brookhaven National Lab, found a third electron hiding in the transaction, one that conventional battery chemistry simply couldn't unlock. By adding chlorine to the mix, described in a new paper in Nature Energy and reported by Tech Xplore and Vanderbilt's engineering school, they got sulfur to give up that extra electron, boosting the amount of charge each kilogram of sulfur could store by about 58 percent and pushing the battery's operating voltage up from roughly 2.05 to 2.54 volts. Picture a savings account you thought only paid two kinds of interest suddenly paying a third — that's roughly the scale of the free lunch here, though as Interesting Engineering notes, the prototype cell still needs to prove it can survive many more than the 100 charge cycles tested so far. Meanwhile, South Korea decided not to wait around for lab chemistry to mature on its own: its trade ministry unveiled a roughly $6 billion battery roadmap, split between government R&D and private investment, aimed squarely at sodium-ion and solid-state batteries rather than doubling down on the nickel-heavy lithium-ion cells that have dominated for years, as the Korea Times and Seoul Economic Daily reported — essentially a national hedge against China's dominance in cheap lithium cells, using table-salt chemistry as the escape hatch. And in Tennessee and Michigan, General Motors and its recycling partner Cirba Solutions closed a loop that battery makers have been chasing for years: they pulled 80 dead EV battery packs apart, turned the leftover "black mass" into more than 12 metric tons of new cathode material made from 100 percent recycled nickel, cobalt and manganese, and used it to build fresh battery cells now rolling out the door in Cadillac, Chevrolet and GMC EVs, per Electrive and Interesting Engineering. It's the battery equivalent of an aluminum can becoming a new aluminum can, no bauxite mine required. Even the unglamorous industrial side of the story moved this week, with battery giant CATL starting trial cell production at its new Debrecen, Hungary plant — a reminder that all this clever chemistry eventually has to be manufactured by the gigawatt-hour, on a continent that currently makes very few of its own cells.

None of these stories will change what's in your phone or car by next month, but together they sketch where the pressure points actually are: squeezing more energy out of cheap materials like sulfur, reducing dependence on any single country's supply chain for the expensive stuff, and not having to mine fresh metal every time an old battery dies. If lithium-sulfur cells with a third electron eventually reach production, it could mean lighter, cheaper batteries for EVs and grid storage alike. If Korea's bet on sodium and solid-state pays off, it means more real competition and, eventually, lower prices at the register. And GM's closed loop is the most immediately reassuring bit: it shows that "recycled battery" doesn't have to mean a downgrade — it can mean an EV battery built from your neighbor's old EV battery, with nothing lost in translation.
