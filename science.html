Batteries & Electricity

This Week in Batteries & Electricity

A battery is really just a very patient chemical reaction — you push electrons uphill into it, and later it lets them roll back down through whatever you plug in. For most of the last decade the interesting question was how to make that reaction denser, so a car could go farther. This year the interesting question has quietly changed. The grid, not the car, is now where the pressure is, and the past week's news reads less like a materials-science story and more like an infrastructure one.

The scale is worth pausing on. Roughly 108 gigawatts of new battery storage went onto the world's grids in 2025, about forty percent more than the year before, and 2026 is on track to add something like 350 gigawatt-hours more — which makes stationary storage the fastest-growing power technology on the planet right now. Almost all of it is lithium iron phosphate, the unglamorous cousin of the chemistry in your phone: less energy per kilogram, but cheaper, more tolerant of being charged and drained every single day, and far less inclined to catch fire. That trade-off, energy density surrendered in exchange for cycle life and safety, is exactly the right one when the battery is going to sit in a field rather than move down a highway. The week's project announcements made the point concretely — Octopus Australia filed a 1.2-gigawatt, 4.8-gigawatt-hour system for environmental assessment, construction began on the first of eight neighborhood-scale batteries across Greater Western Sydney, and the U.S. Department of Energy cleared a storage demonstration project in Vermont to move forward. Not every proposal survives: a compressed-air storage scheme in Huron County, Ontario, which would have stashed energy by squeezing air underground rather than shuffling ions, was rejected outright.

Behind the deployment numbers, the chemistry keeps shifting toward sodium. Sodium sits directly below lithium on the periodic table, behaves similarly, and is essentially free — it is the stuff of table salt and seawater — but its ions are fatter, which makes them awkward to shove into an electrode. The past year has been a run of clever fixes for that awkwardness, from all-solid-state sodium cells that held ninety-five percent of their capacity after five hundred cycles to anode-free sodium-sulfur designs built around a non-flammable electrolyte. Argonne National Laboratory's LENS consortium is pushing the same idea from the supply-chain end, on the theory that a battery you can make entirely from domestic salt and iron is strategically different from one that depends on cobalt and lithium imports. Meanwhile the solid-state world spent August doing the boring work that actually precedes commercialization: a ten-tonne solid electrolyte plant clearing environmental review in Xiantao, hybrid solid-liquid cells being lined up for wind-and-solar-paired storage projects in China.

What ties all of this together is a number from the other side of the meter: U.S. data center electricity demand has climbed from about 23 gigawatts in 2023 to roughly 42 today, with AI server racks drawing 50 to 100 kilowatts apiece where a conventional rack drew five to ten. Grid interconnection queues are now measured in years, which is why operators are increasingly building their own generation on site and why storage that can smooth a spiky load has suddenly become a commercial necessity rather than a climate nicety. The thing to watch over the next year is whether sodium can take a real bite out of the stationary market. If it can, the grid stops competing with electric cars for the same scarce minerals — and that would be a bigger deal than any single cell-level record.

Science Daily — Thursday, August 27, 2026
Biology

This Week in Biology

Biology is the science of things that refuse to stay tidy. Genomes get shuffled, species turn up where nobody was looking, and organisms keep inventing solutions that no engineer would have signed off on. This week delivered all three, starting with a study that quietly rewrote one of the field's default assumptions: that the way DNA gets rearranged over deep time is essentially random.

Researchers at the University of Vienna, writing in Science Advances, compared more than 5,800 chromosome-scale genomes covering 4,454 species across 19 animal phyla — the biggest such comparison ever attempted across the animal tree of life. What they found looks less like a random walk and more like a road network. Chromosomes merge and their genes get stirred together in a process the team calls "fusion-with-mixing," and once that stirring happens it essentially cannot be undone. Think of pouring two buckets of differently coloured sand together: separating them again isn't merely difficult, it's practically impossible. Because these events are one-way, they leave a permanent record, and mapping thousands of them reveals a limited set of routes — "evolutionary highways" — that lineage after lineage has travelled, some racing far down the road, others pulling off early. Mosquitoes, glass sponges and earthworms stand out as genuinely unusual travellers, which makes them interesting targets for anyone thinking about conservation priorities or where the next surprise in genome biology might come from.

Down at the level of individual organisms, the week's finds were more charming and no less instructive. A new species of giant salamander in the genus Andrias was described from Anhui, China, bringing the world's tally of these metre-long amphibians to six — a reminder that we are still discovering large vertebrates in well-populated countries in 2026. Palaeobiologists reported exceptionally rare soft tissue preserved in a 450-million-year-old crinoid, a sea-lily relative; soft tissue at that age is more than 200 million years older than anything comparable previously found, and it means we can look at the plumbing of an animal that lived before the first forests. In the lab, honeybee research turned up something both clever and slightly grim: queens exposed to pesticides can shunt some of that contamination into their eggs, a previously unknown way of protecting themselves that raises obvious questions about what it does to the next generation. And out at sea, researchers identified a marine fungus, Algophthora mediterranea, that infects and kills the toxic algae behind harmful blooms — a potential natural brake on a problem that has been getting worse along warming coastlines.

What ties this together is the recurring lesson that biology is more constrained, and more inventive, than it looks from the outside. Genomes turn out to follow a small number of one-way paths rather than wandering freely; a bee queen turns out to have an escape route nobody had documented; a fungus turns out to be already doing a job we've been trying to engineer. The Vienna genome map is the piece worth watching, because it gives researchers something the field has lacked — a way to say which lineages are genuinely evolutionarily distinctive, and even to simulate where genome architecture might go next. Expect it to start showing up in conservation arguments before long.

Science Daily — Wednesday, August 26, 2026
Chemistry

This Week in Chemistry

Chemistry is the science of persuasion. Every molecule sits in a comfortable energy well, and the chemist's job is to talk it into moving somewhere else — ideally cheaply, cleanly, and without wrecking everything nearby in the process. Most of the field's real progress therefore looks less like a thunderclap discovery and more like a locksmith quietly finding a better key. This week produced several of them, and one that arguably picks the lock by removing the door.

The headline act came out of the University of Wisconsin–Madison, working with colleagues at Colorado State and CU Boulder, who found a way around one of the oldest constraints in reaction design. When you push an electron into a mixture of molecules, it goes wherever it is easiest to go — thermodynamics picks the winner, and the chemist just watches. That rule has quietly walled off huge swathes of useful chemistry, because the molecule you actually want to activate is often not the greediest one in the flask. The new catalyst sidesteps the whole negotiation: instead of handing the electron to a partner molecule, it ejects it into the solvent as a free, unattached electron. A bare electron floating in solution is about as unhappy as chemistry gets, and it will fling itself into essentially anything — which, counterintuitively, restores the chemist's control, because selectivity can now be engineered elsewhere in the system rather than surrendered to thermodynamics. Lead researcher Zachary Wickens framed it as less a new method than a new way to design redox reactions altogether, and the immediate prize is a class of coupling reactions that had been considered simply out of reach.

The rest of the week's work was more grounded but no less consequential, and a theme ran through it: making the awkward leftovers of industrial civilization behave. Chemists reported a highly efficient catalyst that cracks lignin — the tough, tangled polymer that gives wood its rigidity and that pulp mills have historically burned for lack of anything better to do with it — into usable chemical feedstocks under comparatively mild conditions. Lignin is the second most abundant organic polymer on Earth and one of the most stubborn, so any route that opens it up without brutal heat and pressure is a serious step toward getting aromatic chemicals from plants rather than petroleum. In the same spirit, a team at the National University of Singapore demonstrated a way to convert scrap carbon-fibre composite from aircraft — a material designed specifically to be indestructible and therefore a nightmare to recycle — into aerogels, those ghostly ultralight solids that are mostly air. At Birmingham, meanwhile, researchers unveiled a material that slumps from gel to something liquid-like under ultraviolet light, then can be rebuilt with heat or deliberately taken apart with acid: a substance with a built-in undo button, which is precisely what recycling has always lacked. And in the durability column, a Hong Kong University group's "SS-H2" stainless steel continues to draw attention for surviving the savage oxidizing conditions inside seawater electrolyzers, a job currently done by titanium at roughly forty times the material cost.

What ties these together is a shift in what chemists consider a good result. For most of the twentieth century, the win was making a molecule at all. Increasingly the win is making it from something we already threw away, in water instead of a nasty solvent, with a catalyst built from iron or boron instead of iridium or palladium. None of this produces a single dramatic moment — nobody photographs an aerogel made from a retired wing spar and calls it a moon landing — but this is the machinery by which decarbonization actually happens, one reaction at a time. Worth watching next: whether the free-electron catalysis approach survives contact with real pharmaceutical synthesis, where the test is not whether a reaction is elegant but whether it works on a Tuesday, at scale, in the hands of someone who did not invent it.

Science Daily — Tuesday, August 25, 2026
Physics

This Week in Physics

Physics has a habit of finding its biggest surprises in the places that are supposed to be boring — empty space, cold atoms, the inside of a crystal nobody outside the field has heard of. This past week delivered a run of exactly that kind of news, and the through-line is a good one: nothing is ever really empty, and nothing is ever really still.

Start with the vacuum. Nearly ninety years ago Werner Heisenberg and his student Hans Euler argued that a perfect vacuum isn't nothing at all, but a seething broth of "virtual" particles flickering in and out of existence too fast to catch. Their prediction was that if you squeezed that broth with a sufficiently violent magnetic field, it would stop behaving like empty space and start behaving like glass — bending and polarizing light passing through it. The catch is that "sufficiently violent" means field strengths no laboratory on Earth can come close to. So an international team went shopping in the sky instead, pointing NASA's IXPE X-ray polarimeter, the NICER telescope on the space station, and CSIRO's Parkes radio dish at a magnetar called 1E 1547.0–5408 — a dead star with a magnetic field trillions of times stronger than a fridge magnet. The X-rays coming off it were polarized to an extraordinary degree, and, crucially, the polarization was locked to the star's magnetic field in exactly the way the radio waves were. That is the fingerprint of vacuum birefringence, and it is the strongest evidence yet that Heisenberg was right about the emptiness of empty space.

Closer to home, and much colder, physicists at Monash University published a prediction in Physical Review Letters that overturns a long-standing assumption about ultracold matter. Quantum particles come in two temperaments: bosons, which are gregarious and happy to pile into the same state, and fermions, which are territorial and refuse to share, thanks to the Pauli exclusion principle. Mix the two strongly and the received wisdom was that you get a mess — either a collapse or a diffuse cloud, never anything that holds its own shape. The Monash calculations say otherwise: under the right resonant conditions, the attraction pulling the mixture inward is balanced almost exactly by the outward pressure of the crowded, mutually repelling fermions, and the result is a self-bound "quantum droplet," a liquid a hundred million times thinner than air that nonetheless holds together with no container at all. Meanwhile MIT physicists, writing in Nature Physics, zapped a rare-earth crystal called erbium tritelluride with laser pulses to scramble its electrons and then watched them reassemble. The material hosts two competing "charge density waves" — standing ripples in the electron sea — and the surprise was that they rebuild in completely different ways. The dominant wave returns smoothly and everywhere at once; the weaker one nucleates in isolated pockets that spread outward, exactly like ice crystals creeping across a windowpane.

Nuclear physics had its own long-cold case closed. A team led by researchers at Michigan State's Facility for Rare Isotope Beams, with collaborators at 25 institutions across six countries, reported in Nature that they had finally pinned down why certain nuclei — zinc-70 in this instance — spit out far more low-energy gamma rays than models predict. The answer turns out to be magnetic: protons and neutrons inside the nucleus flipping their tiny internal compass needles. That sounds like housekeeping, but it isn't. The rate at which nuclei absorb and emit gamma rays is a key input to models of how heavy elements get forged in supernovae and neutron-star collisions, so a correction here ripples all the way out to the origin story of the gold in your jewelry. Taken together, it was a week that rewarded looking closely at the unglamorous stuff — the vacuum, a cold cloud of atoms, a crystal, a single isotope — and finding structure where the textbooks promised none.

Science Daily — Monday, August 24, 2026
Space

This Week in Space

Astronomy is the rare science where you can't run an experiment — you can only build a better pair of eyes and wait for the universe to show you something. This week the field spent most of its energy doing exactly that, as NASA finished clearing its next great observatory for flight while, out in the data already collected, astronomers found black holes behaving in ways that keep pushing the edges of what anyone expected.

The headline is the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope, which passed its Flight Readiness Review on Friday at Kennedy Space Center and is now certified to proceed toward an August 30 launch atop a SpaceX Falcon Heavy. Roman is best understood as Hubble's wide-angle cousin: similar sharpness, but a field of view roughly a hundred times larger than Hubble's infrared camera, which lets it survey the sky something like a thousand times faster. That difference is not cosmetic. Hubble has spent thirty-five years taking exquisite portraits of small patches of sky; Roman is built to photograph whole neighborhoods at once, which is the only practical way to answer statistical questions — how dark energy has stretched the universe over billions of years, how many free-floating planets drift between the stars, how planetary systems assemble. Mission scientists expect it to turn up more than 100,000 exoplanets, which would roughly multiply the current catalog by a factor of fifteen.

Meanwhile the black hole news kept arriving. Astronomers reported the first confirmed pair of feeding supermassive black holes inside a "Green Pea" galaxy — those are small, absurdly bright, furiously star-forming systems that look like nearby stand-ins for the galaxies of the early universe, and finding two growing black holes packed into one is a useful clue about how the giants at galactic centers get built. A separate team announced three supermassive black holes in a single galaxy, a first. Another caught a black hole tearing apart a massive star in one of the most energetic stellar disruptions ever recorded. Closer to home, a newly identified star orbiting the Milky Way's central black hole turns out to be the fastest and closest yet found, which makes it a natural laboratory for testing whether a spinning black hole drags spacetime around with it the way Einstein's equations say it should. And in an elegant bit of detective work, the jet of a distant blazar was found to be gravitationally bent by something invisible along the line of sight — a lump of dark matter revealing itself purely through its gravity.

There were quieter reminders that the solar system still has surprises too: evidence of moons forming around a distant exoplanet, the first time anyone has watched that process; new modeling suggesting the solar wind peels away Mars' atmosphere by whipping up enormous rolling waves along its outer edge; and a caution that when astronomers measure how fast an exoplanet spins, they may often be clocking its winds instead of its rock — Venus, after all, has clouds that circle the planet about sixty times faster than the planet itself turns. If you want something to look at rather than read about, a deep partial lunar eclipse arrives the night of August 27–28 and will be the best one visible anywhere on Earth until the last day of 2028. Then, two days later, Roman goes up — and the flood of data that will occupy astronomers for the next decade begins.
