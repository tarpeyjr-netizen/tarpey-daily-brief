Batteries & Electricity

This Week in Batteries & Electricity

The battery world right now feels less like a single breakthrough story and more like a land rush, with money and hardware pouring into projects on nearly every continent while the underlying chemistry keeps quietly diversifying beyond the lithium-ion cells that power most of what we plug in today.

The clearest sign of that diversification came from Lincoln Electric System in Nebraska, which switched on a 3-megawatt, 12-megawatt-hour zinc-based battery to help back up the state Capitol complex and other critical infrastructure. Zinc batteries won't power your next phone, but they're cheap, don't catch fire the way some lithium chemistries can, and are increasingly attractive for exactly this kind of steady, unglamorous grid-support duty. Meanwhile LG Energy Solution used its second-quarter earnings call to confirm it's building a pilot line for all-solid-state batteries using a dry-electrode process in the second half of this year, a manufacturing detail that matters more than it sounds. Dry-electrode coating (the technique Tesla popularized for cutting battery costs) is one of the main things standing between solid-state batteries and affordable mass production, so a major cell maker committing pilot capacity to it is a real signal the technology is inching off the lab bench. On the deployment side, Malawi switched on its first large-scale standalone battery storage system, a small project in absolute terms but a meaningful one for a country where grid reliability has long been a bottleneck to growth, and in Ontario, Aecon signed on to build, own and operate a 150-megawatt, 1,200-megawatt-hour storage project in Norfolk County, one of the larger battery farms slated for Canada. Add in Zenobē's acquisition of a German storage developer to gain a foothold in Europe's biggest energy market, and you get a picture of an industry building out in every direction at once, established players buying market share while smaller, poorer grids get their first taste of dispatchable storage.

None of these individually rewrites the textbook, but together they explain why battery storage has become the fastest-growing form of new power capacity on the planet. The real story isn't one miracle chemistry, it's that batteries have gotten cheap and reliable enough to become boring, load-bearing infrastructure, the kind that quietly keeps a Capitol building lit or lets a small African nation stop treating blackouts as a given. Worth watching next: whether LG's dry-electrode solid-state pilot actually ships product on a real timeline, since so many solid-state promises have slipped before.

The Family That Cannot Sleep

Sleep Science

Somewhere in the Veneto countryside outside Venice, sometime around the start of the 19th century, a man went to sleep and simply never really managed it again. His eyes wouldn't close properly at night. His pupils shrank to pinpricks. He sweated through his shirts, his heart raced as if he were running, and over about a year and a half he slid through stages of exhaustion, hallucination, and dementia until he died — awake, essentially, the entire time. He is sometimes called patient zero of fatal familial insomnia, one of the strangest and cruelest diseases in medicine, and the reason his story survives at all is that it kept happening. Generation after generation, his descendants started drawing lots in middle age to guess who among them had inherited the family curse.

The disease has a name now, and a mechanism, largely thanks to one member of that family: a Venetian doctor named Ignazio Roiter, who in the 1980s watched his own wife's aunt die of the same baffling insomnia that had already killed her father and two sisters, and who spent years afterward tracking the family tree back through parish records to that first sleepless ancestor. The condition, now called fatal familial insomnia, or FFI, is what's known as a prion disease — the same grim family that includes mad cow disease, its human cousin Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease, and kuru, the disease once spread by ritual cannibalism in Papua New Guinea. Prions are a genuinely bizarre category of pathogen: not a virus, not a bacterium, just a normal protein that has folded itself into the wrong shape, and which then coaxes every other copy of that protein it touches to misfold the same way, like a rumor that turns everyone who hears it a little unhinged. In FFI, a single-letter typo in the gene for a protein called PrP causes exactly this kind of slow-motion, contagious misfolding, and the clumps of ruined protein accumulate with devastating precision in the thalamus, the walnut-sized structure deep in the brain that acts as the switchboard between wakefulness and sleep. Destroy that switchboard slowly enough, and you get a person who is neurologically incapable of sleeping through even the exhaustion of days without rest. Doctors have staged the disease in four acts: months of worsening insomnia and panic attacks, then hallucinations and sweating so severe patients act out their dreams while still nominally awake, then a phase of near-total sleeplessness and rapid weight loss, and finally a slide into unresponsiveness and death, typically within eighteen months to three years of the first symptoms.

What makes FFI matter beyond its own family tree is exactly the thing that makes it so awful to live with: it is one of the only conditions we know of that kills specifically by taking sleep away, which makes it an accidental, tragic experiment in why we sleep at all. Researchers who study the disease have used it to map, with unusual precision, which brain circuits generate the pressure to sleep and what happens to a body denied that pressure's release — the racing heart, the hormone chaos, the eventual breakdown of basically every system that depends on nightly maintenance. It's a reminder that sleep isn't a passive void we fall into when the day's activity runs out, but an actively defended, biologically essential state that the brain will fight to protect until the circuitry responsible for it is physically gone.

There is no cure, and that's the part of the story still being written. Because the mutation is dominant and the gene is known, children of affected parents can find out with a blood test, decades before symptoms would appear, whether they carry it — a piece of knowledge so heavy that many choose not to ask. Doctors have tried, with mixed and mostly disappointing results, treatments ranging from the antibiotic doxycycline to experimental gene-silencing drugs called antisense oligonucleotides, which work by intercepting the genetic instructions for PrP before the protein is even made, essentially trying to mute the rumor before it starts spreading. One such drug, ION717, has been tested in early human trials on patients with related prion diseases, and researchers are increasingly focused on treating people before symptoms begin rather than after, since by the time the insomnia sets in, the thalamus may already be too damaged to save. It's slow, uncertain work on a disease that affects only a few dozen known families worldwide, but it sits at a strange crossroads of medicine and philosophy: to study fatal familial insomnia is to study, in the most literal way imaginable, what sleep is actually for. The Venetian family whose bad luck opened this whole field goes on having children, half of whom will never carry the mutation and never think about it again, and half of whom carry a question inside them that most of us are lucky enough never to have to ask: what would happen to you, exactly, if you could never again close your eyes.

Agriculture

This Week in Agriculture

Feeding a hotter, more crowded planet keeps coming down to the same three demands pulling against each other: grow more food, make it more nutritious, and do it with a climate that's actively working against you. This week's news showed researchers attacking that problem from three very different angles — genomes, gene editing, and satellites.

Start with the genome work. An international team led by scientists in China published the first graph-based pan-genome for the mung bean, a crop that quietly feeds hundreds of millions of people across Asia but has never gotten the genetic spotlight that rice, wheat, or corn get. Rather than mapping the plant's DNA against one reference genome, the team built a composite map from 580 mung bean varieties around the world, turning up more than 66,000 structural variants — DNA segments that got inserted, deleted, or rearranged as different mung bean populations evolved. Two of those variants stood out immediately: one tweaks a gene that controls flavonoid levels (compounds tied to nutrition and flavor), and another confers resistance to bruchid beetles, a major stored-grain pest. It's the kind of unglamorous cataloguing work that doesn't make headlines on its own, but hands breeders a parts list they can now use to design hardier, more nutritious mung beans deliberately instead of by trial and error.

Meanwhile, a separate team reported that carefully trimming a handful of genes in rice — using only genetic material that already exists naturally in plants, no foreign DNA involved — boosted yields by up to 25% while also improving drought tolerance. That double win, more grain and less water, is the combination plant breeders chase constantly and rarely get simultaneously. And zooming out from individual crops, a consortium of researchers spanning IRRI, Ghent University, and the Max Planck Institute published papers in Nature and the Journal of Integrative Plant Biology arguing that yield, nutrition, and climate resilience need to stop being treated as separate research tracks. Their case: with over 700 million people undernourished and 2 billion suffering from "hidden hunger" — deficiencies in vitamins and minerals rather than outright calorie shortage — breeding programs that optimize for yield alone are solving only part of the problem.

The satellite story rounds things out nicely. Cambridge researchers unveiled Tessera, an AI system trained on years of satellite imagery that can be adapted to track crops, forests, and land use anywhere on Earth at 10-meter resolution. What makes it notable for agriculture specifically is that most existing satellite crop-monitoring tools were built for the vast, uniform fields of industrial farming in places like Iowa or Ukraine — they're nearly blind to the small, irregularly shaped plots that smallholder farmers rely on across much of the developing world, even though those farmers grow a huge share of the world's food. In tests on tiny fields in Austria, Tessera identified crop types more accurately than current methods, and Cambridge is releasing it under an open license so any government or research group can deploy it. So what to watch: none of these are the kind of single dramatic breakthrough that changes farming overnight, but together they point at agriculture becoming a genuinely data-rich science — one where breeders know exactly which genes to tweak and where every farm on the planet, however small, can be seen and helped.

Science Daily — Friday, July 31, 2026
Medicine

This Week in Medicine

A lot of medicine isn't about inventing some brand-new weapon against disease — it's about figuring out why the body sometimes wins the fight on its own, and then trying to nudge more people into that lucky category. That was the theme running through this week's research, which touched brain immune cells, cancer's disguise tactics, and the quiet biology of insulin production.

The most striking finding came out of a joint effort by VIB, KU Leuven, and the UK Dementia Research Institute, published in Nature Medicine. Researchers compared donated brain tissue from older adults with and without cognitive decline — including some cognitively sharp centenarians — and found that the amyloid plaques and tau tangles associated with Alzheimer's aren't the whole story. What seems to matter just as much is how microglia, the brain's resident immune cells, respond to that damage. Some people's microglia settle into a protective mode that seems to hold dementia at bay for years; in others, the same cells tip into a harmful state that accelerates decline. That reframes Alzheimer's treatment as less about scrubbing out plaques and more about coaxing the immune system into the helpful posture, which is a genuinely different angle than most of the amyloid-focused drugs on the market today.

A similar "help the immune system see the problem" logic showed up in cancer research this week. Prostate tumors are notoriously "cold" to immunotherapy — they barely attract the T cells that these treatments rely on, largely because the cancer cells hide the molecular flags that would normally mark them for destruction. Scientists at the University of Rochester used a CRISPR-based tool to block a single protein, SPSB1, that prostate cancer cells use to strip those flags away. Restoring the flags in mice dramatically boosted how well immunotherapy worked, and the same trick might apply to other stubbornly cold tumors like pancreatic cancer. Meanwhile, on the metabolic side, researchers identified helper proteins that insulin-producing cells depend on to manufacture insulin correctly; when one of those helpers goes missing, damaged proteins pile up inside the cell and insulin output drops — a mechanistic clue that could matter for early-stage type 2 diabetes. A companion study added a sobering caveat, though: for at least one high-risk group, shedding weight alone wasn't enough to head off diabetes, a reminder that "eat less, weigh less" isn't a universal fix.

So what's the takeaway for anyone who isn't a researcher? Increasingly, the frontier in medicine isn't just killing bad cells or clearing bad proteins — it's understanding the difference between bodies that cope well and bodies that don't, then figuring out how to move people from the second group into the first. That's slower and less flashy than a miracle pill, but it's also why treatments for Alzheimer's, cancer, and diabetes are all quietly getting more personalized rather than more one-size-fits-all.

Science Daily — Thursday, July 30, 2026
Biology

This Week in Biology

Ecology's great puzzle isn't why nature is beautiful, it's why it doesn't fall apart. Thousands of species are all scrambling for the same light, water, and nutrients, and yet most ecosystems hold together instead of collapsing into whatever wins hardest and fastest. This week's biology news reads like a set of case studies in that stubborn balancing act, from a lake that scientists have been watching hour by hour for five straight years to a beetle caught in the act of splitting into two species and an elephant that remembered, on its own, how to be wild again.

The lake study, published in Nature Ecology & Evolution, is the kind of patient science that rarely makes headlines but ends up mattering a lot. Researchers ran an underwater camera system that photographed everything from ten-micrometer algae up to centimeter-long zooplankton, every hour, for five years straight, essentially building a moving census of an entire aquatic food web. What they found is that toxic cyanobacteria blooms (the "algae blooms" that shut down beaches and poison drinking water) aren't just a matter of too much fertilizer washing in from nearby farms. They're shaped constantly by grazing pressure from tiny animals eating the algae, by competition between algae species jostling for the same nutrients, and by species quietly helping each other out in ways that shift the water's chemistry. It's a reminder that predicting a bloom means watching the whole cast of characters, not just counting nutrients. Meanwhile, a separate paper caught evolution in the act: a Southeast Asian herbivorous ladybird beetle, Henosepilachna diekei, is in the middle of splitting into two species because populations that feed on different host plants have stopped interbreeding with each other. It's a rare, almost real-time glimpse of the process Darwin could only infer from fossils and finch beaks.

There was good news for the ground-level stuff too. Botanists described a new tree species, Mitrephora, tucked into a remote patch of the Himalayan foothills in Arunachal Pradesh, India, one of the few known plants in its genus that carries both male and hybrid flowers on the same individual, a kind of reproductive hedge-betting that helps it get pollinated even when conditions aren't ideal. And a study in PLOS One found that elephants released back into the wild after years in captivity adapt surprisingly well, encouraging news for conservationists who worry that captivity might permanently dull an animal's wild instincts. The less cheerful thread running underneath all of this: bumblebees, already short-lived by nature, are being squeezed harder by extreme heat, pesticide exposure, and the loss of the flowers they depend on, a reminder that the same web of cooperation and competition that lets ecosystems self-correct only works as long as enough players are still in the game.

So the takeaway this week isn't just "nature is resilient," though the beetle, the lake, and the elephant all make that case nicely. It's that resilience has limits, and watching for where those limits get tested, in pollinators, in fragile mountain habitats, in water systems under climate stress, is exactly the kind of unglamorous, long-haul science that tells us which way the balance is tipping before it's too late to do much about it.

Science Daily — Wednesday, July 29, 2026
Chemistry

This Week in Chemistry

At its heart, chemistry is a game of taking molecules apart and putting them back together in smarter configurations, and this week offered a tidy example of both directions at once: one team figuring out how to demolish a class of pollutants that refuse to die, another figuring out how to build complex drug candidates with far less busywork.

Start with the demolition. PFAS, the so-called forever chemicals used in everything from nonstick pans to firefighting foam, are notorious because their carbon-fluorine bonds are among the strongest in organic chemistry — which is great for stain resistance and terrible for anyone trying to get them out of drinking water. Researchers at the Helmholtz-Zentrum Dresden-Rossendorf in Germany reported two distinct ways to crack them open. The first, hydrodynamic cavitation, essentially blasts contaminated water through tight channels to create and collapse millions of tiny vapor bubbles, and the violent implosions rip apart roughly a third of the PFOS molecules present, releasing their bound fluorine in the process. The second uses cold atmospheric plasma paired with rising gas bubbles that drag PFAS to the surface, where the plasma tears the molecules apart almost completely. Neither method is a silver bullet on its own, but the team's stated plan to combine them is the kind of practical, unglamorous chemistry that could actually make a dent in a pollution problem regulators have been wringing their hands over for years.

On the construction side, two independent teams found cleverer ways to build the kind of gnarly, three-dimensional molecules that drug discovery depends on. Chemists at the University at Buffalo, publishing in Science, used ordinary blue LED light and a catalyst to modify two neighboring carbon atoms on a molecule in a single step, something that traditionally takes several separate reactions. The trick works on a chemical building block familiar to any second-year organic chemistry student, and because visible light is much gentler than the ultraviolet light often used in these reactions, it avoids frying the delicate molecules being assembled. Meanwhile, a Münster-based group led by chemist Frank Glorius engineered a light-triggered reaction cascade — one step setting off the next like a row of dominoes — that converts flat, nitrogen-containing aromatic rings into the twisted 3D scaffolds so common in pharmaceutical active ingredients. Fewer synthetic steps means faster, cheaper drug candidate screening, which is the kind of unsexy efficiency gain that quietly speeds up medicine down the line.

And in a reminder that even the most familiar substance on Earth still has secrets, researchers at the University of Osaka used an AI model trained on molecular simulations to dig into a decades-old debate: whether liquid water is actually a shifting mixture of two distinct molecular arrangements, one denser and one looser, rather than one uniform structure. The AI sifted through millions of simulated snapshots and found the clearest evidence yet supporting that two-state picture, which could help explain water's famous list of anomalies, like expanding instead of shrinking when it freezes. It's a nice reminder that the everyday and the exotic aren't opposites in chemistry — sometimes they're the same glass of water.

Science Daily — Tuesday, July 28, 2026
Physics

This Week in Physics

Physics has a habit of finding profound truths in mundane places — a puddle of light, a spinning disk, a block of glass — and this week's crop of research leaned hard into that tradition, threading together stories about controlling light, extracting energy from spinning objects, and building the exotic materials future quantum computers might run on.

The most immediately useful result came from a lab that built a programmable optical chip capable of slowing light down on demand, using structures etched directly into the chip instead of the bulky external equipment engineers have relied on until now. Slowing light sounds like a magic trick, but it's actually a serious engineering problem: fiber-optic communications and optical computers need ways to briefly store or delay light pulses so different signals stay synchronized, and a chip that can dial that delay up or down electronically could make optical circuits far more flexible. Around the same time, a separate team studying quantum dots — the tiny glowing crystals used in some of the best TV screens and increasingly in solar cells — found that adding protons to the mix dramatically speeds up how efficiently energy hops from the dots into nearby molecules, a process called triplet energy transfer. It's the kind of unglamorous mechanistic discovery that won't make headlines but that solar-cell and display engineers will be citing for years.

On the more exotic end of the spectrum, physicists unveiled PLATON, a particle-detector design that swaps out the millions of tiny individual components used in current instruments for a single continuous block of light-producing crystal — a change that could make future particle-physics experiments cheaper and easier to build at scale. And in a genuinely fun bit of analog physics, another group recreated the Penrose process — the theoretical trick by which a spinning black hole can be made to give up some of its energy — using a stationary lab device engineered to mimic ultrafast rotation, essentially building a black hole's energy-extraction party trick on a tabletop. A little further back, a Finnish team finished building the first real-world example of a two-dimensional topological crystalline insulator, a strange quantum material predicted on paper more than a decade ago, whose defining trait is that electricity flows without resistance along its edges while the interior stays inert — exactly the kind of oddity topological quantum computing hopes to exploit.

None of this will change your life by next Tuesday, but it's a good snapshot of where physics quietly does its real work: not in single eureka moments, but in the slow accumulation of better materials, better detectors, and better tricks for bending light and energy to our will. Keep an eye on quantum-dot solar cells and topological qubits especially — both are inching from theory into hardware, and that's usually the stage right before things get interesting.

Science Daily — Monday, July 27, 2026
Space

This Week in Space

Every so often astronomy delivers a discovery that reorders the whole "are we alone" conversation, and this past week delivered one of those: the first confirmed atmosphere ever found on a rocky planet sitting in another star's habitable zone. That's a genuinely new category of find, and it landed alongside a busy stretch of planet-hunting, sun-watching, and telescope-saving news.

The big one came from a Harvard-led team observing LHS 1140 b, a super-Earth 48 light-years away that circles a red dwarf star once every 24.7 days. Using the Magellan Clay telescope in Chile, researchers spotted helium leaking from the planet's upper atmosphere — the tell-tale signature of a real, persistent atmosphere, one that appears to have survived for more than three billion years despite its star's radiation. Scientists have found atmospheres on gas giants and puffy sub-Neptunes before, but never on a solid world of the kind considered the best bet for hosting life. Think of it as the difference between confirming a house has walls versus just confirming it has a roof: LHS 1140 b is looking more and more like an actual, weathered world rather than a bare rock. Meanwhile, in the Beta Pictoris system 63 light-years out, two independent teams using the James Webb Space Telescope confirmed a third giant planet, Beta Pictoris d — a world so faint (about a hundred times dimmer than its sibling planet) that it had been hiding in more than a decade of archived observations before anyone noticed it. It's only the second known system where astronomers have directly imaged three planets at once, which makes Beta Pictoris something like a crowded, camera-shy solar system caught fully in frame for the first time. Add to that a newly identified turning point in the Sun's 11-year activity cycle, which could let forecasters predict solar storms years further in advance than before, and a routine asteroid that turned out to be a "dark comet" — an object that behaves like a comet without showing a visible tail — and it's been a week of things not quite being what they first appeared.

Back closer to home, NASA launched a first-of-its-kind rescue mission to keep its aging Swift space telescope from tumbling into Earth's atmosphere later this year, while the agency also previewed the upcoming Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope, due to launch next month and tasked with mapping billions of galaxies. And JWST marked its fourth anniversary of science operations with striking new images of the Centaurus A galaxy, a reminder that the observatory keeps outperforming even its own advance billing.

So what does it all add up to? The LHS 1140 b result is the kind of finding textbooks get rewritten around: it's the first hard evidence that rocky, potentially livable worlds can actually hold onto air the way Earth does. Nothing here means anyone's found life, but it means the search just got a real target to point bigger telescopes at — including Roman, once it's off the ground.
