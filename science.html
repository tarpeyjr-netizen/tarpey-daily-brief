Chemistry

This Week in Chemistry

Chemistry is the science of arrangement. The same handful of atoms can make a gas that warms the planet or a crystal that traps it, a lump of dull carbon or a sparkling quantum device. What makes the field exciting right now is how much of the progress comes from chemists rearranging things on purpose: steering molecules into the right corner of a pore, or treating a flaw in a gemstone as if it were a molecule in its own right. This week's news is full of that kind of deliberate tinkering, and it sits beside a bigger question about how chemistry turned into life in the first place.

Start with carbon capture, which has always had a problem with humidity. Most sponge-like materials that grab carbon dioxide from smokestack exhaust get clogged by water vapor, so plants have to dry the gas first, and that costs money and energy. A team led by Kyriakos Stylianou at Oregon State University reports in Angewandte Chemie on a metal-organic framework called BVR-X that handles the problem by sorting molecules. Picture a parking garage that sends cars to one level and motorcycles to another: water molecules end up in one part of the pores and carbon dioxide in another, so the water never takes the spots the CO₂ needs. In lab tests it pulled CO₂ out of a very humid stream containing just 4 percent of the gas, which is typical of natural-gas exhaust, and it kept working over dozens of capture-and-release cycles. A similar molecule-by-molecule approach shows up at Princeton Plasma Physics Laboratory, where John Mark Martirez described in ACS Central Science a shortcut for finding diamond defects that might act as room-temperature qubits. Physicists usually try to simulate a huge chunk of crystal. Martirez cuts out a cluster of 16 to 30 atoms around the flaw and treats it as a small molecule, which lets him use the standard toolkit of quantum chemistry. That turned up boron and oxygen defects that should glow ultraviolet and blue, unlike the red of the familiar nitrogen-vacancy center, so several kinds of qubit could in principle sit side by side in one device and be addressed separately by color. Over at Penn Engineering, chemists took a more hands-on approach. Danielle Levin and Igor Bargatin stacked hundreds of sheets of Mylar, the plastic in emergency blankets, one at a time with tweezers. Some were coated with aluminum oxide, and the result was a tough insulating film meant to shield moon habitats from wild temperature swings. The stacks survived repeated squeezing at about 20 megapascals. The team also found that most of the heat leaks through tiny specks added to the film to keep the sheets from sticking together. For now the samples are only about a square centimeter, so a lunar base is still a long way off.

The week's most thought-provoking chemistry wasn't a single result, though. It was Quanta Magazine's new series on how chemistry became biology. One installment, on the metabolism-first idea, explains the theory that life began with self-sustaining reaction cycles rather than genes. The piece revisits experiments in which carbon dioxide and hydrogen, passed over nickel and iron minerals under vent-like conditions, produced formate, acetate and pyruvate, the backbone of an ancient pathway that microbes still use. It also describes a rival finding that the parts of a key metabolic cycle can form in a warm pool without any metal at all. The theory's hardest open problem is the one Scripps chemist Ramanarayanan Krishnamurthy names: combining all these reactions without ending up with "an unanalyzable mess." For a curious non-scientist, that's the thread running through the whole week. Whether you want to clean up a smokestack, build a quantum computer or explain the origin of life, the hard part is rarely making the right molecules. The hard part is keeping them in the right places, apart from the ones that would get in their way.

Physics

This Week in Physics

Some of the most satisfying moments in physics come when a messy, chaotic thing finally does exactly what the equations said it would. Turbulence, plasma, the invisible bookkeeping of matter and antimatter: these are the parts of nature that usually resist tidy description. This past week, a run of careful experiments caught several of them behaving on cue, from a cloud of atoms colder than deep space to the roaring interior of a Japanese fusion machine, along with a reminder from CERN that the universe is still keeping one of its biggest secrets.

At the University of Cambridge, a team led by Sebastian Morris reported in Physical Review Letters that it had watched tangled quantum whirlpools unravel inside a Bose-Einstein condensate, a gas of potassium atoms chilled until they move in lockstep like a single super-atom. Stir such a gas and it fills with tiny vortices, a bit like the knotted eddies in a bathtub drain, except each one carries a fixed, quantized amount of spin. The researchers snapped pictures as the knot loosened and found the vortices fading at precisely the rate predicted by a decades-old theory known as Vinen turbulence. Even better, once they adjusted for the mass of the atoms, the decay matched what is seen in superfluid helium, a completely different substance. It is the first direct look at this kind of three-dimensional vortex tangle dying away in a gas. Over in Japan, Katsumi Ida’s group at the Large Helical Device did something deliberately counterintuitive: instead of taming an unstable plasma, they let it misbehave and found, in Communications Physics, that the waves it threw off actually heated the plasma up. That is one small piece of the “self-heating” a future fusion power plant will need, and the same process plays out in Earth’s magnetosphere. Meanwhile at CERN, the LHCb collaboration published the first measurement of a particular matter-antimatter difference in the decays of particles called Bs mesons. The Standard Model predicts almost no asymmetry here, which makes it a good place for new physics to show itself. It did not: the result fits the textbook, and it rules out any hidden effect larger than about 2 percent.

Two other results pointed toward technology. Cornell engineers led by Karan Mehta described in PRX Quantum a chip that cools trapped ions to near their quantum ground state in about 150 microseconds, roughly three times faster than a conventional bench of lasers, and it handles all three directions of jiggling at once. Since cooling eats a big chunk of a trapped-ion quantum computer’s running time, that is like cutting the warm-up lap out of every race, although so far it has been shown on just a single ion. And at National Taiwan University, Danru Qu’s team reported in Nature Communications the first evidence of the orbital Nernst effect, in which a flow of heat nudges the orbital motion of electrons sideways. The layered device used ordinary oxidized copper as its source, which hints that the waste heat every chip produces might one day help drive magnetic memory instead of simply being dumped.

So what does a week like this mean for the rest of us? Mostly that physics is in a phase of confirmation and refinement, and that is quietly a good thing. When a theory written for liquid helium correctly predicts how a whirlpool dies in a cloud of potassium atoms, it tells us nature uses the same rules in very different places. When LHCb comes up empty again, it narrows the search for why the universe is made of matter at all, a mystery that remains wide open. And when unruly plasma and stray heat turn out to be useful rather than merely annoying, the road to fusion power and cooler, more efficient electronics gets a little clearer.

Science Daily — Monday, November 16, 2026
Space

This Week in Space

Most of astronomy is a game of faint signals. The light from the far edge of the universe arrives after billions of years of travel, a rock on Mars keeps its secrets a few microns deep, and a world the size of a small continent can hide beyond Neptune for a decade because nobody happened to be looking in the right patch of sky. This past week was full of discoveries that came down to looking harder and smarter, with new survey telescopes, patient radio listening and a rover that refuses to stop scratching at the surface of an ancient riverbed.

The headline from Mars came on November 12 in Science Advances. Using SHERLOC, an instrument on its robotic arm that reads the chemical fingerprints of minerals by shining a laser on them, NASA’s Perseverance rover detected complex organic carbon in two rocks from the Bright Angel formation in Jezero crater. These are the same rocks linked to the potential biosignature NASA announced in September 2025. The carbon sits only a few microns below the surface, thinner than a sheet of paper, which makes it the shallowest such detection on Mars. That is surprising, because the Martian surface is bathed in radiation that should break this kind of chemistry apart. Co-lead Ashley Murphy of the Planetary Science Institute called it “encouraging for Martian habitability,” but the team is careful: this kind of carbon can be made by life or by ordinary geology, and telling them apart will take laboratories on Earth. Further out, the Vera C. Rubin Observatory turned up a possible sixth dwarf planet, a body nicknamed 2026 ED138 that orbits about 46 times farther from the Sun than Earth does. Its size is still uncertain, somewhere between 600 and 1,500 kilometres across. Harvard–Smithsonian’s Matthew Holman found it with software that sifts billions of points of light for the handful that move together, like spotting one firefly drifting across a city skyline. The University of Washington’s Mario Jurić called it “the largest trans-Neptunian object found in about 20 years.” It had been hiding against a crowded stretch of the Milky Way, and it is still only a candidate until astronomers confirm it is round.

The deepest look came from Europe’s Euclid telescope, which found 31 ancient quasars. These are blazing disks of gas spiralling into giant black holes, and each one shines about as brightly as a trillion Suns. Two of them, seen when the universe was only about 670 million years old, are now the earliest quasars ever observed, edging past a record set in 2021. Astronomers spent more than a decade finding around ten quasars this old, and Euclid beat that number in about a year. That deepens an old puzzle: how did black holes grow so enormous so soon after the Big Bang? Closer to home, a team led by Sam McSweeney, then at Curtin University, reported in Nature Astronomy on a dead star that spins about every eight minutes and sends out radio pulses as it turns, more than 5,000 light-years away. It belongs to a strange new class called long-period transients, only about a dozen of which are known, and it is the first found with a Sun-like companion. The radio signal was clear enough to trace the white dwarf’s orbit, which points to the pulses coming from at or near the white dwarf itself. Meanwhile, in low Earth orbit, Caltech’s ASTRA robot built part of a structure by itself, extending two ready-made sections of a truss and then assembling a third. The team says it is the first structure ever built autonomously in space, even though the full 1.4-metre antenna will not be finished before the mission ends in December.

So why should anyone outside an observatory care? This week shows space science entering a new phase. Survey machines like Rubin and Euclid now sweep the sky so thoroughly that discoveries arrive in batches rather than one at a time, and robots are starting to do construction work that once needed astronauts. It also shows the discipline that keeps the excitement honest. The Mars carbon is not proof of life, the new world beyond Pluto is not yet a dwarf planet, and nobody knows what makes an eight-minute radio star tick. Still, every one of those open questions came from looking more carefully than anyone had before.

Science Daily — Sunday, November 15, 2026
Sunday Long Read

The Fjord That Rang the Planet Like a Bell

Geology

In September 2023, seismometers from the Arctic to Antarctica started picking up something nobody could explain. Earthquakes arrive as a jumble of jolts across many frequencies, then fade. This was different. It was a single, steady note, a vibration repeating roughly every 92 seconds, far too slow for any human to feel, and it went on. And on. For nine days the whole planet hummed. Researchers trading notes online gave it a name borrowed from UFO lore: an Unidentified Seismic Object. As Kristian Svennevig of the Geological Survey of Denmark and Greenland later put it, everybody was puzzled and no one had the faintest idea what was causing it.

The answer was hiding in one of the emptiest places on Earth. Dickson Fjord, in remote East Greenland, is a long, narrow channel of seawater hemmed in by sheer rock walls, with a sharp bend partway along its length. On September 16, 2023, a mountaintop rising about 1.2 kilometres above the water gave way. More than 25 million cubic metres of rock and ice, enough to fill something like 10,000 Olympic swimming pools, crashed down a gully, rode over a glacier and plunged into the fjord. The impact threw up a tsunami around 200 metres high. Seventy kilometres away, waves still about four metres tall damaged a research station on the island of Ella Ø and wiped out archaeological sites along the shore. Nobody saw it happen. The fjord sits on a route used by cruise ships, but by sheer luck none were nearby that day.

Joining the landslide to the mystery hum took an unusual piece of detective work. Seismologists who had been chasing the signal met up with Greenlandic and Danish scientists who had heard reports of a giant wave; the Danish military sailed into the fjord days later and photographed the scarred mountain face and the freshly gouged shoreline. Eventually 68 scientists from 40 institutions in 15 countries pooled their data, and in 2024 they published their explanation in the journal Science. The tsunami, they argued, never really escaped. Instead of racing out to open ocean, the water got trapped by the fjord's walls and its tight bend, and settled into what physicists call a seiche: a standing wave sloshing from one side of the channel to the other, like the water in a bathtub when you shift your weight. (One team member reportedly tried recreating the effect in a home bathtub, without success. It turns out you need a very particular bathtub: kilometres long, walled by cliffs and bent in just the right place.)

Here is the clever part. Each time that enormous mass of water slapped against one wall and then the other, it pushed on the solid rock of the Earth, with a peak force the team estimated at around 500 billion newtons. Push a planet back and forth at a perfectly regular rhythm and it rings, the way a wine glass sings when you run a damp finger round its rim. Computer models showed that a seiche a few metres high, rocking roughly every 90 seconds, would produce exactly the seismic note detected around the world, and would fade just as slowly as the real signal did. Then, about a month later, a second, smaller collapse in the same fjord set off a second, weaker hum.

Still, a model is a model. What nobody had was a direct picture of the wave itself, and ordinary satellite altimeters, which measure sea height along a single thin track, pass over any given spot only rarely. Rescue came from an unexpected direction. A new NASA and French space agency satellite called SWOT, launched in late 2022 to map rivers, lakes and the ocean surface in two dimensions, happened to pass over East Greenland during both events. In June 2025, a team led by Thomas Monahan at the University of Oxford published an analysis of those images in Nature Communications under a title that could double as a film poster: "Observations of the seiche that shook the world." The water surface was visibly tilted across the fjord, by several metres in September and about half as much in October, tipping first one way and then the other, just as the theory said.

Why should anyone outside a geology department care? Partly because it is science working as it should: a strange anomaly, a global scramble, an explanation almost too odd to be true, then confirmation from an instrument built for something else. But mostly because of why the mountain fell. For decades the glacier at its foot had been thinning as the Arctic warmed, slowly removing the icy buttress that had propped up the slope. This was the first landslide and tsunami ever recorded in eastern Greenland, and the researchers stress that it is unlikely to be the last. As permafrost thaws and glaciers retreat, steep slopes across the polar regions are losing their grip, and more and more ships and visitors are heading into the very fjords where they stand.

There is something quietly unsettling about the whole story. A catastrophe unfolded in a place with no witnesses, and the only reason we know its full shape is that the Earth itself kept ringing for nine days, loud enough for instruments on every continent to hear. The planet, it turns out, was telling us something. It took a whole world of scientists to learn how to listen.

Neuroscience

This Week in Neuroscience

Neuroscience has a habit of finding the brain where nobody thought to look for it, and this week was a good example. Today thousands of brain scientists start converging on Washington, D.C., for the Society for Neuroscience’s annual meeting, which runs through Wednesday at the Walter E. Washington Convention Center. The week leading up to it delivered a handful of studies that, taken together, tell a single story: thinking is not just something that happens behind your eyes. It happens in your gut, at the junctions where nerves meet muscle, and in brains built on a completely different blueprint from ours.

Start with a cup of coffee. Almost nobody likes their first one, yet millions of us end up craving that bitterness. In a study published in Nature on November 4, Zachary Knight’s lab at UCSF found a likely reason. When nutrients such as sugar and fat reach the gut, sensors there signal the brain, which releases dopamine slowly over several minutes into a region called the anterior basolateral amygdala. That is very different from the split-second dopamine bursts textbooks usually describe. Think of it less as a jolt of reward and more as a slow-building note to self: that flavor came with calories, remember it. When the team cut this pathway in mice, the animals still ate normally but could no longer learn which flavors predicted food. When they switched it on artificially, mice developed a taste for a drink with no calories at all. Brain-imaging data from people showed the same signal, and in both species it was oddly stronger on the left side of the brain, which nobody can yet explain. A second gut-to-brain surprise came from the University of Central Florida, where James Hickman and Xiufang Guo grew a tiny nerve-and-muscle junction on a chip using motor neurons carrying inherited Alzheimer’s mutations. With no brain or spinal cord anywhere in the system, the signals from nerve to muscle still faltered. That could help explain why balance and walking sometimes change early in the disease, though the authors are careful to say they have not shown those changes come before memory loss in people. Then there are the crows. In Science, Philipp Schmidbauer and Andreas Nieder at the University of Tübingen showed two carrion crows clouds of moving dots and asked them to peck left or right to report the main direction. The birds answered in about 235 milliseconds on average, roughly half the time humans and monkeys need, and they stayed accurate. Recordings from single neurons in a region that plays roughly the role of our prefrontal cortex showed evidence building toward a threshold, just as in mammal brains, only faster. It is like discovering that a rival engine design reaches the same answer on a fraction of the fuel. Rounding out the week, a team at Florida International University pooled 57 brain-imaging studies in Scientific Reports and found that people with insomnia, sleep apnea, sleepwalking and nightmare disorders tend to have a smaller thalamus, the brain’s relay hub for attention. Whether poor sleep causes that or results from it is still an open question.

So what does this mean for a curious non-scientist? Your cravings, your stride and your split-second judgments are shaped by a nervous system that is far more spread out, and far more varied across the animal kingdom, than the old picture of a lone command center in the skull. The practical payoffs are real but early. Doctors may one day spot Alzheimer’s in how someone walks, and the gut-to-brain dopamine pathway gives researchers a new way to think about why we overeat. The bigger lesson is humbling. A bird brain the size of a walnut can beat ours at a speed test, which is a good reminder that there is more than one way to build a mind.

Epidemiology

This Week in Epidemiology

Epidemiology is the science of counting carefully when counting is hardest. Its tools are case tallies, genetic sequences and contact tracing, and its basic question is always whether a curve is bending. This week that question came up in very different places: an Ebola outbreak in central Africa that keeps setting grim records, measles in the United States, a bird flu virus still moving through American dairy barns, and a mosquito-borne virus turning up somewhere it has no business being.

The largest story remains the Bundibugyo Ebola outbreak in the Democratic Republic of the Congo. According to the UK Health Security Agency’s weekly outbreak monitor, the DRC had logged 11,042 confirmed cases and 5,387 confirmed deaths as of November 9. That was 510 more cases than the previous week, with roughly 65 new cases a day, most of them in Ituri and North Kivu. It is already the second-largest Ebola outbreak on record, and Bundibugyo has no licensed vaccine or treatment. On November 6, militants killed 33 people at a health post in North Kivu, and hundreds of Ebola patients at nearby hospitals reportedly fled into the community. That is the kind of disruption that undoes months of contact tracing in an afternoon. Against that backdrop, Doctors Without Borders urged wider use of Ervebo, a vaccine built against a related Ebola species, the Zaire strain. Its case rests on animal studies and early anecdotal signs that previously vaccinated staff are faring better. Think of it as hoping a key cut for one lock will also turn its cousin. There is better news along the edges of the outbreak: Kenya, which has recorded nine cases, has had no new ones since October 19 and has begun its countdown to being declared Ebola-free.

Closer to home, the Pan American Health Organization’s independent measles commission met in Washington this week to decide whether measles is once again endemic in the United States. A country loses elimination status once a chain of transmission runs unbroken for a year, and the most persuasive evidence is often genomic: sequencing can show whether scattered outbreaks are really one family tree of virus. The CDC has counted more than 5,100 cases in 2026, compared with 85 in 2000, the year the country eliminated the disease, and at least eight deaths this year. Bird flu is also still spreading among cattle. Oregon recorded its first H5N1-infected dairy herd, part of 51 herd detections nationally in the past 30 days. In Washington’s Yakima Valley, where a resident died of H5N1 earlier this month, the United Farm Workers union says human infections among dairy workers are being undercounted, because workers fear immigration enforcement and skip testing. Elsewhere, China has reported more H9N2 bird flu cases, 37 so far this year compared with 29 in all of 2025. Switzerland confirmed its first locally acquired chikungunya cases, four of them in the southern canton of Ticino, another sign that viruses carried by warm-climate mosquitoes are reaching places that used to be too cold for them.

What ties all this together is that an epidemic is only as visible as the people looking for it. A health post that is attacked, a farmworker who stays home rather than get tested, a measles case that is never sequenced: each one leaves a gap in the map. Sometimes, as with the US measles review, what the careful counting shows is uncomfortable. Still, the counting is what lets anyone notice that a curve has started to bend, whether that means Kenya ticking off days without a case or a country deciding to win back a status it once held.

Science Daily — Thursday, November 12, 2026
Mathematics

This Week in Mathematics

Mathematics has spent the autumn in an odd new position. After OpenAI's early-October release of hundreds of machine-generated papers, which included a claimed proof that the hypothetical "Landau–Siegel zeros" of number theory do not exist, the field's humans have been doing something they rarely get to do with a landmark result: tidying it up. Quanta Magazine reported on November 5 that mathematicians are racing to simplify the AI proofs. Oxford's Ben Green produced a reformulation through conversations with OpenAI's Astra model and claims no human originality for it. Jacob Pescador, Kevin Barreto and Roelof van Dijk then refined Green's version further using Astra and Anthropic's Opus 5.5, and Barreto, a Cambridge undergraduate, got it down to three pages. Bryce Kerr of UNSW assembled a separate re-proof of his own. If the result holds, Andrew Granville of the University of Montreal says the strange consequences those zeros would have had, including for the twin primes conjecture, are "all moot now." Quanta also flagged a sobering caveat: some of OpenAI's machine-checked Lean statements turned out to prove weaker claims than the papers themselves, a reminder that turning a sentence into code that a computer can verify still takes human judgment.

Away from the AI headlines, the week brought a lovely result about infinity itself. Ever since Cantor, mathematicians have known that some infinities are bigger than others, and they usually picture them lined up on a single ladder. In a PNAS paper published November 5, William Chan of TU Wien swapped out one of the standard rules of set theory, the axiom of choice, for a rival rule called the axiom of determinacy. Under those rules, certain infinite sizes made by raising one infinity to the power of another stop fitting on a ladder and instead need two coordinates, like points on a map. When both the base and the exponent grow, the comparison is easy. When one grows while the other shrinks, Chan says, "these two sizes need not be comparable at all," much as you can't say whether a mountain to the north is "more" than a lake to the east. Symmetry got a new vocabulary too. A team led by Sonia Mahmoudi at Tohoku University extended orbifold notation, John Conway's compact shorthand for symmetric patterns, so that it covers "layer groups": structures that repeat in two directions but have real thickness in the third, such as layered materials. Fittingly, they tested it on knitted fabric, which has no atomic lattice at all.

Two other studies turned mathematics back on the tools we use to make sense of the world. Physicists Pulin Gong and Andrew Ly at the University of Sydney reported in Nature Machine Intelligence that neural networks trained identically can still end up in different places because of the geometry of training itself. The sets of starting points that lead to each outcome appear to be "riddled basins," fractals in which every point sits arbitrarily close to points that lead somewhere else. Think of a coastline so intricate that you can never stand far enough inland to be sure which sea you'll roll into. After training roughly 500,000 networks, they found that flipping a single bit of the starting settings changed the network's internal configuration in about 48% of cases, and that making the starting settings ten billion times more precise did nothing measurable for reproducibility in one larger image-recognition model. Meanwhile, a systematic review in PLOS ONE from Uppsala University's Per Lytsy and Ronnie Pingel pulled together 55 studies covering more than 15,000 people, statisticians and statistics teachers among them. It found that misreading p-values and "statistical significance" is common even among experienced researchers. The classic mistakes are treating a significant result as proof that a hypothesis is true, or reading a p-value as the probability that it is.

The thread running through all of this is that mathematics is as much about understanding as it is about getting answers. A machine may hand over a proof, but people are still the ones distilling it into three pages that someone else can actually follow. An infinity that refuses to sit on a ladder tells us that our pictures of "bigger" depend on the rules we pick. The fractal coastlines inside AI training, and the stubborn confusion over p-values, are both reminders that a number is only as trustworthy as our grasp of where it came from. As Lytsy puts it, "Being able to use statistical methods is not the same as understanding what the results mean," and that may be the best summary of the field's year so far.

Robotics & Engineering

This Week in Robotics & Engineering

Most of what makes a body graceful never reaches conscious thought. Tap a knee and the leg kicks before the brain gets a vote. Glance at something from two angles and a shadow resolves into a cat. Robotics researchers spend a surprising share of their time trying to rebuild that quiet competence, and this week several labs chipped away at it from very different directions: some built smarts into the hardware itself, others taught the software to doubt itself, and one team applied the same “use only what you need” thinking to something as unglamorous as a concrete beam.

The most literal example comes from Tohoku University, where engineers built a reflex with no electronics at all. Their module, described in Advanced Intelligent Systems, senses when an artificial muscle stretches past a set point and makes it contract, all through its physical structure. It works roughly the way the muscle spindles in your own thigh do. The robot reproduces a knee-jerk and, with a higher-level controller adjusting the trigger point at each phase of a stride, walks on two legs. Think of it as moving some of the thinking out of the brain and into the joints, which saves wiring, computation and power. A similar idea, that the best assistance works with the body instead of overriding it, runs through a Science Robotics piece on underwater exoskeletons from the Shenzhen Institute of Advanced Technology. Their soft hip exosuit, PEAKED, helps a diver’s kicks and cut the volume of air breathed per minute by nearly 30% during flutter kicking. For a scuba diver, that means more time underwater on the same tank.

On the software side, two MIT projects tackle the gap between what a robot is told and what it ought to understand. One, called FLEX and presented at this week’s Conference on Robot Learning in Austin, lets a large language model generalize from a single instruction. A simulated arm told “don’t spill on my laptop” also learned to keep coffee away from a nearby phone, because it had inferred the broader rule: avoid electronics. FLEX has only been tested in simulation so far. The other project, UQ-DAAAM from Luca Carlone’s lab, takes on a stranger problem: robots that misremember what they have seen, logging a gray chimney as a squirrel or a defibrillator as a fire extinguisher. The system scores how unsure it is about each label, picks a couple of extra viewpoints that would settle the question, and merges the descriptions. It resolved the ambiguity for about 92% of objects with only two extra captions each. It is the machine version of stepping sideways to get a better look.

Not all of the week’s cleverness moved. Another MIT group showed that computer-optimized concrete beams can be cast in cheap, sewn fabric molds. Their 3-meter test beam used about a third of the concrete and half the steel of a conventional beam carrying the same load, which matters when cement alone accounts for roughly 8% of human-caused carbon emissions. The beam’s ultimate strength has not been tested yet, and the method is cheaper only where labor is inexpensive. So what links a walking reflex, a diving suit, a skeptical robot and a lumpy beam? Each one is efficient because it is smart about where effort goes. That matters more than headline-grabbing humanoid backflips, because it decides whether these machines and structures end up affordable, safe and useful enough for ordinary people to rely on.
