Batteries & Electricity

This Week in Batteries & Electricity

Battery science has a funny rhythm. The headline-grabbing breakthroughs arrive in bursts, and in between there are stretches where the field is simply doing the slow, unglamorous work of making last year's idea survive ten thousand charge cycles. This past week was one of those stretches.

Our searches turned up no major battery or grid-electricity study published in the past seven days, and the news sources we could reach were thin on anything fresher than mid-October. Stretching the window a little, the most interesting recent item came from the University of Warwick and the University of Birmingham, in Nature Communications. The team heated specially designed molecules and watched what formed on the way to the final material, rather than only looking at the end product. It is a bit like studying a cake by pulling it out of the oven every two minutes. Along the way they caught a previously unknown, short-lived form of bismuth vanadate, a material studied for splitting water with sunlight, plus another intermediate that could store a lot of lithium.

The so-what is gentle but real: some of tomorrow's battery materials may be hiding in the middle of recipes we already use, in structures that vanish before anyone looks. The researchers themselves stress that they tested only a few precursors, so this is a promising map rather than a finished road. We will keep watching for the next real jolt.

Quiet week for Batteries & Electricity — no major developments found in the past seven days. The last notable finding was the Warwick and Birmingham work on hidden “in-between” materials, reported around October 7–9.

Sunday Long Read

One Tree, Forty-Seven Thousand Trunks

Botany

Stand in the right spot in Utah's Fishlake National Forest in October and the hillside looks like any other aspen grove in its autumn gold: a few thousand pale trunks, trembling leaves, a high mountain light. What you cannot see is that you are standing inside a single organism. The grove has a name, Pando, which is Latin for “I spread,” and by the usual accounting it is one quaking aspen, male, genetically identical from one end to the other, sprawling across roughly 106 acres at about 8,900 feet.

The idea took decades to settle. In the late 1960s and 1970s the forest ecologist Burton Barnes studied aspen clones using aerial photographs and the stubborn habits of individual trees, such as which ones leaf out first in spring and which turn colour first in fall. Stems that behave in lockstep are probably one plant. The Pando clone was described in print in 1976, and in 1992 researchers at the University of Colorado Boulder re-examined it and confirmed a single male clone. Michael Grant gave it the name in a 1993 magazine article. A genetic study published in 2008 by Jennifer DeWoody, Karen Mock and colleagues backed up the earlier, more circumstantial work.

How does a tree become a forest? Quaking aspens rarely bother with seeds. Instead, the root system sends up fresh shoots, called suckers, that become new trunks. Each trunk is, in effect, a limb of one underground body. Pando is estimated to hold around 47,000 of these stems and to weigh something like 6,000 metric tons, which is why it is often called the heaviest known living organism on land. No single trunk gets old: they typically live 130 to 150 years and are then replaced from below. If you want an analogy, think of a city that has stood for millennia even though every building in it has been rebuilt many times over.

That is exactly what makes the question of Pando's age so slippery. You cannot count tree rings on an organism whose trunks keep dying and being reborn. For years the figure circulating was 80,000 years, and a still-bolder claim of up to a million appeared in the 1990s, but those numbers were extrapolations rather than measurements. Habitat modelling published in 2008 and 2017 argued that the clone could hardly be older than about 14,000 years, since the last ice age would have scoured aspens from the area. Charcoal studies in 2022 pointed to a lower bound near 9,000 years. Then a 2024 preprint from Rozenn Pineau and colleagues tried something new: counting the tiny somatic mutations that accumulate in the cells of different stems, much like reading a molecular clock. Their first estimate suggested 16,000 to 80,000 years. A revised version this year narrows it to something like 16,000 to 37,000. Whether that survives peer review is still unsettled, and the honest answer is that Pando is somewhere between ten thousand and several tens of thousands of years old, and nobody knows precisely where.

Age, though, is not the most pressing problem. Over the last few decades the grove has had trouble making new trees. Mule deer and elk browse the young suckers before they can grow tall, and in parts of Pando the middle-aged stems that should be coming up behind the old ones are simply missing. Researchers including Paul Rogers have argued that herbivory is the main brake on recovery. Roughly 80 percent of the area is now enclosed by tall wildlife fencing, cattle grazing in one corner ended in 2024, and a protection plan approved in 2022 has been rolling out, with reports of reduced deer and elk activity nearby. Early signs of regrowth inside the fences are encouraging, though a clone this big will take a long time to show whether the cure is working.

There is something quietly humbling in all of this. We usually think of individuality as a body with edges: a dog, an oak, a person. Pando is a reminder that life can be arranged differently, as one genome wearing 47,000 trunks, rebuilding itself constantly, and carrying on, perhaps, since before agriculture existed. It has survived fire, drought and the end of an ice age. What it may not be able to survive, unaided, is an overfed deer population and a warming, drier mountain. The oldest living thing in the grove is not any tree you can touch; it is the pattern underneath, and whether that pattern continues is now partly up to us.

Agriculture

This Week in Agriculture

Agriculture is a field quietly caught in a tug of war between two very old problems: how to grow more, and how to do it without wearing out the soil, the water and the plants themselves. This week's research touched both. Some of it is about squeezing more out of the crops we already have, and some is about understanding why those crops, and the farms around them, are so fragile in the first place.

The most ambitious story comes from KTH Royal Institute of Technology in Sweden, where a team led by Paul Hudson went after photosynthesis itself. The weak link is Rubisco, the enzyme that grabs carbon dioxide from the air and turns it into plant material. It is famously sluggish, and it sometimes grabs oxygen by mistake, which costs the plant energy to clean up. Think of a factory worker on an assembly line who occasionally picks up the wrong part and has to stop to fix it. The team built and tested around 15,000 mutated versions of a bacterial Rubisco, each tagged with a short DNA barcode so thousands could be tracked at once. Two top variants fumbled oxygen less often, but both gave up some of their overall speed, and helpful mutations frequently stopped helping when combined. The paper, in Science Advances, is candid that these effects are hard to predict. Don't expect heat-proof wheat next season; this is a map of the terrain, not a finished road.

Closer to the farm gate, a few other items this week point at how much of agriculture is about managing problems that appear as a side effect of success. Researchers reported that faster growth in Honeycrisp apples raises the incidence and severity of bitter pit, a dead-tissue disorder that costs growers yield. A UC Davis study of 16 California fruit-tree operations questioned the assumption that autonomous machines will simply erase farm jobs. And a trio of University of Queensland plant scientists argued that conventional selective breeding may be nearing its limits, calling for a fresh approach to tapping hidden genetic potential in crops. Meanwhile, on the ecological side, ScienceDaily highlighted work finding that honey bees aggressively chase wild pollinators away from flowers, a reminder that even the helpers in our food system can crowd each other out.

The so-what for a curious non-scientist is that the next gains in food production are unlikely to come from one miracle. They will come from patient work on the unglamorous parts: an enzyme that makes mistakes, an apple that grows too fast for its own good, a labor market that machines may reshape rather than empty. It is slow, but it is the kind of slow that ends up on your dinner table.

Science Daily — Friday, October 23, 2026
Medicine

This Week in Medicine

Medicine moves in two speeds: slow, careful trials, and the sudden days when regulators and conference rooms deliver answers all at once. This week had several of those days. The headline came from the ESMO cancer meeting, where detailed data confirmed that Moderna and Merck's personalized mRNA cancer vaccine cut the risk of melanoma coming back by 42% compared with standard treatment. The idea is to read the genetic fingerprint of one patient's tumour and train their immune system to recognise it, a bit like handing a security guard a photo of one specific burglar instead of a general warning.

The FDA was busy too. On October 20 it approved Hibsago (bepirovirsen) for chronic hepatitis B, given alongside existing antiviral therapy to certain adults without cirrhosis, and the agency calls it a first-of-its-kind treatment. The day before, it granted accelerated approval to a new use of the immunotherapy Jemperli that may let some patients with locally advanced rectal cancer avoid chemotherapy, radiation and surgery. Accelerated approval means the early evidence is strong enough to proceed while longer-term results are still being gathered.

Not every result was a win, and the disappointments matter as much. A two-year clinical trial found that fish oil supplements successfully delivered omega-3s to the brain but did not improve memory or protect against Alzheimer's-related shrinkage. Meanwhile, Alnylam reported early promise for a gene-silencing drug in Huntington's disease, though STAT notes it is still unknown whether it will slow the disease's progression, and a small trial of 63 patients suggested a 15-minute, steroid-free procedure could help people avoid carpal tunnel surgery.

The so-what for the rest of us: the most hopeful stories this week were about precision, treating one tumour or one virus with a tool built for it, while the fish oil trial is a useful reminder that a supplement being popular is not evidence that it works. Early-stage results, like the Huntington's and carpal tunnel ones, are promising signals rather than finished answers.

Science Daily — Thursday, October 22, 2026
Biology

This Week in Biology

Biology’s great trick is that the same few molecular parts get reused for almost everything. A protein that twists a cell in an embryo can end up deciding which way a shell spirals; a bacterial enzyme built to chew on one thing turns out to nibble on another. This week’s stories share that flavour of surprise, with old machinery found doing unexpected work, and several of them are best read as promising leads rather than finished answers.

The headline-grabber came from Bonn, where a team led by Oliver Brüstle reported in Aging Cell that they had taken immature red blood cells from an 80-year-old donor and turned them directly into neural stem cells. Measured with “epigenetic clocks”, which estimate biological age from chemical tags on DNA, the new cells read as younger than 20, a rewind of roughly 60 years that unfolded gradually over about 50 days. Think of it as a rewound odometer rather than a rebuilt engine: the clocks are estimates, this was a dish of cells and not a person, and the authors do not claim a treatment. The value is as a slow-motion model for studying what speeds rejuvenation up or holds it back.

Elsewhere the news was wonderfully small-scale. In Japan and the UK, researchers writing in Current Biology traced whether snails of the genus Euhadra coil their shells left or right to an ancient variant of a myosin gene, a molecular motor that nudges cells to twist one way in the earliest embryo. Press coverage says both mirror-image forms develop normally and can interbreed, and that the left-coiling version shows signs of natural selection; those details are worth checking against the paper itself. Meanwhile, at the University of Konstanz, a bacterial enzyme nicknamed “Pac-Man” for its unusually wide active site was shown in The ISME Journal to digest certain bioplastics, including long-chain polyesters, in under a year, while barely touching conventional polyethylene. Its odd secret is that it also breaks down penicillin, though that does not show it makes bacteria resistant. In Norwich, John Innes Centre scientists reported in Science Advances that they had re-engineered a class of cereal immune receptors so they recognise two different effectors from the rice blast fungus family, a disease that wipes out enough rice each year to feed around 60 million people. That work was done in isolated wheat cells, so field protection is a long way off. And in a cave in Yunnan, China, 2,062 metres up, scientists described Erhaia dijiang, a pale, eyeless freshwater snail, with only three living adults found in the survey, a reminder that new species can arrive already on the edge.

So what for the rest of us? The week’s thread is that biology progresses by noticing how general its parts are. A motor protein that sets a snail’s handedness hints at how our own left-right body plan gets laid down; an enzyme that eats designed plastics suggests we should design plastics for microbes to eat; a retooled immune receptor offers crops a sturdier defence. None of these is a cure or a cleanup yet, but each is a credible next step, and that is usually how the real progress looks.

Science Daily — Wednesday, October 21, 2026
Chemistry

This Week in Chemistry

Chemistry has a reputation for beakers and bubbling, but much of the field is really about one stubborn question: how do you persuade atoms to rearrange when they would rather stay put? The tougher the bond, the more ingenuity it takes, and this week produced a pleasing trio of answers, each aimed at a problem that touches ordinary life: forever chemicals, plastic waste and clean hydrogen.

Start with the forever chemicals. PFAS earn their nickname because the carbon-fluorine bond holding them together is among the strongest in organic chemistry, so water treatment has mostly moved them from one place to another rather than getting rid of them. A team led by Bruce Rittmann at Arizona State University, with partners at Rice, Tongji, Nanjing and the National University of Singapore, reported in PNAS a system that destroys them instead. Hollow membrane fibers are coated with palladium nanoparticles, and hydrogen gas fed through the fibers splits on the metal into very reactive hydrogen atoms. The palladium pulls in the PFAS molecules and the hydrogen together, and the bonds snap. Over a 58-day continuous test on six regulated PFAS, more than 90 percent of each compound disappeared and about 83 percent of the fluorine was accounted for. Palladium is expensive, and the authors are frank that engineering work remains. Meanwhile, at ETH Zurich, Athina Anastasaki’s group published in Nature Chemistry a simpler way to recycle acrylic glass, the clear plastic better known as Plexiglas. One cheap, off-the-shelf reagent coaxes the polymer to fall apart into its original building blocks without solvents, starting a little above 200°C instead of the roughly 400°C earlier methods demanded. Up to 98 percent comes back as monomers, pure enough to make new plastic of the same quality, even from dyed or contaminated starting material. Think of un-baking a cake back into flour and eggs. It works only for acrylic so far, and ETH has filed a patent.

The third story is a reminder that nature got there first. Researchers at Ruhr University Bochum, led by Thomas Happe and first author Jifu Duan, reported in the Journal of the American Chemical Society how certain hydrogenase enzymes, which make hydrogen gas at astonishing speed, pass protons along. A mobile histidine, an amino acid, swings like a pendulum on a fixed pivot, picking a proton off a neighbour and handing it into the enzyme’s internal channel; zinc jams the relay by sticking to one spot. Understanding that hand-off matters because engineers would love catalysts that match these enzymes without relying on rare metals. The so-what for a curious non-scientist: three very different labs are chipping away at the same theme, which is making hard chemistry gentler, cheaper and cleaner. None of these is on a store shelf yet, but each swaps brute force for cleverness, and that is how a lot of chemistry quietly changes the world.

Science Daily — Tuesday, October 20, 2026
Physics

This Week in Physics

Physics has a habit of making its biggest advances in two opposite places at once: in the grand questions about why the universe looks the way it does, and in the fussy, unglamorous business of making a measurement or a machine behave. This week leaned toward the second kind, and a surprising amount of it circled one theme, which is how much of modern physics is really a fight against noise. Quantum computers, neutrino detectors and atomic clocks all live or die by how well their makers can separate a faint signal from the racket of the real world.

The loudest example came from Google’s quantum team, which reported in Science that its Willow processor had cut the error rate of so-called magic states by about 40 times. Magic states are a special ingredient a quantum computer needs to run its most powerful programs, a bit like the rare spice a recipe cannot do without, and until now they have been made by a wasteful process that eats up huge numbers of qubits. Google’s alternative, called magic state cultivation, grows the ingredient in place and throws away the batches that fail the taste test; only about eight percent survive, but those that do reach a logical fidelity of 99.99 percent. It is a hardware first for an idea proposed only in 2024, though the team admits one storage test came out about seven times worse than simulations predicted, and that stubborn lab noise nearly derailed the early runs. In the same spirit, researchers reported silicon spin qubits operating three at a time with gate fidelities above 99.99 percent, a sign that the humble silicon of ordinary chips is keeping pace with fancier rivals. A separate theory result proved a long-suspected speed limit on how fast quantum objects can settle into thermal equilibrium, the physics version of finding out how quickly a hot cup of coffee must, at minimum, cool to room temperature.

Meanwhile, a long-running neutrino puzzle may be dissolving into arithmetic. For years, experiments using gallium to catch neutrinos from radioactive sources have seen about 20 percent fewer than expected, a gap that tantalised some physicists as a hint of a hidden “sterile” neutrino. Newer calculations, which stop assuming that the electron and nuclear parts of the capture process can be treated separately, shrink the predicted capture rate by roughly the missing amount, suggesting the anomaly may be a modelling shortcut rather than new physics. The result is not yet a universally accepted verdict, and a sceptic would say that explanations of this kind have tempted the field before. At the precision end of the spectrum, Korea’s metrology institute KRISS reportedly remeasured a key strontium transition frequency, the first fresh look at it since 1938, with about 50 times better precision, the sort of quiet groundwork on which better clocks are built.

So what does it all mean for the rest of us? None of these stories will change your week, but together they show where the frontier really sits right now: not in a single dramatic discovery, but in the patient craft of making quantum machines reliable, and of double-checking our maths before declaring a new particle. A fault-tolerant quantum computer remains years away, yet each tenfold improvement in error rates moves it from fantasy toward engineering. And the gallium story is a reminder that sometimes the most exciting thing a physicist can find is that the old theory, calculated more carefully, was right all along.

Space

This Week in Space

Space science has a way of making the very large and the very small trade places. This week, astronomers were mapping the lopsided outline of a planet more than a thousand light-years away, while others were fretting over a mirror adjusted by a sliver of a hair’s width, and still others were wondering whether two black holes, each millions of times the mass of our Sun, are about to merge within a human lifetime. It is a good reminder that most of astronomy is patient measurement, and that every so often the measurements start to sound like a plot twist.

The planet is WASP-17b, a puffy “hot Jupiter” roughly 1,300 light-years away. A Cornell-led team, reporting in Nature Astronomy, used a technique they call transmission strings on James Webb Space Telescope data. Instead of assuming the planet’s atmosphere forms a neat circular ring around it, they measured how far the atmosphere extends at many points around the edge, a bit like measuring a balloon’s shape with a tape measure rather than assuming it is round. The result is a slightly egg-shaped atmosphere, more extended on the evening side, where winds sweep heat around from the scorching dayside. Nothing here contradicts earlier predictions; what is new is that we can now actually see the shape. Meanwhile, back at home, NASA reported that its Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope is beating expectations by nearly 30 percent after engineers nudged a mirror by a tiny amount, sharpening its view before it ever leaves the ground. And Webb also turned up an ancient galaxy cluster with an unexpectedly dense concentration of dark matter, the kind of finding that makes cosmologists reach for their models and squint.

The week’s most dramatic headline comes with a generous helping of caution. Researchers led by Silke Britzen at the Max Planck Institute for Radio Astronomy argue that the galaxy Markarian 501 hides two supermassive black holes orbiting each other every 121 days or so, separated by only a few hundred times the Earth–Sun distance. Depending on their masses, they could merge in as little as a century. The case rests on the odd behavior of the galaxy’s jets, and the pair cannot be seen separately, so it is not a conclusive detection; confirmation would most likely come from the faint, low-frequency gravitational waves that pulsar timing arrays are built to hear. On the Moon, the long-running puzzle of why its two faces look so different continues to be chipped away at by samples from the far side, which point to a cooler far-side interior and a more complicated volcanic history, though no single agreed-upon answer has emerged.

So what should a curious non-scientist take from all this? That the universe is being measured more precisely than ever, from the shape of a distant atmosphere to the focus of a telescope not yet in orbit, and that the boldest claims are the ones to hold loosely until a second line of evidence arrives. If you want something to do rather than read, the Orionid meteor shower peaks overnight on October 21–22, and there is a citizen-science project looking for volunteers to hunt for “missing” black holes in the Milky Way in NASA telescope data. Both require nothing more than curiosity.
