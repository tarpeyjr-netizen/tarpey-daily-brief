Sunday Long Read

The Crystal Made of Time

Materials Science

Every crystal you have ever seen — quartz, salt, the diamond in a ring — is a pattern repeating in space. Atoms sit at regular intervals, over and over, and that simple repetition is responsible for an astonishing amount of the modern world. In 1987, two physicists working independently, Eli Yablonovitch and Sajeev John, realized you could exploit the same trick with light. Build a material with a repeating pattern of optical properties at roughly the scale of a light wave, and photons start behaving like electrons in a semiconductor: certain colors are forbidden, others are funneled along invisible channels. These "photonic crystals" now sit inside fiber-optic cables, lasers, and sensors. They are, in a sense, sculptures for light.

But there was always a stranger idea lurking underneath. What if, instead of repeating the pattern in space, you repeated it in time? Instead of alternating stripes of glass and air, you take a single piece of material and make it flicker — changing how strongly it bends light, then changing it back, over and over, at a rhythm so fast it keeps pace with the light wave itself. On paper, the mathematics does something delicious. Swap space for time in the equations and the "forbidden colors" of an ordinary photonic crystal become forbidden momenta. And where a spatial crystal suppresses light, a temporal one does the opposite: it can pump energy in, amplifying the light passing through it. The theory has been sitting there, tantalizing, for years. The problem was that nobody could build one.

The difficulty is easy to state and brutal to solve. To make a time crystal for light, you need to change a material's optical properties by a lot — think of forcing a pane of glass to become something like a mirror and back again — and you need to do it on the timescale of a single oscillation of the light wave. For visible light that means switching things in femtoseconds. Real materials simply do not respond that fast or that strongly. Every attempt ran into the same wall: you could get speed or you could get depth of modulation, but never both at once, and without both there is no crystal, just a slightly wobbly material.

On July 29 of this year, a team led by PhD student Tingwen Guo at the École Polytechnique, working with the Collège de France, CNRS, and Germany's Helmholtz-Zentrum Dresden-Rossendorf, published in Nature the first experimental photonic time crystal. Their solution was to sidestep visible light entirely and work in the terahertz range — frequencies that sit in the awkward no-man's-land between radio waves and infrared light, faster than any electronic circuit can follow but slower than optics normally operates. As Yannis Laplace, the École Polytechnique physicist whose group has been chasing this for years, puts it, terahertz is the frontier between electronics and photonics: full of opportunity, and technologically underdeveloped compared to the territory on either side.

The device itself is a beautiful piece of engineering, and describing it feels a bit like describing a castle. On top sit gold stripes about forty micrometers wide — roughly half the width of a human hair — separated by narrow gaps, arranged in a crenellated pattern like the battlements of a fortress. Underneath is a thin insulating layer, and beneath that a crystal of indium antimonide, a semiconductor. The gaps between the gold battlements act as tiny cavities that trap terahertz light between the metal above and the semiconductor below. At that interface something remarkable happens: the semiconductor's free electrons start sloshing in unison, a collective wave called a surface plasmon that grabs hold of the light and keeps it oscillating in place.

Then the team hit this structure with terahertz pulses from TELBE, a specialized light source attached to HZDR's ELBE particle accelerator — one of the few instruments on Earth capable of producing terahertz pulses that are both intensely powerful and precisely timed, wave crest by wave crest. Under that driving, the metamaterial's reflectivity swung wildly back and forth on picosecond timescales, matching the rhythm of the light itself. The crystal in time existed.

And then it did something counterintuitive. Normally, when you drive a system harder, you heat it and lose more energy — push a swing too aggressively and you mostly make noise. Here, the harder they drove the metamaterial, the less light it lost. Plasmonic systems are notoriously leaky; the electron sloshing dissipates energy into heat with depressing efficiency, which is the main reason plasmonics has struggled to become useful technology. The temporal modulation cut those losses by more than half. A theoretical model built by Marco Schiró's group at the Collège de France reproduced the effect and explained where it comes from: the periodic driving generates a kind of emergent gain that partially cancels the dissipation. The crystal is, in effect, feeding the light back some of what it steals.

Cutting losses in half is not yet amplification, and amplification is the real prize. If a future version can push past break-even — putting more light out than went in — you have the ingredients for an entirely new kind of laser, one whose color and intensity could be retuned on demand in trillionths of a second rather than being fixed by the physics of whatever material sits inside it. That matters for medical imaging, where terahertz radiation can see through tissue without ionizing it, and for communications hungry for bandwidth that electronic components cannot deliver.

What lingers, though, is the conceptual shift. We are used to thinking of a material as a thing with fixed properties, and of engineering as the art of choosing the right thing. This experiment says a material's properties can be a rhythm rather than a fact — that you can build structure not out of stuff arranged in space, but out of change arranged in time, and that light will respond to that structure just as faithfully as it responds to a lattice of atoms. Nearly forty years after the first photonic crystals, someone has finally built one you cannot photograph, only wait for.

Science Daily — Friday, August 14, 2026
Medicine

This Week in Medicine

Most of the drama in modern medicine happens not at the moment a drug is invented but at the far less glamorous moment it actually reaches a person — which is why a quietly astonishing result out of Australia this week may end up mattering more than anything else on the list. Researchers reported the world's first human trial of a “fridge-free” tetanus-diphtheria vaccine, one engineered to survive at 30 °C for at least two years and still work. Nearly every vaccine in use today lives inside what public health people call the cold chain: an unbroken relay of refrigerated trucks, powered clinics and ice packs stretching from factory to upper arm. Break any link and the doses inside become expensive water. A vaccine that shrugs off tropical heat doesn't just save money; it removes the single biggest reason immunisation campaigns stall in places without reliable electricity.

That theme — delivery as the real frontier — ran through the whole week. Moderna dosed the first participants in a Phase I trial of mRNA-1469, a vaccine aimed at Bundibugyo ebolavirus, one of the Ebola family's nastier and least-covered members. Existing Ebola vaccines protect against the Zaire strain and essentially nothing else, so an outbreak of a different species starts from zero. Because mRNA vaccines are, in effect, printed from a genetic sequence rather than grown in vats, they can be retargeted at a new strain far faster than conventional ones — the same trick that made the COVID vaccines possible, now pointed at a pathogen most of the world has never heard of until it's already spreading. The technology also cleared a milestone on the regulatory side: the FDA approved mFLUSIVA, an mRNA-based influenza vaccine, extending the platform beyond the pandemic that made it famous into the ordinary, grinding, tens-of-thousands-of-deaths-a-year problem of seasonal flu.

Elsewhere the approvals leaned on biology doing the work rather than chemistry. The FDA cleared Tudriqev, an oncolytic viral therapy for advanced melanoma, to be used alongside the immunotherapy nivolumab. Oncolytic viruses are exactly what they sound like — a virus deliberately engineered to infect and burst tumour cells — but their real value is subtler: the wreckage left behind acts like a flare, drawing the immune system's attention to a tumour that had been hiding in plain sight, which is why pairing one with a checkpoint inhibitor makes sense. On a completely different track, the agency approved Orzeyful (oveporexton) for narcolepsy type 1. People with that condition have lost the brain cells that make orexin, the neuropeptide that holds wakefulness steady; the drug is the first to simply supply what's missing by switching on the orexin receptor directly, rather than papering over the symptoms with stimulants. And in early Parkinson's, BioVie reported encouraging Phase II results for bezisterim — still early, still a small trial, but a rare piece of good news in a field littered with failures.

If there's a through-line, it's that the week's most valuable advances weren't new molecules so much as new ways of getting biology to cooperate: a vaccine that survives the journey, a platform that can be re-aimed in weeks, a virus recruited as an informant, a missing signal simply replaced. Worth watching next: whether the heat-stable vaccine formulation can be applied to the rest of the childhood immunisation schedule, which is where it would really change the arithmetic of global health.

Science Daily — Wednesday, August 12, 2026
Chemistry

This Week in Chemistry

Most of chemistry, at its core, is about controlling where electrons go — which atom gets to keep them, which one gives them up, and how fast. That sounds abstract until you realize it's the reason batteries charge, metals rust, and catalysts turn cheap raw materials into useful ones. This week's news is basically a tour of chemists getting cleverer about that control, plus a reminder that even a good idea for cleaning up plastic waste still has to survive contact with the real world.

The standout comes from University of Wisconsin–Madison, where chemists broke a rule that's held for decades: when two molecules compete for a free electron, it's supposed to go to whichever one is easiest to reduce, full stop. Instead of fighting that preference, the team built a catalyst that just fires electrons directly into the surrounding solution — making them, in the researchers' words, about as reactive and eager to bond as a free electron can possibly be. That opens a shortcut to reactions and molecules that were previously locked behind chemistry's usual seniority rules, published in Nature. A parallel story out of Seoul National University, KAIST, and the Korea Basic Science Institute found something almost as strange happening inside solid oxide cells, the devices that either make electricity or split water into hydrogen depending on which way you run them: the same silver nanocatalyst was quietly switching which physical spot on its surface does the actual chemistry depending on the cell's mode. It's the kind of hidden mechanism that, once you see it, immediately suggests better catalyst designs for cheaper green hydrogen. Meanwhile a UCLA-led team showed that a decades-old recipe called alkaline thermal treatment can take mixed, unsorted PET, polyethylene, and polypropylene waste — the stuff that normally has to be painstakingly separated before recycling — and convert it directly into hydrogen fuel over 90% pure, at temperatures hundreds of degrees below conventional plastic gasification, while locking the carbon away as a solid mineral instead of releasing it. It's a genuinely elegant piece of chemistry, though Chemistry World's reporting on the recycling industry this month is a useful gut check: promising depolymerization processes keep running into the boring stuff, contaminated feedstock, no profitable facilities to run them at scale, and policy that hasn't caught up. On the materials side, Purdue engineers finally cracked a problem that's dogged intermetallic compounds forever: cobalt-aluminum is normally strong but snaps like a cracker, so the team restructured it at the nanoscale, packing in deliberate crystal defects and flexible amorphous boundaries, and ended up with a material six to ten times stronger than structural steel that can still bend significantly before it fails.

None of these are the kind of discovery you'll notice directly, but they add up. Better catalysts mean cheaper, cleaner ways to make the hydrogen and chemicals an economy runs on; better recycling chemistry means less of the plastic problem ends up in the ocean or a landfill; and a metal that's both stronger and less brittle than steel could eventually show up in everything from jet engines to your car. The plastic-to-hydrogen story is the one worth watching most closely — the lab chemistry keeps getting better while the actual recycling system it needs to plug into keeps struggling, and closing that gap is where the real breakthrough will happen.

Science Daily — Tuesday, August 11, 2026
Physics

This Week in Physics

Physics has a way of turning "wait, that shouldn't be possible" into a Tuesday headline, and this week delivered a nice batch of those moments — from light that can be reshaped in a trillionth of a second to dark matter behaving exactly backwards from what theorists expected.

Start with the dark matter surprise, published August 2nd: researchers modeling how dark matter clumps together found that if dark matter particles carry their own hidden "dark force" pulling on each other, it should actually suppress the growth of cosmic structure rather than accelerate it, the opposite of what most models assumed. That matters because astronomers use exactly this kind of clumping to test dark matter theories against real telescope surveys, so a flipped prediction changes which models survive contact with the data. Meanwhile, a French-German team announced in Nature on July 31st that they'd built the first true "photonic time crystal" — ordinary crystals repeat their structure in space, but this device rapidly flips its optical properties in time instead, letting it reshape terahertz light in trillionths of a second while cutting energy losses roughly in half. Think of it as a strobe light so precisely timed it can grab and reroute light on the fly, a trick that could eventually speed up fiber-optic communications or ultrafast imaging. On the materials side, a team reported on August 5th a new wafer-scale fabrication technique for quantum materials, clearing a manufacturing bottleneck that's kept many promising quantum devices stuck at lab-bench scale, and MIT researchers on August 7th unveiled a precise new way to track how heat moves through the many stacked layers inside a computer chip, the kind of unglamorous but essential measurement that chipmakers need as processors get smaller and hotter.

None of these are the kind of discovery that rewrites a textbook overnight, but together they're a reminder that physics right now is quietly solving the "plumbing" problems — how to build the materials, how to move the heat, how to control the light — that bigger breakthroughs down the line will depend on.
