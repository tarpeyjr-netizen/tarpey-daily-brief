Space

This Week in Space

Space science has a way of making the very large and the very small trade places. This week, astronomers were mapping the lopsided outline of a planet more than a thousand light-years away, while others were fretting over a mirror adjusted by a sliver of a hair’s width, and still others were wondering whether two black holes, each millions of times the mass of our Sun, are about to merge within a human lifetime. It is a good reminder that most of astronomy is patient measurement, and that every so often the measurements start to sound like a plot twist.

The planet is WASP-17b, a puffy “hot Jupiter” roughly 1,300 light-years away. A Cornell-led team, reporting in Nature Astronomy, used a technique they call transmission strings on James Webb Space Telescope data. Instead of assuming the planet’s atmosphere forms a neat circular ring around it, they measured how far the atmosphere extends at many points around the edge, a bit like measuring a balloon’s shape with a tape measure rather than assuming it is round. The result is a slightly egg-shaped atmosphere, more extended on the evening side, where winds sweep heat around from the scorching dayside. Nothing here contradicts earlier predictions; what is new is that we can now actually see the shape. Meanwhile, back at home, NASA reported that its Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope is beating expectations by nearly 30 percent after engineers nudged a mirror by a tiny amount, sharpening its view before it ever leaves the ground. And Webb also turned up an ancient galaxy cluster with an unexpectedly dense concentration of dark matter, the kind of finding that makes cosmologists reach for their models and squint.

The week’s most dramatic headline comes with a generous helping of caution. Researchers led by Silke Britzen at the Max Planck Institute for Radio Astronomy argue that the galaxy Markarian 501 hides two supermassive black holes orbiting each other every 121 days or so, separated by only a few hundred times the Earth–Sun distance. Depending on their masses, they could merge in as little as a century. The case rests on the odd behavior of the galaxy’s jets, and the pair cannot be seen separately, so it is not a conclusive detection; confirmation would most likely come from the faint, low-frequency gravitational waves that pulsar timing arrays are built to hear. On the Moon, the long-running puzzle of why its two faces look so different continues to be chipped away at by samples from the far side, which point to a cooler far-side interior and a more complicated volcanic history, though no single agreed-upon answer has emerged.

So what should a curious non-scientist take from all this? That the universe is being measured more precisely than ever, from the shape of a distant atmosphere to the focus of a telescope not yet in orbit, and that the boldest claims are the ones to hold loosely until a second line of evidence arrives. If you want something to do rather than read, the Orionid meteor shower peaks overnight on October 21–22, and there is a citizen-science project looking for volunteers to hunt for “missing” black holes in the Milky Way in NASA telescope data. Both require nothing more than curiosity.

Sunday Long Read

Fifty-Seven New Animals in Two Weeks

Marine Biology

In the summer of 2025, a research ship called Falkor (too) sat over a gash in the seafloor off Argentina and lowered a yellow robot into the dark. The robot, an underwater vehicle named SuBastian, carried cameras that streamed everything it saw to the internet, and more than four million people ended up watching. What they saw was a place that most of us would struggle to imagine: the Mar del Plata Canyon, a submarine valley plunging more than 3,500 metres, nearly twice the depth of the Grand Canyon, with a landscape of mud slopes, rock walls and drifting snow that is not snow at all.

That "snow" is the clue to why the canyon is so alive. Where the ocean floor is this deep, sunlight never arrives, so almost everything down there eats what falls from above: dead plankton, fish scraps, flecks of organic debris sinking in slow, endless flurries. The Mar del Plata Canyon sits beneath one of the planet's most dramatic ocean collisions, the Brazil-Malvinas Confluence, where warm subtropical water pushed south meets cold, nutrient-rich water pushed north from the sub-Antarctic. The meeting churns up food, and a great deal of it rains downward. A canyon beneath that kind of feast, scientists suspected, might hold a menagerie that no one had properly catalogued.

They were right, but finding an animal and knowing what it is are two very different things. Over the following months, the expedition's samples sat waiting for the slow, painstaking work of taxonomy, the discipline of deciding whether a creature is already known or genuinely new. Taxonomy has a staffing problem. For many deep-sea groups there are only a handful of living specialists in the world, and each one may spend years on a single batch of specimens. So in July 2026 the organisers tried something different. The Nippon Foundation-Nekton Ocean Census, working with the Schmidt Ocean Institute, gathered about twenty taxonomists and early-career researchers from Latin America and Europe in one room for a species discovery workshop, with the specimens in front of them, and let them work side by side.

The results, announced this month, were startling. In roughly two weeks the group described 57 new species and five new genera. Twenty were copepods, tiny crustaceans that look like a grain of rice with antennae and form a huge part of the ocean's food web. Twenty-two were echinoderms, the family of sea cucumbers, sea stars and sea lilies. Fourteen were cnidarians, the group that includes corals and sea pens, one of which is a feathery, quill-shaped animal that stands up from the mud like a plume. One was a mollusc. The copepods alone tell you how little we know: the team examined only about a tenth of the copepod samples, and every single specimen they checked turned out to be new to science.

Some of the finds have the kind of personality that makes a headline. One sea cucumber, a member of the genus Peniagone, has earned the nickname Loki because of the horn-like appendages that sprout from its body, a reference to the Norse trickster god. Sea cucumbers in this group are strange, translucent animals that drift and glide just above the seabed rather than crawling along it, and they look more like something dreamed up by a costume designer than a creature that eats mud. There was also a bright purple sea cucumber photographed on the canyon floor, and a red-orange coral spotted at nearly two thousand metres.

It would be easy to treat this as a pure curiosity, a list of odd names. But the deeper story is about speed and about who gets to do the science. Michelle Taylor, the Ocean Census head of science, put it plainly when she said that describing so many species in two weeks shows the power of collaborative taxonomy. Traditionally, specimens from a southern hemisphere expedition might be shipped to museums in the north and described there, years later, by researchers who never saw the canyon. This workshop kept the work close to home, trained young Latin American scientists, and will house the specimens permanently in national collections, including the natural science museum in Buenos Aires. The data went onto an open-access platform so that anyone can check the work.

There is also a quiet urgency under all of it. We are starting to push industry into the deep ocean, from fishing gear to the prospect of seabed mining elsewhere, and you cannot protect or even measure the damage to a community you have never named. Estimates suggest that most deep-sea species have never been described at all. Every canyon surveyed so far seems to add a long list to that ledger rather than shortening the unknown.

The team is already planning to return, with a follow-up expedition in early 2027 to a different canyon that is suspected to hold species found nowhere else. It is humbling that a canyon off a major coastline can still hand us dozens of animals nobody has ever seen. The map of life on Earth, it turns out, still has a great deal of dark space on it, and some of it is filled with horned, drifting, gloriously strange sea cucumbers.

Science Daily — Saturday, October 17, 2026
Neuroscience

This Week in Neuroscience

The brain is the one organ that spends its whole life trying to understand itself, and neuroscience is the strange discipline that results: a field where the instrument and the thing being measured are made of the same stuff. This week the field's attention was split between celebrating how far its tools have come and quietly poking at questions it still cannot answer.

The celebration came from Stockholm. The 2026 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine went to Karl Deisseroth, Peter Hegemann and Georg Nagel for optogenetics, the trick of making chosen neurons respond to light. The idea began with a humble protein from algae, channelrhodopsin, which the algae use to find sunlight; install it in a mouse neuron and you can switch that cell on with a flash, like fitting one lightbulb in a vast city grid with its own private dimmer. That is a dozen days old now, so it falls just outside our usual window, but it frames everything else. Fresh reports in the journals this week used the same spirit of poking circuits to see what they do: one group described stimulating groups of motor-cortex neurons in mice to evoke learned movement patterns, hinting that this region actively writes skilled movement rather than merely relaying orders, while another traced a fruit-fly circuit that generates the slow, rolling brain waves of deep sleep. Elsewhere, researchers reported that human claustrum neurons, a thin and mysterious sheet of cells deep in the brain, track uncertainty and prediction errors while we learn to anticipate something unpleasant. I am relying on journal summaries here rather than the full papers, so treat the details as headlines, not verdicts.

So what does a curious non-scientist take from all this? That brain science is increasingly about causation rather than correlation: not just noticing which cells light up during a behavior, but flipping them on and off to find out who is really in charge. It is slow, patient work, and the week's news is a good reminder that even the old debates, such as whether adults grow new neurons at all, are still being argued over in the pages of Nature. The brain remains the last great unexplored continent, and we are finally building decent boats.

Science Daily — Friday, October 16, 2026
Epidemiology

This Week in Epidemiology

Epidemiology is the detective work of public health: counting who gets sick, where and why, so that officials can step in before a few cases become thousands. This week the counting was dominated by an old enemy, measles, which is proving that a disease we have the tools to stop can still come roaring back when vaccination slips.

In the United States the CDC had logged 4,080 confirmed measles cases as of October 8, and New York declared a statewide disaster emergency on October 5. Numbers differ depending on who is counting, which is itself a lesson: trackers update on different schedules and use different definitions. Overseas, Bangladesh's outbreak, which began in March, is far larger. One tracker puts it above 200,000 total cases, while the national figures cited on Wikipedia were lower as of September, though still in the hundreds of thousands once suspected cases are counted. Measles is so contagious that, in a community with low immunity, one case can infect most of the unprotected people it meets, like a match dropped in dry grass. Meanwhile, cholera is surging in northeast Nigeria, where Borno State had reported more than 68,000 suspected cases since May, and the European disease agency counted about 46,000 new cases worldwide in a recent month, as covered in its week 40 threats report.

There was good news too. A French study reports that hepatitis C among people who use drugs fell substantially between 2013 and 2023, which the authors credit to wide treatment and harm-reduction programs, and Scotland's latest respiratory virus report shows flu positivity easing after an earlier-than-expected rise. A separate Oregon study suggests that testing wastewater for measles virus could serve as an early-warning system, though the authors caution the samples were not sequenced. The takeaway: the same patient, careful counting that reveals a growing outbreak also shows where public health work is paying off.

Science Daily — Thursday, October 15, 2026
Mathematics

This Week in Mathematics

Mathematics has an odd relationship with news. A proof can take a decade to be believed, and the moment a result becomes “true” is usually a slow social process: someone posts a manuscript, specialists poke at it for months, and eventually the field shrugs and says yes, that holds. So the headline-sized events in math tend to be either prizes, which are quick, or arguments about how we decide something has been proved, which are slower and more interesting.

This week the second kind dominated. Reports say OpenAI has put a large batch of machine-generated mathematical manuscripts into a public repository, the product of an internal model that was pointed at thousands of problems. The coverage we could find is uneven, and some of it is secondary and unreliable, so treat the specifics with care. The most consistent account is that only a minority of the results have been formally machine-checked in Lean, a proof-checking language that works like a pedantic referee who never gets tired, and that human experts have so far endorsed only a first few. Think of it as a mountain of freshly printed maps: some have been walked and confirmed, most are still waiting for someone to try the trail.

Alongside it, the human side of the field is still glowing from the summer. Hong Wang, whose work on a geometric problem about how thin lines can fill space drew the “once in a century” description, is now being profiled after her Fields Medal, the third woman to win one. It is a useful counterweight: the same week that raises the question of whether machines will do the proving, the field celebrates a proof that took deep human intuition about how shapes behave.

So what for the curious non-scientist? Not much has been settled, and that is the point. The real question is not whether an AI can emit a plausible proof, but whether we can build the habit of checking at the speed it writes. Formal verification may end up being the quiet hero of this story. Expect the next few months to be about referees, not breakthroughs.

Note: a quiet week for confirmed new theorems; coverage of the AI manuscript release was thin and partly unverified at press time.

Science Daily — Wednesday, October 14, 2026
Robotics & Engineering

This Week in Robotics & Engineering

For decades the dream of a dexterous robot has been chased mostly with software: smarter planning, better vision, more data. But a quiet theme running through this week's robotics news is that the body matters as much as the brain. A machine built from the right stuff, with joints that give a little and muscles that respond quickly, can do things that no amount of clever code will coax out of stiff, heavy hardware.

The clearest example comes from the Robotics and AI Institute, whose two-armed robot AthenaZero was described in Science Robotics. It throws a tennis ball at more than 30 metres per second, catches balls fired back at it, and managed to connect with a bat in 27 of 33 swings. The trick is in the arms: they are light, with motors that connect almost directly to the joints, so the limbs have very little inertia to fight against. Think of the difference between swinging a tennis racket and swinging a fence post. Lighter arms can speed up, slow down and react in a heartbeat. The caveat is that the tests were run in a lab, with two robots standing about seven metres apart, so this is a long way from a ballpark.

Elsewhere, the same body-first thinking showed up in softer forms. Engineers reported a beetle-inspired soft robot steered by the bend of a user’s finger, a fish-like swimmer driven by electrohydraulic muscles, a feather-star-inspired underwater robot that moves through three dimensions with only two actuators, and a thread-like motor that moves flexible devices by sliding fibres past each other with no gears at all. At the humbler, more playful end, UCLA researchers posted a preprint on PhoneBot, a walking robot of about 48 centimetres that uses an old Android phone as its eyes and brain, for roughly $400 in hardware plus a phone that might otherwise be in a drawer. It is a preprint, so it has not yet been peer reviewed, but the idea is charming: the sensors and processor in a cheap old phone are already better than what many robots carry.

So what for the rest of us? Robots that are light, gentle and inexpensive are the ones most likely to leave factory cages and show up in homes, hospitals and disaster zones, where bumping into a person should not be a catastrophe. This week was less about one dramatic breakthrough and more about a steady shift in philosophy: build the machine so that physics does part of the thinking.

Science Daily — Tuesday, October 13, 2026
Oceanography

This Week in Oceanography

The ocean is a strange place to do science: most of it is dark, cold, under crushing pressure and out of reach, so researchers learn about it the way you might learn about a house by listening through the walls. Satellites read the skin of the sea, floats and ships sample it in thin slices, and every so often a robot brings back a piece of the bottom and something unexpected happens. This week had a bit of all three, from the vast scale of a Pacific climate swing down to two lumps of rock behaving badly on a lab bench.

Start with the big one. On October 8 the World Meteorological Organization said that El Niño, the periodic warming of the central and eastern tropical Pacific, is “firmly established and strengthening rapidly.” The key gauge, the Niño 3.4 temperature anomaly, climbed from about 1.6°C above normal in June to 2.5°C in August, and the forecast for October through December is around 3.7°C, which would beat the previous record of 2.6°C set in the winter of 2015–16. Think of the Pacific as a giant bathtub that occasionally sloshes its warm water eastward and rewrites weather on several continents. Forecasters say it is near-certain to last through February 2027, with a peak around December, and that August 2026 saw the warmest global sea-surface temperature ever observed.

Heat also featured in a study from the Virginia Institute of Marine Science, reported this month from work in Communications Earth & Environment. The team examined more than 2,580 marine heatwaves at 20 U.S. estuaries and found that the usual way of counting them, which looks only at the days above a threshold, misses the warm water that comes before and after, undercounting total heat exposure by more than 150 percent on average. It is like judging a fever by only the hours the thermometer read above 103. A third of the events were “compound” ones with about 90 days of prolonged warming and more than triple the accumulated heat. The authors point out that lab experiments often warm organisms for only days or weeks, so past estimates of how well estuary life copes may have been too optimistic. A related coral story, covered on October 12, describes a Florida Keys trial in Frontiers in Marine Science in which floating shades over 20 brain-coral colonies helped them regain color during heat stress, offered by the researchers as a triage tool rather than a cure.

And then the fire. In Scientific Reports, U.S. Geological Survey researchers describe how two of 57 hydrothermal samples collected from the Escanaba Trough, off the Oregon–California border, spontaneously ignited when freeze-dried and crushed in the lab, with temperatures passing 100°C. Both were rich in nanocrystalline marcasite, an iron sulfide that reacts eagerly with oxygen, much as a pile of oily rags can heat itself in a garage. Not every such rock will do this, but the finding is a flag for any future deep-sea mining of these sulfide deposits, since ore hauled onto a ship or a dock would suddenly meet plenty of air.

The common thread is that the ocean keeps surprising us when we measure it more carefully. A hotter sea does more than raise a number: it lingers, compounds and reshapes weather, and the seafloor holds materials that behave very differently once we pull them up. For a curious non-scientist, the practical takeaway is that this winter’s storms, floods and droughts will likely carry the fingerprint of that Pacific warmth, and that careful measurement, boring as it sounds, is how we find the hazards before they find us.

Batteries & Electricity

This Week in Batteries & Electricity

A battery is a deceptively humble object: two materials that each want to trade electrons, kept politely apart so the trade happens through a wire where we can use it. Almost every improvement in the field comes down to finding better partners for that trade, or better chaperones to keep them from trading too fast, too hot or in the wrong place. It is slow, stubborn work, and it rarely follows a news cycle.

Quiet week for Batteries & Electricity — my searches found no clearly dated study or discovery from the past seven days (or the past fourteen) that I could confirm, only older material and general market chatter. The last notable development I could verify was a September 14 report that Lava Blue commissioned Australia’s first grid-connected sodium-sulfur battery, a chemistry that swaps scarce lithium for cheap, abundant sodium and sulfur.

That is a good story to carry into the week. Sodium-sulfur cells have long been loved for grid storage and held back by the heat and engineering they demand, a bit like a brilliant but temperamental chef. Seeing one wired into a real grid is the kind of unglamorous milestone that, over years, changes what the lights cost to keep on. Check back as the week unfolds; the lab results tend to arrive in bursts.
