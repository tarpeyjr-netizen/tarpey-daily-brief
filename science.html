Mathematics

This Week in Mathematics

Mathematics doesn't often make headlines for drama — a proof is either correct or it isn't, and the field has always prided itself on settling arguments with logic rather than shouting. This week, though, the discipline found itself in the middle of a genuine identity crisis, and the trigger was a claim almost too big to slot quietly into a journal: that a piece of software, not a person, had cracked one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems, the million-dollar questions that have humbled the best mathematicians alive for a quarter century.

The claim came from OpenAI, which announced on September 8 that an internal model, directing roughly 10,000 autonomous AI agents working in parallel, had found a genuine "blowup" — a point where the equations stop behaving — in the Navier-Stokes equations, the notoriously stubborn system that describes how fluids like water and air actually flow. It's the kind of result that, if it holds up, resolves a problem the Clay Mathematics Institute has offered a million dollars for since 2000. But the 88-hour timeline raised eyebrows immediately, not least because NYU mathematician Tristan Buckmaster says OpenAI began chasing the same question shortly after learning he was pursuing it with Anthropic researcher Levent Alpöge — the mathematical equivalent of a much bigger, much faster competitor overhearing your idea in the hallway and beating you to the finish line with sheer brute force.

What followed was less a debate about the proof itself and more a referendum on what mathematics is for. Caltech's planned "Mathathon," which would have handed teams $20,000 in AI tokens and 40 hours to attack open problems, collapsed under an open letter signed by 771 mathematicians warning it risked flooding the field with unverified "slop mathematics," while a separate letter from 25 Fields Medalists cautioned that rushed AI announcements — arriving before proper writeups, citations, or peer scrutiny — make it nearly impossible to credit the humans quietly working the same territory. OpenAI pulled its sponsorship on September 10; Anthropic, notably, did not. Out of the same week's unease came a new grassroots group, the Association for Human Mathematics, and a widely shared post on Terence Tao's blog, co-written by philosophers Silvia De Toffoli and Eamon Duede, arguing that a solved problem is only one of mathematics' many products — that the field also exists to build theories, train the next generation, and produce something close to art, none of which a chess-playing engine for theorems can hand you no matter how many problems it clears.

For a non-mathematician, the value of a proof has always seemed obvious: either the bridge stands or it doesn't. What this week made clear is that the mathematical community cares just as much about how a result is reached and who gets to understand it as it does about whether it's true — because a discipline built on training human minds to think clearly doesn't automatically benefit from a black box that occasionally spits out the right answer. Watch this space less for the next AI headline and more for whether the field can agree on rules for crediting and verifying what these systems produce, because that fight, not the Navier-Stokes equations, is the one still wide open.

Science Daily — Wednesday, September 16, 2026
Robotics & Engineering

This Week in Robotics & Engineering

Robotics is a field where the interesting question is almost never whether a machine can do a trick once in a lab. It's whether the trick still works on the ten-thousandth try, on a real factory floor, for less money than paying a person to do it. That's the question this week's news kept circling back to, from a factory in southern China that builds humanoid robots with the help of humanoid robots, to a Wall Street bank running the actual arithmetic on what a robot costs per hour versus a human, to a funding round for robots whose entire job is scraping rust off the bottom of ships. None of it is flashy the way a backflipping robot is flashy. All of it is closer to the truth of where robotics actually stands right now.

Start with the factory that eats its own cooking. On September 15, the Chinese firm UBTech opened what it calls the world's first smart production base built specifically for humanoid robots, in Liuzhou — a plant where humanoid arms help assemble more humanoids, turning out a finished Walker S or Cruzr unit roughly every ten minutes, with capacity for 10,000 a year. It's a small, satisfying loop: the machines meant to work in factories are themselves being built inside a factory optimized around having some of them do the building. That same week, JPMorgan published estimates putting a humanoid robot's operating cost at roughly $10 to $12 an hour against about $30 for comparable human factory labor — though the bank's own analysts flag that the robots are still considerably less productive today, the unglamorous asterisk that sits under every big robot-economics headline. The clearest real-world test of that math is already running: Figure's robots have spent recent months on an actual BMW assembly line, contributing to production of more than 30,000 X3 vehicles and placing over 90,000 sheet-metal parts into welding fixtures with 5-millimeter precision in under two seconds per part — not a demo reel, but a shift schedule. Boston Dynamics, meanwhile, is shipping production Atlas units to Hyundai's Robotics Metaplant and to Google DeepMind, a relationship made simpler by the fact that Hyundai owns Boston Dynamics outright, so the robot's roadmap and the car company's factory needs already point the same direction.

If the body of the robot is increasingly a manufacturing and cost-accounting problem, the brain is still very much a research one. Benchmarks published this week gave OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra direct control of a pair of robot arms and set it against Anthropic's Claude Fable models on two pick-and-place tasks. On the easy version — put a red block in a bowl — Astra nailed 19 of 20 attempts, comfortably ahead of Fable 5.1's 8 and Fable 5's 1. Hand the same models a harder task, though, fitting a round puzzle piece into a matching groove, and Astra and Fable 5.1 both fell to 2 out of 20 — a reminder that today's AI models are a bit like a toddler who can confidently grab a cup off a table but hasn't yet worked out how to thread a shoelace through an eyelet. The gap between those two numbers is, roughly, the gap between a robot you'd trust to tidy a room and one you'd trust with anything requiring real precision. And the week's least glamorous story might be its most telling: a startup called RobotPlusPlus closed a large funding round on September 15 to expand its fleet of robots that crawl the rusting hulls of ships in dry dock — work that's dirty, dangerous and tedious for people — already deployed at more than 100 shipyards and running five to six times faster than a human crew at a fraction of the cost.

Add it up and the story of robotics this week wasn't really about any single breakthrough machine — it was about robotics quietly turning into a labor market. There's a plant that manufactures the manufacturers, a bank running spreadsheets on robot wages, a car company already putting robots on the clock, and a hull-scraping robot fleet that will never make a magazine cover but that shipyards are paying real money for anyway. For a curious non-scientist watching from the outside, the sign to watch isn't the next viral robot video — it's stories like these: funding rounds, cost-per-hour estimates, factories building factories. That's what it looks like when a technology stops being a curiosity and starts being infrastructure.
