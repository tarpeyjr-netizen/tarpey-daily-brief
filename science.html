Neuroscience

This Week in Neuroscience

Neuroscience runs on tidy categories. A neuron is “a face cell” or “a place cell,” a child “has ADHD” or “has anxiety,” and an AI model that predicts brain activity is assumed to work the way the brain does. The brain keeps refusing to cooperate with those labels, and this week several studies showed it plainly. The most basic one concerned the neuron itself. A Columbia Zuckerman Institute team led by Stefano Fusi and Lorenzo Posani, writing in Nature, analyzed International Brain Laboratory recordings of single cells across 43 regions of the mouse cortex. Outside the primary sensory areas, very few neurons were narrow specialists. Most were generalists, each responding to many kinds of information in a pattern that rarely matched any other cell’s. Of the classic one-job neurons in textbooks, Fusi said, “We’re saying they are the exceptions. They’re not the rule.”

If single cells don’t sort neatly, it is less surprising that diagnoses don’t either. At Stanford Medicine, Yu Zhang’s group, with lead author Yong Jiao of the University of Iowa, applied an AI model to brain scans of thousands of children in the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development study. They looked at how brain regions coordinate with one another and reported in Nature Mental Health three distinct “biotypes”: externalizing-disinhibited, internalizing-distressed, and a mixed type. The biotypes don’t line up with the labels ADHD and anxiety, since every combination of biotype and diagnosis turned up. The patterns were also already visible in scans of 693 children who were only diagnosed later. Compare it to sorting books by the inside of the pages instead of the cover. Meanwhile, York University’s Sabine Muzellec and Kohitij Kar ran the usual AI-versus-brain comparison in reverse in Nature Machine Intelligence. Vision models predicted recorded neuron responses well, but the neurons predicted many of the models’ internal features poorly. In other words, two systems can produce the same answers with different machinery.

Two animal studies looked at sleep and memory. At the University of Basel, Alex Schier’s lab reported in Nature that two groups of brainstem neurons, one GABAergic and one serotonergic, grow more active the longer a mouse stays awake. They behave like a fuel gauge for sleep pressure that steadily falls toward empty. Turning them up produced deep recovery-style sleep. Suppressing both groups cut sleep by roughly 70 percent, and most of those mice avoided the serious behavioral problems that sleep loss usually causes. In Sydney, Greg Neely’s team described nasal drops in Advanced Science that use the lipid-nanoparticle mRNA approach of COVID vaccines to make brain cells produce BDNF, a growth factor that helps neurons survive. Aged mice that received the drops did about as well as young mice on memory tests. Mice engineered to develop Alzheimer’s-like disease showed no further cognitive decline by four months, even though their amyloid plaques did not shrink. The researchers themselves caution that “human noses are different from mouse noses.”

So what does this mean if you aren’t a neuroscientist? It means many of our labels for the brain describe what it does more than how it does it. Neurons that multitask, childhood biotypes that cut across diagnoses, and AI models that match the brain’s outputs without matching its mechanism all point to the same lesson: similar behavior can come from very different machinery. That has practical consequences. Two children with the same diagnosis might need different help, and a drug that protects neurons might work without ever clearing the plaques that drug developers have targeted for decades. The sleep and nasal-drop results come from mice and are a long way from the clinic. Still, they show researchers asking a different kind of question, about which wiring underlies a condition rather than what to call it.

Epidemiology

This Week in Epidemiology

Epidemiology is detective work done at the scale of whole populations. Nobody can watch a virus jump from one person to the next, so the field reconstructs the chain afterward from the clues it leaves: who fell ill, where, when, and which strain they carried. This week brought a reminder of how much those clues can reveal, and of what happens when they come in too late. Six months into the Bundibugyo Ebola outbreak in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, CIDRAP reported 11,793 confirmed cases and 5,779 deaths as of last weekend, which means roughly one in two confirmed patients has died. Africa CDC says weekly notifications have held at about 460 to 480 confirmed cases since late September. The most telling figure is about contact tracing: only 11% of confirmed cases came from people already on a contact list, against a target of 95%. Most new patients, in other words, appear from nowhere the response can see. Heavy fighting near a hospital in Ituri on November 21 made the work harder, and WHO has counted 87 attacks on health workers and facilities in the country this year.

Elsewhere, researchers showed how a virus's genetic fingerprints can stand in for the interviews that tracers never get to do. A Broad Institute-led team writing in Cell analyzed more than 85,000 SARS-CoV-2 genomes from 666 Massachusetts sites, including schools, colleges and nursing homes, collected between late 2021 and early 2023. Closely related viruses cluster the way gossip does inside a friend group, so the team could see where spread was happening locally. Nursing homes and colleges stood out, while primary and secondary schools added little extra risk. On campuses, the Omicron subvariants BA.1, BA.2 and BA.2.12.1 reached majority frequency 4 to 13 days earlier than elsewhere. The authors suggest that young adults could act as an early warning system for genomic surveillance, much as canaries once warned miners. A JAMA Network Open study led by a University of Washington researcher made a related point about measles. Across counties covering 237 million Americans, higher rates of nonmedical vaccine exemptions among kindergarteners were tied to 16% higher odds of an outbreak, while overall MMR coverage figures showed no such link. A county average can look comfortable and still hide small, tightly clustered pockets of unprotected children, like dry brush scattered through a green forest. The real-world cost is visible: CIDRAP reported that new deaths in Virginia and Pennsylvania bring the US measles toll to 13, and Wisconsin confirmed its third measles-related death, with 920 cases so far in 2026.

The widest lens of the week was trained on dengue. A team led by the London School of Hygiene and Tropical Medicine presented work at the American Society of Tropical Medicine and Hygiene meeting that pieced together surveillance data from 143 countries covering 1990 to 2024. They found that reported dengue incidence has risen 34-fold, and they tallied about 74 million cases, 10.7 million of which never made it into WHO databases. Growth was fastest in places where the disease is new, in Africa, the Middle East and South Asia. Outbreaks have also roughly doubled in cyclical strength and are increasingly rising and falling in step around the globe, like ripples from separate stones merging into one wave. Closer to home, CIDRAP noted that H5N1 bird flu activity is rising across North America, and a large zoo in Mexico has closed because of the virus.

So what does this mean for someone who never reads a surveillance report? Each of these stories is about seeing what an average conceals. In Congo, most chains of infection are invisible until a new patient arrives at a clinic. In Massachusetts, viral genomes showed that a few settings did much of the spreading. In American counties, a reassuring overall vaccination rate hid the clusters where measles takes hold. And for dengue, thousands of scattered national reports added up to a single global trend that is growing fast. Epidemiology rarely makes headlines for a cure. Its contribution is knowing where to look, and this week showed that where we look, and how quickly, can matter as much as any vaccine.

Mathematics

This Week in Mathematics

For more than a century and a half, the Riemann hypothesis has been the Everest of mathematics. Bernhard Riemann first wrote it down in 1859. It is a statement about where a strange function, the zeta function, equals zero, and it turns out to control how the prime numbers are scattered along the number line. Prove it and you have tamed the most unruly sequence in arithmetic. Last Friday, Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic, announced that he and the company's Claude model had done exactly that, and had also proved the broader “generalized” version of the problem. The odd part is how it was delivered. There was no elegant paper. There was a four-page note and a proof written in Lean, a programming language in which a computer checks every logical step. Alpöge says he does not yet fully understand all of the mathematics himself.

The scale is hard to picture. As The Conversation reported, the formal proof runs to more than 17 million lines of code, and Alpöge's attempts at an English explanation passed 250,000 pages. Imagine being handed a cathedral that a machine has inspected brick by brick and declared sound, when no architect can yet explain why it stands. The Lean Focused Research Organization and the Mathlib community said they support the proof's correctness, and the mathematician Daniel Litt summed up the mood: the proof is “exceedingly likely to be correct,” but “no human understands it yet.” Experts still have one worry. Lean can only confirm that the code proves the statement that was typed in, so humans must check that this statement really is Riemann's hypothesis. Riemann specialist Brian Conrey called it “a monumental moment in human history” and said he expects simpler routes to exist. In a twist, a separate 940-page proof written entirely by a human, from Stanford's Alessandro Maria Masullo, appeared at almost the same time, and readers reported possible problems with it within hours. Quanta Magazine's weekly column on math and AI asked the obvious follow-up: who counts as the author when a machine does much of the work? It notes that thousands of mathematicians have signed the Leiden Declaration, which says AI use should be disclosed and credit should stay with humans, while different AI companies are now crediting their results in very different ways.

Not every result this week was so contested. A team from Cornell, Imperial College London and NYU's Courant Institute proved a neat “zero-one law” in PNAS about learning the equations behind a physical system from a single experiment. For a broad class of models, if any one experiment can reveal the system's hidden parameters, then almost any randomly chosen experiment will too. The co-author Samuel Otto compares the failures to the thin wires between the sections of a dartboard: in principle they exist, but a thrown dart will never land on one. That matters for tests that are expensive, destructive or impossible to repeat, such as working out how a bridge flexes under a passing train. There is a catch. The result is theoretical, and it assumes perfect, noise-free measurements, so the team's next job is to work out how much real-world noise widens those wires.

So what should the rest of us make of all this? The mathematician David Manderscheid, writing this week, argues that the field's real work is moving from solving to “digesting”: taking proofs that machines produce and turning them into understanding that people can use. “If you don't know why it works,” he writes, “then you can't apply the math.” Even if the 17 million lines hold up, the most important step is still to come. Someone has to tell the story of why the primes behave as they do, and for now that is still a job for people.

Robotics & Engineering

This Week in Robotics & Engineering

Most robots still struggle to be small, fast and self-aware all at once. The smaller you build a machine, the less room you have for batteries, chips and sensors. The faster you make it, the harder it is to control. And however clever its software, a robot rarely knows when it is out of its depth. This week's research chipped away at all three problems, and some of the best ideas came from leaving things out: fewer electronics, fewer motors, and a robot willing to admit it is stuck.

Start with speed. At UC San Diego, Yichen Zhai and Michael Tolley built a six-legged soft robot that runs at up to 4.72 meters per second without a single electronic component, as reported in Science Robotics. It is printed in one piece from a single material and runs on compressed air. Its trick comes from the Victorian compound steam engine: the air works twice, first driving the valves at high pressure and then moving the legs at low pressure. That made it more than a hundred times faster than the team's 2022 robot, which managed about four centimeters per second. With no circuits to fry, a machine like this could one day scout high-radiation zones or strong magnetic fields, although for now it still trails an air hose. MIT's Kevin Chen and colleagues tackled the opposite problem: how to fit a brain into something tiny. Their insect-scale flying robot, also described in Science Robotics, weighs less than a paper clip yet carries its own sensors and computer. A 244-milligram package holds a motion sensor, an optical-flow camera, a height sensor and a microcontroller. That lets it hover and follow paths to within a few centimeters without the room-sized motion-capture rigs such robots usually need. In one demonstration it landed on a sunflower.

Other teams went for flexibility. A European group led by Etor Arza posted a preprint, not yet peer-reviewed, on a drone whose arms fold when they hit the edges of a gap, shrinking its width by 55 percent in mid-flight. Spring-loaded cam joints hold the arms rigid until they bump the frame, and then they give way, much as a cat's whiskers and shoulders let it slip through a fence. The drone passed through a 15-centimeter slot at about two meters per second. At Sandia National Laboratories, Steve Buerger's team built SPIINE, a six-inch crawler for the aging waste pipes at the Hanford nuclear site, some of which are only two inches across. Its bendy body and stretchy wheels let the pipe walls steer it around tight 90-degree corners that stopped older push-cameras about ten feet in, and operators drive it with an ordinary video game controller. And at the Technical University of Munich, Ralf Römer and Angela Schoellig gave a robot something like humility. Their "self-reflective" vision-language-action model checks whether it can actually do a task, and if it isn't sure, it stops and asks a human for help. It then stores each new skill in a separate module so it doesn't overwrite what it already knows.

Why should you care? Robots are leaving the factory and heading into places that are cramped, dangerous or unpredictable: inside nuclear pipes, through collapsed buildings, past the flowers in your garden, and eventually into homes and workplaces next to people. Getting there will take more than bigger AI models. It also takes good mechanical design, like air circuits that replace chips or joints that bend on contact, along with robots that know their own limits. Most of these machines are still lab prototypes that need tethers and more field testing.

Oceanography

This Week in Oceanography

We tend to picture the ocean as a place, a vast blue volume of water with things living in it. Oceanographers see it more like a set of plumbing and wiring that the whole planet runs on. Nutrients rise from the deep, gases leak out into the sky, dust and smoke rain back down, and the animals at the top of the food web ride those currents for a living. This week's research was full of reminders of how tightly those pipes connect, and of how fast the consequences show up when one of them clogs.

The most vivid example came from Panama. Every dry season, trade winds push surface water offshore in the Gulf of Panama, and cold, nutrient-rich water wells up from below to feed a burst of plankton, which in turn feeds the anchoveta that support the country's fishmeal and fish-oil industry. In 2025 that upwelling failed for the first time on record. A team from the Smithsonian Tropical Research Institute, writing in PNAS, traced what happened next: plankton production in February 2025 fell by almost two-thirds, the lowest in 32 years of measurements; anchoveta landings dropped to 11% of normal, the lowest in sixty years; the fish that were caught had about half their usual fat; and Panama's fish-oil exports fell 88% that year. It is the marine equivalent of a farm losing its rain for a single season, and the whole business going dry within months. The upwelling returned in 2026 and catches recovered, but with a strong El Niño now underway, the researchers warn that warm, nutrient-poor water could reach the coast again in 2027. Meanwhile, two other studies looked at the ocean's exchanges with the air above it. In Science, a team co-led by the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology analyzed 418 aerosol samples from 21 research cruises and estimated that organic nitrogen falling out of the atmosphere delivers about 12.8 million tonnes of nutrient to the oceans every year, roughly 27% of all nitrogen deposition. Around 70% of it comes from land, with biomass burning the biggest single source, so wildfire smoke turns out to be fertilizing distant seas. And from a small island in the Baltic, University of Copenhagen researchers reported in Nature Communications that they tracked the exchange of 73 different gases between water and air over 51 days, about ten times as many compounds as most previous studies, and watched the mix shift sharply with the seasons. Emissions of dimethyl sulphide, a gas that helps seed clouds, collapsed to almost nothing as the water cooled and cyanobacteria faded.

Higher up the food chain, the week brought two lessons about how hard it is to manage animals whose lives play out across huge, interconnected waters. A study in Biological Conservation, co-led by France's CNRS, the Centre Scientifique de Monaco and Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution, satellite-tracked 48 young emperor penguins from three Weddell Sea colonies through their first year at sea. The birds followed remarkably repeatable routes of more than 1,500 kilometers, riding the advance and retreat of the sea ice, but they spent only 15 to 32% of that time inside proposed marine protected areas, and juveniles from one colony spent more than 60% of their time in recently fished waters in February and March, where trawlers target krill and icefish. Because the routes are predictable year after year, the authors argue protected areas drawn around them would keep working. Off the US West Coast, meanwhile, a modeling study in Science led by Dylan Gomes of Oregon State University and NOAA Fisheries tested a popular idea for rescuing Chinook and coho salmon: cull the seals, sea lions and seabirds that eat them. The model says it would do little, because those same predators also eat the fish that compete with or prey on young salmon. Remove the predator and its other prey fills the gap, a bit like firing a goalkeeper and discovering he was also the team's best defender.

The so-what for the rest of us is that the ocean rarely responds to one lever at a time. A missed season of wind in Central America can wipe out a national export within months; smoke from a forest fire can end up feeding plankton half a world away; a seemingly simple fix like culling sea lions can be cancelled out by the food web's own bookkeeping. The encouraging part is that scientists are getting much better at seeing these connections: dozens of gases instead of one, hundreds of aerosol samples from pole to pole, penguins wearing satellite tags. The sobering part is what they keep finding, which is that the systems we depend on are both more connected and quicker to shift than we tend to assume, and with a strong El Niño building, the coming year should put that understanding to the test.

Batteries & Electricity

This Week in Batteries & Electricity

We think of electricity as something that simply arrives when we flip a switch, but every watt you use is the end of a long and fiddly chain: chemistry inside a cell, electrons moving through wires, and a continent-sized grid that has to stay balanced second by second. It was a fairly quiet seven days for headline battery papers, so this roundup reaches back two weeks. That longer view turned up something nice: researchers working at every link in that chain, from a vitamin in your medicine cabinet to a mysterious heartbeat in the power grid itself.

Start with the strangest ingredient. A physical chemistry group at Spain's University of Córdoba built a rechargeable zinc-air battery that uses vitamin B12 as its catalyst, reporting the work in the Journal of Materials Chemistry A. Zinc-air cells, already familiar from hearing aids, breathe oxygen from the surrounding air instead of carrying all their reactants inside, which makes them light, and their water-based electrolytes don't burn. The hard part is recharging, because the same electrode has to turn oxygen into water on the way down and back into oxygen on the way up. The cobalt atom at the heart of B12 can do both jobs. B12 also dissolves easily in water, so the team bolted it onto carbon nanotubes, a little like screwing a faucet to the wall so it can't float away, and their prototype ran for up to 250 charge cycles. Over at University College London, Guanjie He's group took on a different water-based chemistry. In a Nature paper they designed an electrolyte that lets each iodine atom pass four electrons per cycle instead of the usual two, roughly like letting a delivery truck make two drops per trip instead of one. Their zinc-iodine cells kept almost all of their capacity over thousands of cycles. On the lithium side, a KAIST team reported in ACS Nano a liquid-crystal polymer separator whose molecules stand upright. That turns the winding path ions normally take into something closer to a highway, cutting its twistiness about 2.5-fold, discouraging the needle-like lithium growths called dendrites, and keeping 94 percent of capacity after 100 fifteen-minute fast charges.

Two stories this week were about electricity itself rather than storing it. Physicists at Utrecht University reported in Science on what may be holding back room-temperature superconductors, the dream materials that would carry current with no loss at all. Superconductivity needs electrons to pair up and then move in lockstep. Using instruments that can tell current carried by single electrons from current carried by pairs, the team found that in a copper-oxide superconductor the pairs hold together far above the temperature where superconductivity actually appears. The pairing is fine; what fails is the marching in step. Meanwhile a team led by Dirk Witthaut at Germany's Forschungszentrum Jülich and the University of Cologne cracked a decade-old puzzle. Grid frequency on five continents has been twitching by a few thousandths of a hertz once every minute. The culprit, described in Nature Energy and widely covered last week, turned out to be ordinary backup batteries in office buildings running automatic self-tests, all synchronized to the same clocks, like an entire stadium clearing its throat at the same moment.

So what does any of this mean if you just want your phone to last the day? Mostly, the future of batteries won't hinge on one miracle material. It will come from many small, clever fixes: a cheap, non-flammable chemistry here, a smarter separator there, a vitamin that can run a reaction in both directions. The grid story is a reminder that as we plug in millions more batteries, data centers and smart devices, how they behave together starts to matter as much as how each one works on its own. The fix there was refreshingly simple: the manufacturer pushed a software update that staggers the tests. Sometimes the most useful thing physics can do is tell your machines not all to cough at once.

Science Daily — Sunday, November 22, 2026
Sunday Long Read

The $1,000 Dare to Write an Encyclopedia on a Pinhead

Nanotechnology

On December 29, 1959, at the American Physical Society's annual meeting at Caltech, Richard Feynman gave a talk with a cheeky title: “There's Plenty of Room at the Bottom.” He spoke without notes, and we only have it because an admirer recorded it. Near the end he did something physicists rarely do in public. He put money on the table. “It is my intention,” he said, “to offer a prize of $1,000 to the first guy who can take the information on the page of a book and put it on an area 1/25,000 smaller in linear scale in such manner that it can be read by an electron microscope.” It took twenty-six years, and a graduate student who was three years old when the dare was made, to collect.

The talk was a tour of a world nobody could yet visit. Feynman asked why we could not write the entire Encyclopaedia Britannica on the head of a pin, then estimated that, stored in three dimensions, every book in the world could fit in a speck about a two-hundredth of an inch across. He floated the idea, credited to his friend Albert Hibbs, of “swallowing the doctor”: a tiny surgical robot you could gulp down like a pill. And he sketched a gloriously odd route there. Build mechanical hands a quarter of your size, use them to build smaller hands, and those to build smaller ones still, until perhaps a billion tiny factories work side by side. He warned that the rules would change on the way down: gravity stops mattering, while the faint stickiness between molecules known as van der Waals forces takes over, so every tool would need rethinking.

He offered a second prize too, for a working electric motor that would fit inside a cube a sixty-fourth of an inch on a side. By November 1960 a Caltech graduate named William McLellan had built one, using two and a half months of lunch hours, a microscope, a watchmaker's lathe and a toothpick. It met the rules exactly but advanced nothing; it was superb craftsmanship rather than new physics. Feynman had technically only announced an intention to offer a prize, but his conscience nagged and he paid anyway. Caltech's magazine reported his “worried thoughts” with mock sympathy: he had since married and bought a house and did not have another spare $1,000. Its appeal to anyone chasing the writing prize: “TAKE YOUR TIME! WORK SLOWLY! RELAX!”

The world obliged. The paper drifted around among enthusiasts for a quarter of a century, until a Stanford graduate student named Ken Polasko pointed it out to fellow student Tom Newman. Newman's doctoral research was on speeding up electron beam lithography, drawing patterns with a focused stream of electrons, and his system worked rather like a dot-matrix printer. He needed an arbitrary pattern to show it off. “Text is ideal,” he said, “because it has so many different shapes.” He picked the opening page of A Tale of Two Cities, a favourite novel with a famous first paragraph. With a beam about one five-millionth of an inch wide, he traced the words onto a thin plastic membrane, then dissolved the exposed regions away in ethanol. A good start over one weekend became a month of hard work to make the letters legible. Then came the strangest problem: finding the text at all. Compared with the writing, the little square of plastic was a vast empty desert, and it took Ann Marshall of Stanford's Center for Materials Research tuning her electron microscope to bring up a readable image.

Newman telegrammed Feynman to ask whether anyone had claimed the prize. Nobody had. The page measured about 5.9 by 5.9 micrometres, and Feynman's letter with the check is pure delight. Each dot, he calculated, was only about 60 atoms on a side, the whole page only ten wavelengths of light across. The entire Britannica at that scale would fit on less than two millimetres on a side, “the head of a small plain pin.” Newman passed his PhD oral exam that December 4, tiny Dickens and all.

Here is the twist. When the historian Chris Toumey traced the talk's influence in 2008, he found it was barely cited for its first twenty years, and several of the scientists who built the field said they had not read it until later. It was rediscovered only after the word “nanotechnology” caught fire in the late 1980s, after the scanning tunneling microscope arrived in 1981, and after researchers arranged 35 individual xenon atoms in 1990. Then it became scripture. In January 2000, President Clinton quoted Feynman's question about arranging atoms one by one while calling for a $500 million National Nanotechnology Initiative.

So was the talk a prophecy, or a story we told ourselves afterwards? Perhaps both. In 2016, researchers at TU Delft stored a paragraph of Feynman's own speech, 1,016 bytes of it, by positioning chlorine atoms on a copper surface, each bit marked by an atom and the gap beside it, at 502 terabits per square inch. The lecture had finally been written at the scale it described. The lesson is a lovely one: sometimes the most useful thing a scientist can do is not solve a problem but point at it, offer a modest reward, and tell everyone to take their time.

Science Daily — Saturday, November 21, 2026
Agriculture

This Week in Agriculture

Every crop in a field is fighting on several fronts at once. Insects chew it, viruses get into its sap, the soil around its roots turns sour, and heat waves cook it. Plant breeders have spent ten thousand years picking winners without seeing how those fights work. This week's research looked at the mechanics: the genetic toolkit of barley's wild cousins, the layered defenses of one commercial hybrid, a pest that stole a gene from a fungus, and the simple chemistry of a bag of lime.

Start with barley, the grain behind beer, whisky and a lot of animal feed. An international team led by Germany's IPK Leibniz Institute sequenced 27 samples covering 25 species across the whole Hordeum genus and published the results in Nature. The genes themselves turned out to be remarkably similar from species to species. What varies quickly is the regulatory DNA, the switches that decide when a gene turns on and how strongly, a bit like many cooks sharing one cookbook but each keeping their own oven timer. One South American species appears to use small RNA molecules to silence a gene that normally holds back flowering, which lets it bloom without a winter chill. That trick could interest breeders who want barley for warmer climates. Stranger still, 611 genes seem to have jumped into wild barley from millet-related grasses, and they arrived sideways rather than being passed down from parents. A second barley study shows what wild genes can already do. Researchers at Nottingham, Syngenta and Keele examined SY Kestrel, a commercial hybrid that carries DNA from the wild relative Hordeum bulbosum, and found three defenses stacked like a castle's walls. A scent blend cut aphid settlement by more than 75 percent. Aphids that landed anyway struggled to keep feeding on the sap. And barley yellow dwarf, the virus the aphids carry, had trouble getting established inside the plant.

Pests have their own tricks, and one reported in eLife is a good example of evolutionary theft. Tea leaves are full of catechins, the bitter antioxidants that make green tea astringent and that also act as the plant's own insecticide. Scientists at the Tokyo University of Agriculture and Technology showed that the Kanzawa spider mite gets past them with DOG15, an enzyme encoded by a gene its ancestors picked up from fungi. Tea-adapted mites switch the gene on hard when they feed, and when the researchers turned it down, the mites struggled to survive on tea. Below ground, a University of Nottingham team writing in Nature Communications found that soil acidity changes how roots cope with heat. In acidic soil, heat damage got worse. In neutral soil, roots kept growing and recovered better after a short heat spell. That suggests liming, a very old farm practice, could also help crops through heat waves. Other researchers are testing an equally low-tech fix: spraying soybeans with kaolin, a common white clay that Florida citrus growers have long used, to keep pests off and cut insecticide bills.

Why should someone who never sets foot on a farm care? The food on your plate depends on these unseen contests, and the warming climate is giving pests and heat an advantage. This week's research offers some hope. Some of the best new defenses already exist in wild grasses, and breeders can now read them in detail. Some of the cheapest fixes, like lime and clay, have been sitting in farm sheds for generations. And understanding why a defense works, rather than just noticing that it does, is how farmers stay ahead of pests that keep finding new ways around them.
