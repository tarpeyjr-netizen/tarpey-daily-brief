Google Gemini

This Week in Google Gemini

Google Gemini is the AI that lives inside Google’s apps and phones, and it also powers the tools coders use to build their own AI projects. Last week Gemini moved deeper into your phone. This week it learned to run errands for you, and to use your computer like a person would.

The biggest change landed on November 17, according to Google’s Gemini release notes. Tasks, the feature that lets Gemini chew on a multi-step job in the background and ping you when it is done, is now free in the US, India, Canada, Australia, Japan and Korea. And Tasks got a lot more hands. You can now text Gemini from Google Messages or iMessage to start a job, or just forward it an email, like a school calendar you want copied into Google Calendar. In the US, Gemini can watch for a sold-out item to come back in stock or a price to drop within your budget, then line up a Google Pay checkout. It only buys once you say yes. It can even phone several local shops at once to compare quotes or check what is on the shelf. It tells them it is an AI, and you approve the call plan first. Then on November 19, paying subscribers in the US got computer use in the Gemini app for Mac. Gemini can click, type and fill in forms across apps and websites in its own separate window, and it stops and waits for you whenever a login or payment comes up. Think of it as a helper borrowing your keyboard while you sit beside them.

Builders had a busy week too. Google’s Gemini API changelog shows Gemini 4 Neon going generally available on November 18. It is a heavy-duty model aimed at tough coding, science and office work, and it can hold about a million tokens in mind at once (a token is roughly a chunk of a word). The same day, apps gained the power to read Word, PowerPoint and Excel files directly, and to slice out just the pages or spreadsheet tabs you need, so you only pay for what you use. On November 19, Genie 4, Google DeepMind’s world-maker, became generally available to developers. You describe a place, or show it a picture, and it streams back a world you can steer with your arrow keys, with sound included. It costs about ten cents a second, which makes it a pricey video game, but a magical one.

So what is the big picture? Gemini is turning from something you talk to into something that does things for you, from texting a shop to filling in a form. The safety rule is the same everywhere: it checks with you before it spends money or does anything big. Expect more of these “do it for me” powers to spread beyond the US over the coming months.

Meta AI — Llama models

This Week in Meta AI — Llama models

Quick reminder first. Meta’s big AI push no longer goes by the name Llama. Its flagship is now Muse, a personal AI agent that can actually get things done, like booking, shopping and planning. This week there was no new Llama news at all, but Muse packed its bags and went traveling.

On November 17, Meta announced that Muse is now available in Australia. It comes with local “connectors,” which are like plug-in adapters that let Muse work with apps people already use. Early partners include Canva, Kogan, Officeworks, Ticketek and the property site realestate.com.au. Muse can also link to Gmail, Google Calendar, Instagram and OpenTable. Australians get real-time voice chats too, plus Spaces, where you describe a mini-app like a trip planner and Muse builds it. A day later Muse crossed the Pacific to Canada. Meta said Loblaw is its first Canadian retail partner, so Muse users can plan meals, fill a grocery basket and check out at participating Loblaw stores without leaving the app. Muse can even remember that you are allergic to peanuts the next time it plans dinner. Canada also got voice chats and Spaces the same day. In both places, Meta says Muse runs on its own locked-down cloud computer that keeps your passwords and card details out of the agent’s sight, and it asks before it sends an email or buys anything.

Meta’s research side had news as well. At its Open Source AI for Science Summit, written up on November 20, Meta’s FAIR lab released UMA Nitro, a faster version of its model that simulates how atoms behave. Its UMA Playground chemistry tool is also headed into high school classrooms through Discovery Education. So while Llama is quiet, Meta is still sharing open research, just with a new cast of characters. Watch for Muse to keep landing in new countries, and for the long-promised support on Meta’s AI glasses.

Microsoft Copilot

This Week in Microsoft Copilot

Microsoft Copilot is the AI helper that lives inside Word, Excel, PowerPoint, Outlook and Teams. Last Sunday we said Microsoft Ignite was the week to watch. It delivered. The big conference ran November 17 to 20 in San Francisco, and Copilot was the star of almost every keynote.

The headline is that Copilot now has its own permanent sidekick. It is called Autopilot, and The Neuron reported on November 17 that it is a personal agent with its own memory, tools and even its own work ID. Think of a helper who keeps working on a project after you leave the room. It can follow up on chats and handle repeat chores without you asking each time. Coworkers can even @mention your Autopilot in Teams to ask it for something. Every action it takes is tagged with its own ID, so a company can tell what you did from what your assistant did. It is rolling out first through Frontier, Microsoft’s early-access club. Next comes a feature called Today. It reads your email, calendar, chats and meetings, then lines up draft replies and schedule changes for you to approve. Picture a morning briefing from a very organized friend. It is in private preview for now. The piece that is ready for everyone is Office in Copilot. You can now open and edit Word, Excel and PowerPoint files right inside the Copilot app, with the same file permissions your work already uses. According to Data Studios, that is generally available and included with a Copilot license.

There is more on the way. Copilot Code lets you build simple apps and dashboards just by describing them in plain words. Microsoft says it is due for general release in December. Copilot Workspaces is also replacing Copilot Notebooks, and your old notebooks carry over. One catch is still hanging around from last week. Microsoft did not publish prices for Copilot Credits, the meter that some of these always-on features run on. The Today Feed, which updates all day, eats those credits.

So what does it all mean? Copilot is changing from a chatbot you ask questions into a coworker that keeps going on its own. The next test comes in December, when Copilot Code is due and we may finally learn what the meter costs.

Canva AI / Magic Studio

This Week in Canva AI / Magic Studio

Canva is the drag-and-drop design app that millions of students use for posters, slides and social posts. It has packed in a lot of AI lately, from its Canva Craft image model to Canva Brush for editing designs. This week it went the other way. It gave people a way to turn the AI off.

On November 20, Mumbrella reported that Canva launched two free “AI Controls” for Canva Free and Pro users. The first is a simple switch. Flip it, and the AI features that make new pictures and text disappear from Canva. Flip it back any time. It sits under Profile, then Settings, then Permissions. Handy everyday tools like Background Remover and Magic Grab still work, because they only edit what you already made. The second control trims how much AI-made stuff shows up when you browse Canva’s library of graphics. Some backgrounds and illustrations are tagged as AI-made, and this setting pushes them down in search results. It is a bit like telling a music app to play fewer remixes. Canva says the goal is to “put creative choice back in your hands,” and notes its AI tools have been used more than 38 billion times.

The timing is interesting. Earlier this month Canva announced a restructure that hit about 22% of its staff, and it has been betting heavily on AI. Now it is also listening to people who would rather draw with their own hands. Expect other creative apps to face the same question: how do you sell AI while respecting the folks who want less of it?

SAP Joule

This Week in SAP Joule

Joule is the AI helper built into SAP, the huge software system big companies use to run payroll, factories, shipping and their money. For most of the week it was quiet. Then on November 20, SAP explained how Joule is moving into Microsoft’s world, right after Microsoft’s big Ignite conference.

The idea is simple. Lots of office workers spend their whole day in Microsoft Teams, so SAP wants Joule to meet them there. SAP says people can already reach SAP business information from inside Teams through Joule Work, its new workspace where you type a goal in plain words and Joule’s helpers go do the steps. Developers building agents in Microsoft Copilot Studio can now plug in SAP’s ready-made connectors and templates, and they can call SAP’s Joule Agents from Microsoft workflows without rebuilding the SAP logic from scratch. Think of it like a translator who lets two coworkers who speak different languages finally work on the same project. SAP also says Joule Work will connect with Work IQ, a Microsoft layer that understands your meetings, emails and files, but it only says that will roll out “over time.”

The bigger promise is that Joule and Microsoft Copilot will eventually talk in both directions, so SAP insights show up in Microsoft 365 and SAP apps can draw on Teams and SharePoint. SAP says “soon” but gives no date, so treat it as a plan, not a product yet. The so-what: companies don’t want two AI assistants that ignore each other, and SAP is betting that playing nicely with Microsoft makes Joule more useful, not less.

The brains came first. Copilot is also learning to use more tools. One goodbye: GitHub says MAI-Code-1.1-Flash will be retired in December.

The so-what: Copilot is turning from a single helper into a whole workshop, with a growing list of AI models, agents that can click around a browser, and tools to grade those agents. More power means more choices, so teams will need those new testing tools to know which setup really works best.

This Week in ChatGPT

ChatGPT is the chatbot most people picture when they hear "AI": you type, or talk, and it answers. This week it learned to do a few things that feel very grown-up. It can now pay for stuff, it got two new brains, and it got a lot better at listening.

Companies also got an Accounting plugin that can match up transactions, enter invoices and build month-end reports, once an admin says yes.

Put it together and ChatGPT is acting less like a chat window and more like an assistant that can actually get things done. That's handy. It also means the boring-sounding safety steps, like approving every purchase yourself, matter more than ever.

Anthropic Claude Code

This Week in Anthropic Claude Code

Claude Code is Anthropic’s coding helper that lives in your terminal. You tell it what you want in plain English, and it reads your project, writes the code and runs the commands. It ships updates almost every day, and this week it put out five of them. The most interesting one teaches Claude a new way to use its own tools.

That one landed on November 12. Version 2.1.323 added an experimental, opt-in feature called Code Mode. Normally Claude uses its tools one at a time, like asking a friend to hand you each Lego brick separately. In Code Mode, Claude writes a little JavaScript program that calls the tools for it, more like handing your friend the whole instruction booklet. The same update let plugins say which versions of Claude Code they work with, so a mismatched plugin simply won’t load instead of breaking things. Code Mode kept growing after that. On November 16, version 2.1.326 let it work alongside agent teams, where several copies of Claude split up a big job. Then on November 18, version 2.1.328 dropped a three-quarter-second pause that came after most of its scripts in auto mode. That same release made auto mode the starting setting for interactive sessions on Opus 4.6 and Sonnet 4.6 across big cloud platforms like Amazon Bedrock and Microsoft Foundry, as long as no permission mode is set. It also tidied up /code-review. On most models it now reports only real bugs and broken project rules, and it tags each finding as Important, Pre-existing or Nit. Style cleanups moved over to /simplify. In between, the locks got tighter. Version 2.1.325 on November 13 fixed a security problem in how compressed web responses get unpacked, and 2.1.327 on November 17 let you feed connection settings in through a pipe so secret tokens don’t sit in plain view on the command line.

The so-what is that Claude Code is quietly changing how it thinks about tools, from tapping one button at a time to writing its own little scripts. A date to remember is November 30, when fast mode for the older Opus 4.8 model switches off for good.

DeepSeek

This Week in DeepSeek

DeepSeek is the Chinese AI lab famous for building strong models cheaply and giving a lot of them away. After a sleepy few weeks, it woke up on November 13 with a release it calls the Full-Suite App API. The short version: everything the DeepSeek chat app can do, developers can now plug into their own apps.

Think of it like a restaurant that used to sell only meals, and now sells its whole kitchen too. Its speedy V4.1-Flash model now comes with built-in tools you switch on with a single setting. It can search the web and show its sources. It can do “deep thinking search,” reading pages while it works through a hard problem step by step. It can read PDFs, Office files and images you hand it, and it can open links you paste into a message. DeepSeek’s servers do the fiddly work of calling those tools behind the scenes. There are also separate APIs for search, page fetching, reading text from images, turning speech into text and turning text into speech. There is a catch, though. Any app the public can use must switch on DeepSeek’s safety checks, and the tools cost extra on top of the normal per-word fees. DeepSeek also shared a free, open-source demo of a chat app on GitHub, so builders can see how all the pieces fit together.

So what? Until now, building your own “ChatGPT with web search” meant wiring up lots of parts yourself. DeepSeek just made that a few switches. Expect a wave of small apps quietly running on DeepSeek under the hood, and expect rivals to feel the pressure on price again.

Google Gemini

This Week in Google Gemini

Google Gemini is the AI that runs through Google’s apps, its Pixel phones and the tools coders use to build their own AI projects. This week Gemini had two big jobs at once. It moved deeper into your phone, and it shipped two new Gemini 4 models for the people who build apps.

The phone news came first. The November Pixel Drop, which started rolling out on November 10, brings “Gemini Intelligence” to the Pixel 10 family (but not the cheaper Pixel 10a). The fun part is Rambler, a voice-typing tool that cuts out every “um” and “like” and hands you a clean message to check before you send it. Gemini can now also run errands in Japan, the UK and India, like ordering groceries, booking a ride or grabbing a restaurant table. You can even describe a home-screen widget, say a countdown to your next trip, and Gemini builds it for you. On the Pixel 11 in the US, Call Screen got smarter too. Gemini now chats with unknown callers to find out what they want, a bit like a polite receptionist who screens your calls.

Under the hood, the developer side was even busier. Google’s Gemini API changelog shows Gemini 4 Carbon going generally available on November 10, at an introductory price of $2 per million input tokens (a token is roughly a chunk of a word) through November 30. Two days later came Gemini 4 Hydrogen, a cheaper model that even works on the free tier and adds an extra-hard “xhigh” thinking setting for tough problems. In between, on November 11, Google launched Gemini Decision 1, a model that does one thing: it makes a call. Show it text, a photo, audio, a video or a PDF and ask a yes-or-no or pick-a-category question, and it answers without writing a single word of chatter. Think of it as a referee instead of a commentator.

So what does it all add up to? Gemini is turning from a chatbot you visit into a helper that quietly does stuff, on your phone and inside other people’s apps. The Pixel Drop keeps rolling out over the next few weeks, so if your phone hasn’t picked up the new tricks yet, it probably will soon.

Meta AI — Llama models

This Week in Meta AI — Llama models

Quiet week for Meta AI — no major announcements in the past seven days, and no new Llama news either. That is mostly because Meta has moved its flagship AI from Llama to a new family called Muse. The last notable moves came just before our window opened. On November 5, Meta added real-time voice chats and expressive avatars to its Muse app, starting in the US. On November 6, it began rolling out new specialized models trained with Muse Wonder to both Muse and Meta AI. They are built to remember what matters from earlier chats and to keep going when a task hits a snag. Meta says Muse is now 2.4 times less likely to forget key details, though that number comes from Meta’s own tests.

Cursor

This Week in Cursor

Cursor is a code editor with AI agents built in. You describe what you want, and the agents write, test and fix the code with you. This week brought one fun new feature and one serious bit of homework.

The fun part landed on November 10, when Cursor Projects went multiplayer. Projects, which launched in September, used to be a solo space. Now you can invite teammates. Everyone gets a shared task board showing who is working on what and what each person’s agents are up to. Each person still has a private chat, but decisions and design docs live in a shared layer every agent can read. The neat twist is that the agents talk to each other. If yours and a teammate’s are about to change the same database, they can agree on an order first, like two movers deciding who carries the couch through the door. When someone new takes over the on-call shift, their agent can pick up the work already in progress. It is in beta and rolling out to everyone.

The serious part came on November 13, when Cursor posted an update on a recent security incident. An attacker got hold of an access key Cursor had given to Fireworks, one of its AI partners, and copied stored coding-session records, mostly from 2025. Cursor says users in Privacy Mode were not affected. Fewer than 0.05% of other users had their records exposed, and those people were contacted directly. The attacker also slipped fake copies of Anthropic’s Claude Code extension into Cursor’s extension store for about 11 hours on October 29. Cursor says the last attacker activity it saw was on November 1. Since then it has swapped out its passwords and keys, rebuilt the extension store and added scanning that catches leaked passwords.

The takeaway is simple. If Cursor emailed you, change the keys it listed. If you installed Claude Code versions 2.1.312 or 2.1.313 from Cursor’s store, follow Cursor’s October 29 cleanup steps. Then go enjoy coding with your friends, and your friends’ robots.
