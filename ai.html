Google Gemini

This Week in Google Gemini

Gemini is Google's flagship AI, the brain baked into everything from Search to Android to Google Docs. If you've talked to an AI inside a Google product recently, odds are Gemini answered you. And this week, Gemini leaned hard into one thing: making video feel less like a rendering job and more like a conversation.

The headline move is Gemini Omni Flash, a new video model that lets you build and edit clips just by describing what you want, whether you're starting from text, a photo, an old video clip, or even a snippet of audio. Think of it like editing a movie by chatting with a very fast, very literal film crew instead of dragging clips around a timeline. It's already showing up across the Gemini app, Google's Flow video tool, AI Studio, the developer API, and the Enterprise Agent Platform, so it's not a one-off demo, it's being wired into everything at once. Alongside that, Gemini is quietly getting smarter about the physical world too: Google opened up its WeatherNext forecasting models, including a new one built specifically for tracking cyclones, giving Gemini-powered tools sharper storm predictions built from thousands of simulated scenarios rather than a single best guess. And back in the classroom, Gemini is rolling out tools that let teachers generate grading rubrics instantly from an assignment, plus new study aids for students like flashcards and practice quizzes tied directly to their class materials.

The common thread here is Gemini pushing outward from "chatbot you type into" toward "assistant baked into whatever you're already doing," whether that's editing a video, checking the weather, or grading homework. For regular users, that mostly means less friction: fewer separate apps, more of the AI just showing up where you need it.

Meta Llama

This Week in Meta Llama

Llama has been Meta's answer to "what if the best AI models were free for anyone to download and tinker with?" For a few years, that open approach made Llama the go-to foundation for developers who didn't want to be locked into a paid API. But this week brought a real plot twist in that story.

Meta just launched Muse Spark, a brand-new AI model, and unlike Llama, it's proprietary, meaning you can't just download the weights and run it yourself. That's a notable shift for a company that built its AI reputation on being the open alternative to the likes of OpenAI and Anthropic. The backdrop makes it easier to understand why: open-weight competitors from Chinese labs like Alibaba and DeepSeek have been eating into Llama's dominance, reportedly accounting for a huge share of open-model downloads by late last year, with newer releases like Alibaba's Qwen and Zhipu's GLM even out-benchmarking Llama 4 on some tests. Meta says it still plans to open-source future versions down the line, so this isn't necessarily Llama's funeral, more like a hedge while the company regroups. On a smaller note, Meta also wrapped up its Llama Impact Grants, handing over $1.5 million to ten international teams using Llama for things like nonprofit and public-good projects.

The bigger picture: the "open vs. closed" AI race is getting messier, and Meta blinking toward a closed model, even partially, is a sign of just how competitive that open-source lane has become. Worth watching whether Muse Spark stays a one-off experiment or becomes the new default.

Anthropic Claude Cowork

This Week in Anthropic Claude Cowork

Claude Cowork is Anthropic's "AI teammate" app — instead of just chatting, it can actually work with your files, your calendar, and your other apps to get real tasks done. Think of it less like a search box and more like a helpful coworker sitting at the desk next to you. And this week, that coworker got some serious upgrades under the hood.

The biggest news is still rippling out from Anthropic's decision to break Cowork free from the desktop. What started as a desktop-only app is now rolling out to the web at claude.ai and to phones on iOS and Android, starting with Max subscribers and gradually opening up to more plans over the following weeks. The clever part is that these remote sessions run on Anthropic's own servers — so if you kick off a task and then close your laptop, the work keeps going without you. It's like leaving a slow cooker running while you're at school. Alongside that, Anthropic just shipped support for the new MCP spec (that's the "universal plug" standard that lets Claude connect to outside tools), adding stronger security logins, embedded mini-apps inside conversations, and private network tunnels so companies can hook Claude up to their internal systems safely.

There was model news too: Claude Opus 5 arrived, a faster and cheaper engine for coding and knowledge work, which means the brain powering your Cowork sessions keeps getting sharper without you doing anything. And for the business crowd, Enterprise admins picked up new dashboards showing usage, cost, and spending alerts — less exciting than robot coworkers, but it's the kind of plumbing that convinces big companies to go all-in.

The big picture: Cowork is turning from "an app you open" into "a service that follows you everywhere and keeps working when you're not looking." That's a genuinely different way to use AI, and the race to own that idea is heating up fast.

Eleven Labs

This Week in Eleven Labs

ElevenLabs is the company behind some of the most realistic AI voices on the internet — the tech that can read a script aloud so naturally you'd swear it was a human narrator. This week the company was busy on two fronts: raking in serious money and quietly making its tools smarter.

First, the money. ElevenLabs closed a $500 million Series D funding round that values the company at roughly $11 billion — that's billion with a "b," for a company whose product is essentially very convincing talking. One of the investors, tech consulting giant DXC Technology, didn't just write a check; it signed a strategic partnership to bring ElevenLabs' voice tech to big enterprise clients. A day later, ElevenLabs teamed up with OpenHome to launch a developer program in Japan, helping local builders create voice apps and AI agents there. Two partnerships in two days is a pretty clear signal: ElevenLabs wants its voices everywhere, in every language.

On the product side, the updates were nerdy but genuinely useful. The Scribe realtime transcription engine (the part that turns speech into text as you talk) can now be fed a list of up to 50 "keyterms" — special words like names or jargon it should listen for — and a new setting strips out the "ums," false starts, and filler words so transcripts read cleanly. The ElevenAgents platform also picked up finer controls over how AI voice agents take turns in conversation, plus a zero-retention mode for dialogue, meaning your words aren't stored afterward — a big deal for privacy-conscious companies.

Put it together and you get a company sprinting to own the entire "computers that talk and listen" market before anyone else catches up. With half a billion dollars of fresh fuel, they can sprint for a while.

🔍 Tool Spotlight

DeepSeek V4 Flash

DeepSeek is the Chinese AI lab famous for building models that punch way above their price tag, and on July 31 it officially released DeepSeek V4 Flash after months in preview. Flash is the "small but scrappy" member of the V4 family: a mixture-of-experts model with a massive 1-million-token context window, meaning it can hold roughly an entire book series in its head at once. The official release didn't change the architecture — instead, DeepSeek gave it extra training that sharply boosted its coding, tool-calling, and agent skills, the abilities that let an AI actually operate software rather than just chat about it. The wild part is the price: about 14 cents per million input tokens and 28 cents per million output tokens, roughly a third the cost of its bigger sibling V4 Pro — and pennies compared to Western frontier models. Benchmark scores like 82.7 on Terminal-Bench put it in genuinely serious company. For students, indie developers, and startups watching every dollar, Flash makes "build your own AI agent" suddenly feel affordable. That pricing pressure is exactly why the big labs are paying attention.

Anthropic Claude

This Week in Anthropic Claude

Claude is the AI assistant made by Anthropic, the company known for building models that are careful, good at writing and coding, and increasingly good at plugging into the other apps on your computer. This past week wasn't about one flashy headline — it was about Claude getting bigger, cheaper at the high end, and a lot easier to wire into everything else you use.

The biggest release was Claude Opus 5, a new flagship model that Anthropic is calling "near-frontier" — meaning it's right up near the smartest models out there — but at half the price of Claude Fable 5. Right alongside it, Claude Sonnet 5 on the developer platform got a huge memory boost: a 1 million token context window and 128,000 tokens of output room. Think of it like upgrading from a notebook to an entire desk covered in reference books Claude can flip through at once, without losing track of what it read on page one. Under the hood, Claude also picked up support for a new version of MCP, the connector standard that lets it talk to outside apps and services. That plumbing update makes it easier for developers to build tools Claude can use, and it's clearly working: MCP has crossed 400 million monthly downloads, four times where it stood at the start of the year. Claude Code, the coding-focused version, also got a batch of smaller quality-of-life fixes, from emoji autocomplete to tighter guardrails around background tasks and budgets.

The one thing to flag for anyone building on Claude: promotional pricing on Sonnet 5 ($2/$10 per million tokens) ends August 31, with standard pricing ($3/$15) kicking in September 1. For everyday users, though, the takeaway is simpler — Claude just got noticeably more capable at handling long documents and complex, multi-app workflows, and a cheaper top-tier model means more power without necessarily paying frontier prices.

Perplexity AI

This Week in Perplexity AI

Perplexity started life as an AI search engine that answers your questions with real cited sources instead of a pile of blue links. Lately, though, it's been pushing hard to become something bigger: less a search box, more a digital assistant that can actually do things for you.

The clearest sign of that shift is Personal Computer, Perplexity's AI agent that can open apps, edit files, and run multi-step tasks on your machine, which just expanded from Mac-only to Windows — instantly putting it in front of over a billion more devices. Perplexity also rolled out Computer support directly inside Microsoft 365, so it can now work inside Word, Excel, PowerPoint, Outlook, and Teams, complete with a live task-tracking panel and inline citations so you can double-check its work. On the research side, there's a new feature called Model Council, which takes a single tough question and hands it to a whole panel of different AI models at once, then writes up a summary of where they agree and where they diverge — a bit like getting several doctors' opinions and having someone helpfully highlight the disagreements for you. Under the hood, Perplexity also started using Claude Opus 5 across its Search and Computer products.

Add in new enterprise controls like custom roles, permissions, and identity-provider syncing, and the picture is clear: Perplexity is betting its future on being the assistant that does the work, not just the one that finds the answer. For regular users, that means more of your day-to-day busywork — drafting a spreadsheet, organizing files, digging through sources — might soon happen with barely any typing on your part.

Google Gemini

This Week in Google Gemini

Gemini is Google's flagship AI assistant, the one baked into Search, Docs, Vids, and pretty much your entire phone, built to feel less like a chatbot you visit and more like a helper that already lives inside the apps you use every day.

This week Google pushed two new model versions, Gemini 3.6 Flash and Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite, out of testing and into the "ready for prime time" tier, kind of like a video game leaving early access and landing on the real store shelf. The 3.6 Flash version is tuned to think through multi-step tasks more efficiently, so when you ask it to plan something complicated, it burns through less mental scratch paper to get there, and Flash-Lite is built to be cheap and fast for the kind of high-volume, behind-the-scenes automation businesses run constantly. On the creative side, Gemini's video tools got a boost called Omni, now built right into Google Vids, letting anyone type a request like "make this clip look more realistic" or "change the color grading" and watch the edit happen without touching a single slider. Gemini also picked up streaming speech generation, so it can start talking back in real time instead of processing your whole request first and then speaking, which makes voice conversations feel a lot less like a walkie-talkie and a lot more like an actual chat. On top of that, Gemini inside Google Docs now understands 11 more languages, including Mandarin, Hebrew, and Swedish.

None of this is one big flashy headline moment, but stacked together it's Google doing what it does best: quietly making Gemini faster, cheaper to run, and usable by a lot more people in a lot more languages, which matters more for everyday users than any single splashy demo.

Meta Llama

This Week in Meta Llama

Meta Llama has spent the last couple of years as the poster child for "open" AI, giving away powerful models for free so any developer could download, tweak, and build on them without paying anyone a cent.

That era looks like it's ending. Meta just launched Muse Spark, its newest AI model, and unlike every Llama before it, this one is proprietary, meaning you can't download the weights and run it yourself, you have to use it through Meta's own products. That's a genuinely big deal for a company that built its whole AI reputation on being the open alternative to closed shops like OpenAI. The shift has been brewing for a while: Meta reorganized its AI research into a new group called Meta Superintelligence Labs, and its next major flagship model, code-named Avocado, has reportedly been pulled under tighter internal control and developed further away from the open-source spotlight that used to define how Meta shipped its models. After Llama 4 landed to a fairly lukewarm response from developers last year, Meta now appears to be betting that a more locked-down approach will help it compete rather than continuing to give its best work away for free.

If you're a developer who leaned on Llama's free, downloadable models, this one is worth watching closely. Meta says it still plans to open-source future models eventually, but for now the company that made "open" cool in AI is starting to look a lot more like everyone else.
