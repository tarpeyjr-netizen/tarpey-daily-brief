Anthropic Claude

This Week in Anthropic Claude

Claude is the AI assistant built by Anthropic, and it's best known for being the one people trust with long, messy, real work — reading a hundred pages, writing the thing, then actually doing the follow-up steps. This past week Claude got two upgrades that both point in the same direction: it's trying to stop being a goldfish, and it's trying to show up where your job already lives.

The goldfish problem first. Until now, Claude kept separate memories depending on where you talked to it — the chat you had in the app didn't carry over to Cowork, the file-and-task side of the product. Anthropic merged those, so anything Claude learns in one place it now knows in the other. Better still, it opened the box: you can read what Claude has remembered about you, edit it, or delete it, topic by topic. That's a small design decision with a big feel, like the difference between a friend who quietly takes notes and one who hands you the notebook and says "check this, is it right?" Alongside that, the desktop app picked up a built-in browser — a web panel Claude can open, read, click, and type into — which turns "here's a link, go look" into something it can just handle.

Then came the enterprise news. On August 26, Salesforce and Anthropic announced Claudeforce, a partnership that plugs Salesforce's customer data and business rules straight into Claude. It ships with a plugin carrying 37 prebuilt sales skills, so a salesperson can ask about live pipeline, update records, and take action without leaving the conversation. Anthropic also expanded on the education side, offering a free managed version of Claude to K–12 schools and districts — single sign-on, admin controls, classroom-ready material — with a year free for organizations that sign up by mid-2027.

Put those together and the theme is Claude trying to become less of a chat window and more of a coworker: one that remembers yesterday, can open a webpage itself, and knows what's in your company's systems. The interesting tension is that all three of those things require handing Claude more access — which is probably exactly why the memory controls arrived in the same week.

Perplexity AI

This Week in Perplexity AI

Perplexity started life as a search engine that answers your question in a paragraph with sources attached, instead of handing you ten blue links. It has been steadily mutating into something bigger, and this week it took the strangest and most ambitious step yet: it wants to live in a box on your desk.

The headline is Personal Computer, announced in late August — an always-on Perplexity that runs on a dedicated Mac mini and blends your local files and apps with its cloud service. The pitch is that it works around the clock as a kind of digital stand-in, watching for triggers, running tasks on its own, and pushing work forward while you're asleep. It's a genuinely odd idea in an era when everything else is moving to the cloud, and it drew some raised eyebrows in the tech press, which framed it as Perplexity muscling into the local-AI scene. Whether people want a second computer whose only job is to think for them is an open question, but it's a real answer to a real annoyance: cloud assistants can't see the files on your laptop.

Underneath that, the more useful upgrade is Brain, Perplexity's self-improving memory. Rather than storing a flat list of facts, Brain builds a private map of your sessions, connected accounts, files, and past decisions, and refreshes it overnight so the next task starts already knowing what worked and what flopped. Perplexity's own testing claims a 25% lift in answer correctness and 16% better recall while costing 13% less on tasks that had prior context — the AI equivalent of a colleague who's been on the project long enough to stop asking where the files are. The same stretch brought a faster Opus 4.8 mode, the ability to swap models mid-task, per-model usage stats, website publishing, and Comet — Perplexity's AI-first browser — opening up to enterprise customers.

The pattern here mirrors Claude's week almost exactly: memory that persists, a browser that acts, and a push into the workplace. Two companies that started at opposite ends — one an assistant, one a search box — are converging on the same product idea, which usually means the idea is right and the fight is about to get expensive.

Google Gemini

This Week in Google Gemini

Gemini is Google's flagship AI — the thing that answers your questions in Search, writes your emails in Gmail, and increasingly runs quietly in the background of nearly every Google product you touch. This week was less about one big splashy launch and more about Gemini seeping into places it wasn't before, which is arguably the more interesting story.

The headline is that Gemini 3.7 Flash is now sometimes powering AI Mode in Google Search. "Flash" is Google's word for its fast, cheap models — the sports car rather than the freight train — and putting it behind Search means billions of queries are getting a smarter engine without anyone clicking a button. Google also shipped 3.7 Flash with tightened safety guardrails around chemical, biological, and cyber-attack topics, which is the sort of unglamorous work that only makes news when someone skips it. Meanwhile, the Gemini API got two dedicated speech-to-text models called Gemini 3.5 Transcribe, which can handle 85+ languages, figure out who is speaking (that's "speaker diarization"), stamp a timestamp on every single word, and even learn your weird industry jargon so it stops mangling it. If you've ever fought with automatic captions on a recording of a noisy meeting, this is aimed squarely at you.

Workspace got its own share. On August 26 Google introduced "Ask Gemini in Google Chat," essentially a single command line that sits inside your work chat and lets you ask for things without hopping between apps. Then on August 31 Google began rolling out the ability to start and stop the "Take notes for me" feature straight from the touchscreen controller in a physical Google Meet room — a small thing, but it's the difference between an AI notetaker you have to remember to set up and one that's a button on the table. On the enterprise side, Gemini Omni 1.1 Flash entered public preview for video, image, and text tasks, while the older gemini-robotics-er-1.6-preview model was switched off today and the omni-flash-preview endpoint is scheduled to go dark on September 30.

The pattern here is worth noticing: Google isn't really selling you a chatbot anymore. It's wiring the same model into Search, Chat, Meet hardware, and the developer API, then quietly upgrading the engine underneath. For most people the upshot is that things you already use just get better without you ever downloading anything. For developers, the flip side is the deprecation notices — the models are improving fast enough that last year's endpoint has an expiration date on it.

Meta Llama

This Week in Meta Llama

Llama is Meta's family of AI models, and its claim to fame has always been openness: while most top-tier AI lives locked behind a company's servers, Meta published the actual model files so anyone could download them and run them on their own machine. That made Llama the backbone of a huge amount of independent AI work — and it's why the last few months have felt so strange to people who follow it.

Quiet week for Meta Llama — no major announcements in the past seven days.

The last genuinely notable move came on August 10, when Meta Superintelligence Labs released a model called Muse Glimmer under the permissive Apache 2.0 licence and put the weights on Hugging Face with no gatekeeping. It's a 30-billion-parameter multimodal model, meaning it reads both text and images, handles a 128,000-token context window and over 100 languages, and — the clever part — was squeezed down to roughly 4-bit precision so it fits under 20GB. Translated: it runs on a single decent gaming GPU or a well-specced Mac, offline, on your desk. That's a real capability sitting on consumer hardware rather than rented from a data centre.

The bigger question hanging over all of this is what happens to the Llama name itself. Meta's frontier model this year, Muse Spark, launched back in April as closed-weight and API-only — a sharp turn away from the open philosophy Llama was built on. Alexandr Wang, Meta's Chief AI Officer, has said an open-weight version of Muse Spark 1.2 is coming, but as of mid-August nothing had been published. So the story to watch isn't a feature release; it's whether Meta keeps its open-source promise, or whether Llama quietly becomes a brand it used to have.

Anthropic Claude Code

This Week in Anthropic Claude Code

Claude Code is Anthropic's coding assistant that lives inside a programmer's terminal — the plain black text window developers use to boss their computer around. Instead of copying code back and forth into a chat window, you just tell Claude what you want and it edits your actual files. This week was less about flashy new toys and more about the boring-but-important stuff: making the thing faster, safer, and harder to trip over.

The headline change is how permissions work. Claude Code now runs in "auto mode" by default, and the rules for what it's allowed to do can be written as ordinary sentences rather than cryptic settings — think "you can run tests but don't touch the database" instead of a wall of configuration. There's a new tab in the /permissions screen for tuning this, and you can now open that screen while Claude is still working, which sounds tiny but matters a lot when you're mid-task and suddenly want to pull the reins. Alongside that came a warning system for risky shell commands, so if a rule you wrote is looser than you probably intended, you hear about it before something goes wrong. Version 2.1.239 landed on August 21 and 2.1.245 followed by the 25th, carrying a long tail of fixes across plugins, themes, keyboard shortcuts, and session resuming.

Two changes stand out for anyone who runs Claude on big jobs. Subagents — helper copies of Claude that go off and work in parallel — are now capped at twenty running at once, and they can no longer spawn their own helpers. That closes off a genuinely alarming scenario where one task quietly multiplies into hundreds of background processes chewing through your budget. Separately, the built-in claude-api skill went on a diet: it used to cost over 200,000 tokens just to load, and now costs about 25,000 because the reference documents are fetched only when actually needed. That's roughly the difference between hauling an entire library into every conversation versus walking to the shelf when you need a book.

Rounding out the month, there's a /design skill that turns a rough idea or even a screenshot into an editable interface, a "Concise" output style that leads with the answer instead of the preamble, and auto-continue so hitting a usage limit no longer kills your momentum. Memory also now carries across Claude chat and Cowork, with editable topics. The through-line here is trust: Anthropic seems to be betting that the next wave of adoption comes not from Claude Code doing more, but from people feeling safe letting it do more unsupervised.

xAI Grok

This Week in xAI Grok

Grok is the AI assistant built by Elon Musk's xAI (now trading under the SpaceXAI banner), best known for being wired directly into X and for a chattier, less buttoned-up personality than its rivals. This week it had a genuinely busy stretch — a new flagship model, a big enterprise deal, and a product that lets anyone build software by describing it out loud.

The centerpiece is Grok 4.6, xAI's new frontier model for coding, agent work, and general knowledge tasks, now live on the company's API. Its standout number is a 500,000-token context window, which in practical terms means you can hand it something the length of a few novels and it keeps track of all of it at once. Pricing sits at $2 per million input tokens and $6 per million output tokens below the 200k mark, doubling above it — aggressive enough to undercut several competitors. Google promptly added Grok 4.6 to its Enterprise Agent Platform's Model Garden, complete with adjustable reasoning levels, which is a meaningful vote of confidence: big companies shopping for AI on Google's platform can now pick Grok off the shelf next to everything else.

On the consumer side, Grok Build rolled out to every plan on web and mobile. You describe an app, game, website, or dashboard, and you watch it get built in real time — then publish it, hook up a custom domain, export it to GitHub, or share it straight to X. Grok Bot arrived too: persistent AI teammates that live on a cloud computer and keep working between conversations, with messaging, approval steps, and scheduled routines. Developers got agentic tools like web search, X search, and code execution that can be mixed with their own tools in a single conversation. Grok's video generator picked up native 1080p and reference-to-video, and Grok Voice gained new multilingual voices.

The oddest item of the week: Interactive Brokers now integrates Grok directly into its trading platform for portfolio analysis, scenario modeling, and even placing order instructions. Whatever you make of an AI chatbot sitting next to your brokerage account, it's a clear signal of where xAI is aiming — not just a clever answer machine, but something embedded in the places where consequential decisions get made.

Anthropic Claude CoWork

This Week in Anthropic Claude CoWork

Cowork is Anthropic's attempt to let Claude do actual work on your computer instead of just talking about it — opening your files, building your spreadsheets, running your errands across the apps you already use. Think of it less as a chatbot and more as an assistant who gets handed the keyboard. And this week it picked up the one thing that had been quietly holding it back: a memory.

Until now, Cowork and regular Claude chat were like two coworkers who never talked to each other. You could spend twenty minutes in chat explaining how your company names its files, then hop into Cowork and have to explain the whole thing again from scratch. On August 25, Anthropic merged the two memory systems, so anything Claude learns in one place now shows up in the other. Better still, they made that memory visible — you can open a settings panel, read exactly what Claude has filed away about you, and edit or delete any of it. Sensitive categories like health and personal beliefs are switched off by default, but if those are the very things you're sick of re-explaining, you can flip them on yourself. That's a notably grown-up approach: most products that remember things about you don't hand you the notebook.

The memory news lands on top of a big week for reach. Anthropic confirmed on August 18 that Cowork is now fully rolled out on mobile and desktop for every paid plan, and the web version runs sessions remotely in beta — meaning your work and files live in your Claude account rather than on one particular laptop, and you can pick up a job on your phone that you started at your desk. Enterprise customers got their own quiet win too, with Anthropic's Compliance API now covering Cowork across desktop, web, and mobile, which is the unglamorous plumbing that makes IT departments willing to say yes.

Put those together and the shape of the thing changes. A tool that only worked on one machine and forgot you between sessions is a toy; a tool that follows you across devices and remembers your preferences is something you can actually build a routine around. The interesting question now is how much a person is willing to delegate once the friction of re-explaining themselves finally disappears.

Eleven Labs

This Week in Eleven Labs

ElevenLabs makes computers talk — and lately, listen, dub, transcribe, and sing. It started as the company whose text-to-speech was so convincing people had to double-check whether they were hearing a human, and it has spent the last two years turning that into a whole audio toolbox: voice agents that hold phone conversations, dubbing that carries your voice into another language, transcription, even music generation. This week was less about flashy new voices and more about rewiring the pipes underneath.

The headline change is that ElevenLabs retired its local MCP server. MCP is the standard that lets AI assistants like Claude plug into outside services, and until now, hooking ElevenLabs into your assistant meant installing software on your own machine and juggling an API key. That version is now archived and frozen. In its place is a hosted server at api.elevenlabs.io/v1/mcp that you simply sign into with your ElevenLabs account — no installation, no key to lose. It's the difference between having to run your own mail server and just opening Gmail, and it means far more people can wire ElevenLabs into their AI setup without touching a terminal.

Underneath that, the developer-facing updates piled up fast. New asynchronous "Flows" APIs let you kick off image, video, and speech generation jobs and collect the results later rather than sitting and waiting — a small change that matters enormously when you're generating hundreds of audio clips at once. Media assets became reusable, conversation summaries got lighter, and the voice agent platform gained finer controls over turn-taking and soft timeouts, which is the difference between an agent that talks over you and one that waits its turn. The default speech recognition engine also switched to scribe_realtime, and there's now zero-retention text-to-dialogue for anyone who needs audio generated without a copy being kept.

None of this is the kind of news that makes a demo video, but it's the kind that decides whether a technology becomes infrastructure. ElevenLabs is clearly betting that the future of AI audio isn't people typing sentences into a website — it's software quietly generating speech in the background of a thousand other apps.

🔍 Tool Spotlight

Muse Glimmer

Most powerful AI models live in someone else's data center. Muse Glimmer, released in early August by Meta Superintelligence Labs, is a deliberate push in the opposite direction: a 30-billion-parameter agentic model with open weights under the Apache 2.0 license, built specifically to run on hardware you own. Squeeze it down to 4-bit and it fits under 20 GB, which means a MacBook with an M4 Max chip or a desktop with an RTX 4090 can host it — no subscription, no cloud, no data leaving the room.

"Agentic" is the key word. Glimmer isn't tuned to write you a nice essay; it's tuned to take actions — calling tools, running multi-step tasks, working through long jobs with a 120,000-token context window so it doesn't lose the thread halfway through. A separate 1.8-billion-parameter encoder lets it see images and text together, so it can look at a screenshot and act on what's in it. Meta got the quality by distilling from its bigger Muse Spark model, essentially having the large model tutor the small one.

The surprising part is how easy it is to actually run: it works out of the box with llama.cpp, Ollama, LM Studio, Apple MLX, ExecuTorch, and vLLM. If you've ever wanted an AI assistant that runs entirely offline and never phones home, this is the most capable one yet.

Anthropic Claude

This Week in Anthropic Claude

Claude is the AI assistant built by Anthropic, best known for being unusually good at long documents, careful reasoning, and writing code without going off the rails. This week was less about a flashy new model and more about Claude quietly growing hands and feet — the tools that let it actually do things instead of just talking about them.

The headline is that the computer use tool and the browser use tool both went live on the Claude Platform, alongside the new Skills API and Files API. In plain terms: developers can now build apps where Claude clicks around a screen, drives a web browser, and works with uploaded files, all through official channels rather than duct-taped workarounds. Skills are the interesting one — think of them as little instruction manuals you hand Claude so it knows your team's specific way of doing a task, and now they can be stored on GitHub and pulled in automatically. Microsoft Foundry picked up the Skills and Files APIs too, and Google Cloud's Vertex AI is getting the computer and browser tools soon, which means Claude's new abilities aren't locked behind one door. Meanwhile the Developer Platform added grown-up controls for teams running Claude agents unsupervised: session budgets so an agent can't burn unlimited money, geographic pinning so data stays in the right country, and the ability to restrict exactly which websites an agent is allowed to visit. That last one matters more than it sounds — an AI browsing the open internet on your behalf is a lot safer when you get to draw the fence.

There was also a small piece of genuinely good news for anyone paying the bills. The introductory price on Claude Sonnet 5 — $2 per million input tokens, $10 per million output — was supposed to jump to $3 and $15 on September 1. Anthropic scrapped that increase and made the intro price permanent. Price hikes getting cancelled is rare enough in this industry to be worth noticing. On the consumer side, Anthropic launched Claude Academy, a free learning hub with courses, tutorials and badges aimed at people who want to actually get good at using AI rather than just poking at it. Claude Code, the terminal version, got a steady stream of unglamorous fixes: better cost estimates, smoother cloud sessions and remote control, Alpine and Bedrock support, and improvements to the /resume and /goal commands.

Put it together and the pattern is clear: Anthropic is spending less energy on making Claude sound smarter and more on making it trustworthy enough to leave alone with real work. Budgets, geo-fencing, site allowlists and audit-friendly admin tools are the boring plumbing that has to exist before a company will let an AI touch anything that matters. If you use Claude casually, the price freeze and Claude Academy are the parts you'll feel. If you build with it, this was a big week.

Perplexity AI

This Week in Perplexity AI

Perplexity started life as a search engine that answers your question with a written explanation and footnotes instead of a wall of blue links. Lately it's been morphing into something stranger and more ambitious — less a search box, more a digital assistant that works while you're asleep.

The most eye-catching move is Personal Computer, which just shipped on Mac. The idea: you dedicate a Mac mini to Perplexity, and it runs an always-on agent that has access to your local files, your apps, and your ongoing sessions. It watches for triggers, kicks off tasks on its own, and picks work back up the next day — a digital proxy that keeps going around the clock rather than waiting for you to type something. Perplexity even ships a prebuilt "Personal CFO" that keeps an eye on your money. It's an unusual bet in a world where everyone else is racing to put AI in the cloud; Perplexity is putting a physical box on your desk. Alongside it, the Agent API opened up to more outside brains — Claude Opus 4.7, GPT-5.5, and Grok 4.20 Reasoning can all now drive Perplexity's agents, which is a reminder that Perplexity has always been happy to rent other companies' models rather than obsess over building its own. And the Perplexity MCP Server now installs with one click into Cursor, VS Code, Claude Desktop, and Claude Code, so developers can pull Perplexity's search into whatever tool they already live in.

There's a courtroom subplot too. Earlier this month the Ninth Circuit vacated Amazon's injunction against Comet, Perplexity's AI browser, in a fight over Comet's shopping agent buying things on Amazon. The appeals court's reasoning was elegant: when Comet acts on your instruction, it's you accessing Amazon, not Perplexity — so the computer-crime law Amazon leaned on probably doesn't apply. That's a meaningful early marker for the whole agent industry, because the question of "who is actually visiting this website" underpins every AI that browses on your behalf. Comet itself also went enterprise, with silent MDM deployment across Mac and Windows and admin policy controls.

The through-line is that Perplexity no longer wants to be the thing you open when you have a question. It wants to be running in the background, holding your context, doing errands, and pushing back in court when someone tries to lock it out. Whether people are ready to hand a laptop-sized box the keys to their files and finances is the open question — but Perplexity is clearly betting the answer is yes.
