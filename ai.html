GitHub Copilot

This Week in GitHub Copilot

GitHub Copilot is the AI coding buddy that lives inside the tools programmers use every day. Last week was a fireworks show, with more than a dozen updates dropped at Microsoft Ignite. This week was the morning after: no new models and no new features, just one change that matters to whoever pays the bill.

On November 25, GitHub said it is changing who pays for Copilot code reviews by default. Right now, if a company on Copilot Business or Enterprise never picked a setting, a review uses up the reviewer’s own Copilot allowance. Starting December 30, it will be billed to the organization that owns the code instead. Think of it like a team lunch: until now, everyone has been paying out of their own meal card, and soon the bill goes to the company account. That only works if the company has turned on paid AI Credits, and GitHub warns it can increase spending. Companies that already chose a setting keep it. Anyone who wants things to stay the same has to pick “Member” in their Copilot policy settings before December 30. In a related bit of bookkeeping, GitHub said on November 23 that its detailed, AI and premium request usage reports will add a user ID column starting December 1. That makes it easier to match usage to the right person, even after they change their username.

So what? None of this changes what Copilot can do, but it shows that AI coding help is turning into a real line on the budget. With last week’s flood of new models and agents, companies will want to know exactly who is using what. For the next month, the main job for team admins is checking that billing setting.

🔍 Tool Spotlight

Inkling 2 Mini

Inkling 2 Mini is a new AI model from Thinking Machines Lab, the startup led by Mira Murati. It was released on November 20, and its big trick is size. It needs about 20GB of memory, so it can run on a Mac laptop with 32GB of RAM. It’s also open-weights under the Apache 2.0 license, so anyone can download it from Hugging Face and run it for free. It’s built mainly for coding. The surprising part is how it thinks. Instead of being taller, it reuses its middle layers, running them three times for each word, like rereading the hard part of a textbook before you answer. The lab says it beats Qwen3.8-27B on the DeepSWE coding test, 48.2% to 42.2%. The independent testers at Artificial Analysis say it’s about six times cheaper and four times faster per task. The lab is honest about the weak spots. It’s not the best small model for long chats or for following lots of instructions at once. And because the looping lets it reason without writing its thoughts down, it may be harder to check what it’s thinking. The lab calls its early results on that question preliminary.

So the big story is that ChatGPT keeps growing past the chat box. It's turning into a place where you build and share things, not just ask questions. Google Gemini is the AI that lives inside Google’s apps and phones, and it also powers the tools coders use to build their own AI projects. Last week Gemini moved deeper into your phone. This week it learned to run errands for you, and to use your computer like a person would.

So what is the big picture? Gemini is turning from something you talk to into something that does things for you, from texting a shop to filling in a form. SAP also says Joule Work will connect with Work IQ, a Microsoft layer that understands your meetings, emails and files, but it only says that will roll out “over time.”

The bigger promise is that Joule and Microsoft Copilot will eventually talk in both directions, so SAP insights show up in Microsoft 365 and SAP apps can draw on Teams and SharePoint. SAP says “soon” but gives no date, so treat it as a plan, not a product yet. The so-what: companies don’t want two AI assistants that ignore each other, and SAP is betting that playing nicely with Microsoft makes Joule more useful, not less.

Salesforce Agentforce / Einstein

This Week in Salesforce Agentforce

Agentforce is Salesforce’s family of AI agents that help salespeople and support teams do their jobs. Like SAP, Salesforce spent this week cozying up to Microsoft. On November 17, at Microsoft Ignite, the two companies announced Copilotforce, which brings Salesforce’s customer information and sales tools into Microsoft Copilot, Teams and Fabric.

Here is what that looks like in practice. A salesperson preparing for a meeting can ask Microsoft Copilot something like “how healthy is this deal?” and get an answer that mixes Salesforce records with their Teams meetings, Outlook emails and shared documents. That part is in public preview, available through Salesforce’s AgentExchange and Microsoft’s Copilot plugin directory. Inside Teams, a Sales Cloud app works with Agentforce Coworker, Salesforce’s AI teammate, so sellers can act on what they learn and direct Agentforce agents, though that piece is still a pilot. On the data side, companies can now look at data stored in Microsoft’s OneLake from inside Salesforce Data 360 without copying it over, and that is generally available. One nice safety touch: if the AI wants to change a customer record, the person has to confirm it first. Salesforce has not shared pricing yet.

The same day, Salesforce said Informatica, the data company it now owns, is bringing trusted company data into Microsoft Copilot, Foundry and Fabric. Put it together and the message is clear. Salesforce no longer insists you come to its app. It wants Agentforce to show up wherever you already work, a bit like a favorite band that starts touring your town instead of making you fly to theirs. Salesforce had also promised Coworker apps for Teams, iPhone, Mac and Windows in November, but we did not find a launch announcement this week.

GitHub Copilot

This Week in GitHub Copilot

GitHub Copilot is the AI coding buddy that lives inside the tools programmers use every day. Microsoft Ignite landed on November 17, and GitHub treated it like a holiday sale, dropping more than a dozen Copilot updates in a single day.

The brains came first. OpenAI’s GPT-6.2 Astra and GPT-6.2 Sol are now generally available for Pro+, Max, Business and Enterprise users across almost every place Copilot runs, from VS Code to Xcode to GitHub Mobile. GitHub says Sol found nearly twice as many real bugs in code reviews as the older GPT-6.1 Sol in early testing. Microsoft’s own MAI Code 2 and MAI Code 2 Flash arrived the same day, and Google’s Gemini 4 Neon followed on November 18. Copilot’s code reviewer got smarter too. It can now decide how hard to look at each pull request, going light on a typo fix and deep on a risky change, like a teacher who skims the easy homework and carefully checks the tough proofs. It can also spot passwords and secret keys accidentally left in code before they get merged. Both features are in public preview.

Copilot is also learning to use more tools. In the Copilot app, a preview lets it work inside your signed-in Chrome or Edge browser to fill out forms, grab information from web pages and test the website you are building. It is off by default. Another preview brings Azure DevOps projects into the app. A new tool called Copilot Evals, also in public preview, lets teams write repeatable tests that check whether their custom agents and skills actually behave well. And on November 18, team customizations became generally available for Business and Enterprise, so a team leader can switch on approved plugins for everyone at once. One goodbye: GitHub says MAI-Code-1.1-Flash will be retired in December.

The so-what: Copilot is turning from a single helper into a whole workshop, with a growing list of AI models, agents that can click around a browser, and tools to grade those agents. More power means more choices, so teams will need those new testing tools to know which setup really works best.

🔍 Tool Spotlight

Qwen4-Max

Qwen4-Max is the newest and most powerful AI model from Alibaba, the Chinese tech giant. It is the first member of Alibaba’s new Qwen4 family, and it became generally available on November 16 after a short preview that started November 11. It is enormous, with 2.4 trillion parameters, but it uses a “mixture of experts” design, so only part of the model wakes up for each question, like a hospital that sends you to the right specialist instead of having every doctor examine you. It can look at images directly and hold up to a million tokens in mind at once, roughly a shelf of long novels. Developers can reach it through Alibaba’s QwenCloud, Qwen Studio, QwenWork and the Qoder coding tool. The surprising part is the price. On Alibaba’s own Work360 test, the average cost of finishing a task fell about 58 percent compared with the older Qwen3.8-Max, because the model uses fewer tokens and Alibaba cut prices. Alibaba also released a plugin that lets agents show a first draft and fix it from your feedback. One catch: Alibaba plans to open-source smaller Qwen4 models, but Qwen4-Max itself is API-only for now, and its numbers come from Alibaba itself.
