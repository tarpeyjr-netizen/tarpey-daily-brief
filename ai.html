Anthropic Claude Code

This Week in Anthropic Claude Code

Claude Code is Anthropic’s coding helper that lives in your terminal: you describe what you want, and it reads your project, edits files and runs commands for you. Think of it as a very fast teammate who never gets tired of boring chores. This week the news was mostly about making that teammate easier to customize.

The big one landed on October 1, when Anthropic introduced “mods”: small TypeScript functions that change how Claude Code behaves and ship inside plugins, so teams can share them. The first built-in example is an opt-in “you should know” side agent that quietly flags things you or Claude might have missed, a bit like a friend peeking over your shoulder while you work. Anthropic also began testing a redesigned Projects experience in beta, where one project can run several threads in parallel with shared memory and a library of files.

Then on October 6 came version 2.1.292, a smaller release with practical upgrades: a --marketplace option so one command can add a plugin marketplace and install from it, an effort setting so you can tell a sub-agent how hard to think, and a new setting to lengthen the wait before retrying when the servers are overloaded. The pattern is clear: Claude Code is turning from a single tool into a platform people can bend to their own habits.

Eleven Labs

This Week in Eleven Labs

Eleven Labs makes the AI voices you hear in audiobooks, videos and phone assistants, and this week it gave its whole voice lineup a big upgrade. On September 28 it launched Eleven v4, a new model built to sound less like a robot reading a script and more like an actor who understands the scene.

The trick is context. Eleven v4 pays attention to who is speaking, what was said before, and how each line should land, the way a good actor reads a line differently after an argument than after a joke. You can steer it with simple tags typed right into the text, like [laughs], [whispers] or [door slams], and it handles more than 90 languages and several speakers in one scene. A feature called context stitching keeps a voice consistent across a long project, which matters if you are narrating a whole book. Professional Voice Clones, which were unavailable with the last version, are back too.

Alongside it came Eleven v4 Turbo, the speedy sibling made for live conversations. Its median response time is around 100 milliseconds, quick enough that a talking AI agent does not leave those awkward pauses that make you wonder if the call dropped. Both models are available on every plan, including the free one. The takeaway is that AI voices are getting good at feeling, not just pronouncing, and that is going to change how games, videos and customer calls sound.

🔍 Tool Spotlight

🔍 Tool Spotlight: Z.ai GLM-5.3

Most of the AI headlines belong to a handful of American companies, but Z.ai, the Chinese lab behind the GLM family, is quietly keeping pace. GLM-5.3 is its latest lineup of language models, the same kind of technology that powers chatbots, and a faster version called GLM-5.3 Fast showed up on October 7, the newest entry on one popular model-routing service this week.

Think of the family like a car brand with a sports model and an economy model. The flagship handles hard problems, while the “Flash” version, released in late August, trades a little brainpower for speed and a very low price, around fifteen cents per million input tokens (small chunks of text). It can hold roughly a million tokens in memory at once, which is about a shelf of novels. Developers who build apps that need instant answers are the obvious audience, since cheap and quick matters more to them than winning every benchmark.

The surprising part is that Z.ai reportedly held back the biggest model's weights rather than publishing them freely, a shift from the open-sharing habits of other Chinese labs. It is a reminder that the AI race is getting more competitive, and more cautious, at the same time.

Anthropic Claude

This Week in Anthropic Claude

Claude is Anthropic's AI assistant, and this week it picked up a faster, cheaper middle sibling and a pile of new ways to get work done. Think of it like a band releasing the radio-friendly version of last week's big album.

The headline was Claude Sonnet 5.5, which arrived September 28. Anthropic says it runs about 30% faster than Sonnet 5 and costs up to 30% less, which matters because Sonnet is the model most people end up using day to day. The very next day, Claude had a rough patch: an outage on September 29 left lots of people staring at error messages until a fix rolled out. Then on September 30, Anthropic launched Claude for Government, a version for federal and state agencies that meets the strict FedRAMP High security bar and adds audit logs and spending controls, like giving a school a locker system with a sign-out sheet.

Then today, Anthropic announced a redesign of Claude Code projects in cloud sessions: one project can now run several threads at once, all sharing memory, so Claude can split a big job into pieces and check its own results.

The so-what: the pattern is Claude moving from answering one question at a time to managing whole jobs. If you code, the project redesign is the one to try first.

Google Gemini

This Week in Google Gemini

Gemini is Google's family of AI models, the brains behind the chatbot in your phone and the helper tucked inside Gmail and Docs. This week Google showed off its newest brain, and it is a big one: Gemini 4 Argon, announced on September 30.

Argon is built for what Google calls deep reasoning, meaning problems that take many steps to solve, like debugging a huge program or digging through a pile of legal documents. It can write up to a million tokens in a single answer (roughly a few novels' worth), a giant jump from the old 64,000 limit. On Google's own tests it edged past Anthropic's Claude Opus 5.5 at fixing real software bugs, 77.9% to 74.2%. For now it is only in the hands of trusted testers and security defenders, with Google AI Ultra subscribers and paying developers getting it soon. The same day, Google added reusable "skills" to the Gemini app and Workspace. Think of them as recipe cards for the AI, and they are slowly replacing Gems. It also launched SynthID Bio, a hidden watermark for AI-designed proteins, and Live Avatar for businesses, which gives the AI a talking face with lip-sync.

Then came the catch. On October 3, reports said that from October 9, people without a paid Google AI plan will only get Flash-Lite, the smallest and cheapest model, instead of regular Flash and Pro. It is like a free sample table that suddenly shrinks right after the big restaurant reopening. If you rely on free Gemini, expect a noticeable change this Friday.

Microsoft Copilot

This Week in Microsoft Copilot

Imagine if your computer's helpful assistant stopped being a chatbox in the corner and became a whole workspace you live in. That is basically what Microsoft just did with Copilot, its AI helper that lives inside Windows, Word, Excel and the rest of the Microsoft world. The September update rebuilt the Copilot app around a new "Home" screen, where chatting with the AI and handing it bigger jobs sit side by side, with Word, Excel and PowerPoint built right in.

Two new pieces stand out. "Code," which borrows tech from GitHub Copilot, lets you describe an app or dashboard in plain English and have the AI build it, a bit like telling a very fast intern what you want on a whiteboard. "Autopilot" is a personal agent that keeps working in the background with its own identity and permissions, and it can nudge you before you even ask. Microsoft also widened the model menu, adding Grok models from SpaceXAI for early testers and a new OpenAI reasoning model for deeper thinking, so Copilot is no longer tied to just one brain.

The less flashy news matters too. Microsoft added usage-based billing and "Copilot Credits," so companies can see what their AI is costing, like a phone plan with a meter. Copilot in SharePoint and OneDrive also hit general availability, with Copilot Search becoming the default search there. The big question now is whether people actually want one AI workspace for everything, and whether the bills stay friendly. Even tech writers are arguing about whether calling Copilot an "operating system" is fair, since on Windows 11 it is still largely a web app.

🔍 Tool Spotlight

🔍 Tool Spotlight: Shopify Canvas

Imagine building a whole online store by simply chatting. That is Canvas, a new tool from Shopify that launched on October 1. Store owners talk to Sidekick, Shopify's AI assistant, and watch the shop redraw itself in front of them as they type. They can also click on any piece of the page, like a banner or a button, and change it directly. Shopify says a fully custom store can come together in about twenty minutes.

What makes it interesting is that it is not a flimsy mockup. The changes are the real code behind the store, because Shopify gave Sidekick direct access to theme files and simplified how themes are built so the AI can understand them. That matters because designing a shop has usually meant hiring a developer or wrestling with templates. Small business owners, side-hustle sellers and anyone with a product idea are the obvious users. The surprise is the fine print. It is desktop-only for now, it is rolling out over several days, and it does not yet support third-party themes, app blocks or translations. Shopify says those are coming.

This Week in ChatGPT

The headline is speed. OpenAI introduced a premium setting called Ultrafast for its new GPT-6.1 Sol model, which can spit out around 300 tokens (tiny chunks of words) every second. Teams got a lot too. Business and Enterprise plans picked up shared Pages and Spaces, team tasks, collaborative Sites editing, Codex Cloud for coding work, and the ability to call @ChatGPT inside Slack and Microsoft Teams. It was not all smooth, though. ChatGPT had a rough patch of degraded performance on September 30 that OpenAI says it will explain in a root cause write-up, and news outlets also reported that the company pulled a newer model version after safety concerns came up in testing. Details were thin when we checked, so treat that one as a story still unfolding. The big question now is whether people actually want an AI sitting in every app they use, or whether faster and cheaper turns out to matter more.

OpenAI Codex

This Week in OpenAI Codex

Codex is OpenAI’s coding agent, basically a tireless junior programmer that you can hand a task to and check on later. This week was its big moment, because DevDay on September 29 was packed with Codex news.

The biggest change is where Codex works. Until now, each cloud task was like a disposable sandbox that got thrown away afterward. There are a couple of grown-up additions too. Small updates shipped right behind the keynote, including instant interrupt so you can cut Codex off mid-thought and redirect it. The takeaway is that Codex is trying to stop being a tool you babysit and start being a teammate you can send off with a job.
