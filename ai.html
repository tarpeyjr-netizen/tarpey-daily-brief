Google Gemini

This Week in Google Gemini

Gemini is Google's all-purpose AI assistant — the thing that lives in the search bar, in Gmail, on your phone, and increasingly inside every Google app you already use. Google ships updates to it at a pace that borders on relentless, and this past week was no exception. The headline is a new model called Gemini 3.7 Flash, which is the fast, cheap version of Gemini that most people actually end up talking to.

What makes 3.7 Flash interesting isn't raw speed — the "Flash" models were always quick. It's that Google trained this one to be noticeably better at dense, jargon-heavy material: finance documents, legal contracts, biology papers. In benchmark testing it pulled clearly ahead of the 3.6 version on long PDF comprehension and on business-workflow tasks. Google also says it handles roadblocks better, meaning when it hits something confusing it asks a clarifying question instead of barrelling ahead and producing garbage. Anyone who has watched an AI confidently do the wrong thing for ten minutes will understand why that matters. It's launching at a discounted introductory price through the end of the year, which is Google's usual way of saying "please come try this." Alongside it, the older 3.6 Flash and 3.5 Flash-Lite models graduated out of preview into stable, production-ready status — boring news, but the kind that lets companies actually build on them.

On the everyday-user side, two smaller changes are arguably more noticeable. Gemini is now baked directly into Google Chat, replacing the old side panel with an "Ask Gemini" experience that can catch you up on a conversation you missed, draft replies, or pull together tasks and calendar events without you leaving the chat window. That rolled out fully on the web earlier this month and hit mobile on August 17. And on Mac, there's a new dictation trick: hold down the Fn key, talk into whatever window you're in, and Gemini transcribes it into clean, punctuated text — optionally with reasoning switched on so it can tidy up rambling speech into something coherent.

The through-line is that Google is quietly moving Gemini from "app you open" to "layer that's always there." The dictation feature in particular is a small thing that changes habits — once talking into any text box works well, typing starts to feel optional. One housekeeping note for developers: the gemini-robotics-er-1.6-preview model gets switched off on August 31, so if you're building anything on it, now is the time to migrate.

Meta Llama

This Week in Meta Llama

Meta's Llama family was, for years, the reason ordinary developers could run a serious AI model on their own machine instead of renting one from a big company. Meta has since folded that effort into a new model line called Muse, and this stretch has been the most consequential for Meta's open-source story in a long while — the aftershocks of the August 10 announcement were still landing all through the past week.

Here's what happened. Meta released Muse Glimmer, a 30-billion-parameter model, under the Apache 2.0 license — which in plain terms is about as permissive as licenses get. You can download it, modify it, build a business on it, and Meta doesn't get a say. Glimmer is deliberately small enough to run on hardware people actually own: compressed down to under 20 gigabytes, it fits on a single consumer graphics card or a reasonably specced Mac. It reads text and images, speaks over 100 languages, handles a 128,000-token context window, and was built specifically for agent work — coding, digging through documents, calling tools. Think of it less as a chatbot and more as a small, tireless assistant you can keep entirely on your own laptop, with nothing leaving the building.

The bigger surprise came attached to it. Mark Zuckerberg published a 6,500-word essay arguing that America needs to lead on open AI models, and announced that Meta will also release the weights for Muse Spark 1.2 — its most capable model, the proprietary flagship launched back in April. That's a genuine reversal. In May, Meta's chief AI officer Alexandr Wang had said flatly that Muse Spark was "not suitable for open sourcing." The stated motivation is competitive: Chinese open-weight models have been picking up serious momentum, and Meta would rather the world's free, downloadable AI infrastructure came from an American lab.

For most people this won't change anything tomorrow. But it's the kind of move that shapes the next few years — when a frontier-grade model becomes something anyone can download, the floor rises for every startup, researcher, and hobbyist who couldn't afford API bills. Whether the rest of the industry follows Meta back toward openness, or treats this as a one-off strategic play, is the thing worth watching.

Microsoft Copilot

This Week in Microsoft Copilot

Copilot is Microsoft's AI assistant, and it lives basically everywhere Microsoft does — inside Word, Excel, Outlook, Teams, and Windows itself. Think of it as a helper sitting in the corner of every app you already use for school or work. This week the big story wasn't a flashy new trick. It was Microsoft finally cleaning up a mess it made itself.

For the past couple of years there have been two different Copilots: a free consumer one, and a paid work one called Microsoft 365 Copilot. Same name, same logo, totally different apps. Confusing? Extremely. Microsoft has now started rolling out a single unified Copilot app that merges the two, with clearer indicators showing which account you're signed into and a simplified name and icon. The web address is moving from the clunky m365.cloud.microsoft over to copilot.cloud.microsoft. It's the software equivalent of finally merging two junk drawers into one organized one. Alongside that tidying, Microsoft quietly killed a couple of features on August 18: the Podcasts tool, which turned your documents into an AI-narrated audio show, and Group Chats, where several people could talk to Copilot together. Anything made in either one doesn't carry forward, which is a good reminder that AI features can vanish as fast as they appear.

The less glamorous updates are arguably the more useful ones. Copilot's connectors — the pipes that let it read your company's files — now run their content scan and their permissions scan at the same time instead of one after the other, so newly added documents show up in answers noticeably faster. SharePoint gained "authoritative sites," which is a way for an organization to tell Copilot "trust this page over that random old draft." Outlook picked up coaching and meeting prep, so it'll nudge you before you send something snippy and brief you before a call. Copilot in Excel also stopped caring whether autosave is on, which sounds tiny until you're the person it kept refusing to help.

Not everything landed smoothly. Microsoft rolled back a domain exclusion feature it had already announced, and there's an open bug where Copilot buttons simply disappear from Classic Outlook on Windows after a recent update — still unresolved as of August 21. The pattern here is worth noticing: the AI assistant race has shifted from "look what it can do" to "make it actually work reliably in the places people already are." Less magic, more plumbing. That's usually the sign a technology is growing up.

Artlist

This Week in Artlist

Artlist is a subscription library where video creators grab music, sound effects, stock footage, and editing plugins without worrying about copyright strikes. Over the last two years it's been aggressively reinventing itself as an AI video company rather than just a stock catalog. This week, though, things were quiet.

Quiet week for Artlist — no major announcements in the past seven days, and nothing significant in the two weeks before that either. The last notable moves were the launch of Artlist Studio in April, an AI production platform built around shot-by-shot directorial control, letting creators build a scene in layers and swap out a character, a background, or a camera angle independently rather than re-rolling one giant prompt and hoping for the best. That launch came alongside the Artlist Original 1.0 image model, trained on Artlist's own premium catalog, and news that the company had crossed $300 million in annual recurring revenue on 600% new-user growth.

The other half of that story is harder. In June, despite those revenue numbers, Artlist cut roughly 200 jobs — about 40% of its workforce — a year after hiring 120 people. It's a stark example of what's happening across the creative software industry right now: AI tools can generate enormous revenue growth and enormous internal disruption at the same time, and a company can be winning on the scoreboard while shrinking dramatically.

For anyone making videos, the practical takeaway is that Artlist's bet is on control rather than novelty. The pitch is no longer "type a sentence, get a clip" but "direct a scene the way a real production would." Whether that's enough to hold off the giant general-purpose video models is the question worth watching over the next few months.

ChatGPT

This Week in ChatGPT

ChatGPT is OpenAI's chatbot, and by now it's basically the front door to AI for most people — you type something, it types back. This week the interesting stuff wasn't a flashy new brain but a pile of small changes to how the thing actually feels to use, plus one feature getting quietly put out to pasture.

The headline for anyone paying for it: GPT-5.6 Sol now comes with an effort slider for Plus and Pro users. Think of it like the difference between asking a friend for a quick guess versus asking them to sit down and really work it out — you now get to decide which one you want, instead of the model deciding for you. Free and Go users got a bumped-up default model too, along with Think mode arriving on the web, so the "let me actually reason about this" button isn't a paid-tier luxury anymore. Elsewhere OpenAI kept sanding down rough edges: interactive quizzes for people using ChatGPT to study, tighter control over what a Project remembers about you, smoother dictation on Android, pinned chats that finally sync between desktop and iOS, and a public preview of a Linux desktop app for the people who have been asking since roughly forever. On the work side, Apple Messages support landed for ChatGPT Work on Apple silicon Macs, and admins got proper APIs for handling invitations and members instead of clicking through menus one person at a time.

Two things worth circling on a calendar. First, "Sign in with ChatGPT" is now in beta on partner sites like Notion, GitLab, HubSpot, Supabase, Airtable, and Vercel — the same idea as "Sign in with Google," except your ChatGPT account is the key. That's a small feature with a big implication: OpenAI would like to be an identity layer, not just a chat box. Second, the original DALL·E GPT gets retired on August 30. If you have images sitting in there you care about, download them now; image work moves to ChatGPT Images going forward.

None of this is dramatic on its own, but stacked together it reads like a company shifting from "look what the model can do" to "make it not annoying to live with." The effort slider especially — giving users a dial instead of guessing on their behalf — is the kind of change you stop noticing after a week and then desperately miss when it's gone.

Mistral AI

This Week in Mistral AI

Mistral is the French AI company that made its name giving away powerful models for free and being Europe's answer to Silicon Valley. This week it made clear it's chasing something bigger than chatbots — it wants to run the machinery of actual industry, and it's building the buildings to do it.

The centerpiece is an industrial AI stack aimed at aerospace, automotive, and semiconductor engineering, with Airbus, BMW Group, and ASML signed on as customers. Those aren't casual names. ASML makes the machines that make the world's advanced chips, and Airbus builds planes; if your AI is touching that kind of engineering work, you're playing a very different game than writing marketing emails. Backing it up is Mistral's acquisition of Emmi AI, which brings physics-simulation smarts to the table — the difference between an AI that can describe how air flows over a wing and one that can actually model it. Meanwhile the chat product formerly known as Le Chat has been rebranded as Vibe and turned into a single agent that handles both office work and coding: it can catch up on your inbox and calendar, run deep research, draft documents, and also build features and ship reviewable pull requests, with a VS Code integration for developers.

On the model side, two quieter but genuinely useful releases. Shieldstral is a small 3-billion-parameter safety classifier that reads both text and images, spits out calibrated risk scores, runs on a single 16GB graphics card, and ships fully open under Apache 2.0 — basically a free, self-hostable bouncer for anyone building their own AI product. And Leanstral 1.5 improves on Mistral's formal mathematical proof model, with better long-context reasoning for the kind of airtight logic that mathematicians and verification engineers need. Underneath it all, Mistral is opening a 10 MW data center at Les Ulis near Paris this quarter and reportedly poking at designing its own chips.

Put simply, Mistral is betting that European companies will want AI that runs on European soil, built by a European company, on hardware it increasingly controls. That's less about beating GPT at trivia and more about being the plumbing for a continent's industry — a slower race, but a much stickier one to win.

Anthropic Claude Code

This Week in Anthropic Claude Code

Claude Code is the version of Claude that lives inside a programmer's terminal — the plain black window where developers type commands instead of clicking buttons. You describe what you want built or fixed, and it goes off and edits the actual files on your computer. This week was less about one big splashy feature and more about a steady drumbeat of small releases that quietly make the thing tougher and easier to live with.

The headline change landed on August 13, when subagent forking became the default. Subagents are helper versions of Claude that get sent off to handle a side task, and until now each one started cold, with no idea what the main conversation had been about. A "fork" inherits the whole conversation and the cached memory of it — imagine handing a new colleague the full meeting notes instead of a sticky note. It makes helpers faster and cheaper, because nothing has to be re-explained from scratch. Version 2.1.233 followed the next day with GitLab merge-request support in the worktree view and, more usefully for anyone who has ever watched their laptop fans scream, memory limits that stop a runaway build command from freezing the whole machine. Version 2.1.234 arrived on August 17. Alongside those, the tool picked up an ANTHROPIC_DEFAULT_MODEL setting so you can pin which model new sessions start on, cross-session idle notifications that ping you when a long job finishes, and tighter macOS sandbox protections that keep Claude fenced into the folders it is supposed to touch.

Round that out with the polish: optional spellcheck in the prompt box, more readable terminal text and list rendering, faster startup, better screen-reader support in VS Code, quicker background cloud sessions, and a long list of squashed bugs around sessions, permissions, dialogs, and slash commands. On the business side, Anthropic's Compliance API coverage was extended to both Claude Code and Cowork on August 11, which is the sort of unglamorous plumbing that lets big companies actually approve the tool for their staff.

The pattern here is a product growing up. Nobody writes headlines about memory limits and sandbox rules, but those are exactly the features that decide whether a tool stays a weekend toy or becomes something you trust with real work every day. Claude Code is clearly aiming for the second one.

xAI Grok

This Week in xAI Grok

Grok is Elon Musk's AI assistant, built by his company xAI and wired directly into X, the site formerly called Twitter. It has always leaned on being fast, cheap, and a bit less buttoned-up than its rivals. This week it leaned hard into all three.

The big one was Grok 4.6 landing on Amazon Bedrock on August 19. Bedrock is Amazon's cloud menu of AI models, so this puts xAI's flagship in front of every business already building on AWS without them having to sign a separate deal. Grok 4.6 itself only launched on August 12, and it is a genuinely serious model: a 500,000-token context window, which means you can hand it something the length of a couple of novels and it keeps the whole thing in mind, plus dial-able "reasoning effort" from low all the way up to xhigh, so you choose between quick-and-cheap and slow-and-careful depending on the job. Pricing is the sharp end of the pitch — roughly $2 per million words in and $6 per million out, which undercuts rival frontier models by around half and puts real pressure on everyone else's API bills.

On the creative side, Grok Imagine's Image 2.0 is now the default Quality Mode on grok.com/imagine and in the iOS and Android apps. It follows instructions more precisely, renders text inside images far more legibly — long an embarrassing weak spot for image generators — and handles dense multi-part layouts, smart resizing, and multiple reference images. Musk also confirmed on August 9 that it now does native meme generation, which is either the most or least serious feature shipped this month depending on your outlook. Grok Build, the coding side of the house, picked up searchable sessions, better handling of queued messages, sturdier subagents, cleaner permissions, and smoother copy-paste and shortcut behaviour on Linux and Windows.

What is coming next is arguably bigger than what shipped. Grok 4.7, a 2.1-trillion-parameter model, has finished initial training, and Musk says xAI is now feeding it a "massive amount" of SpaceX engineering data — a genuinely unusual training ingredient nobody else has. It was billed as three to four weeks out and is already slipping a little. Grok 5 is still targeted before the end of 2026. For anyone watching costs, the takeaway is simple: xAI has decided the way to win is to be very good and very cheap at the same time, and the rest of the field now has to answer that.

Anthropic Claude Cowork

This Week in Anthropic Claude Cowork

Claude Cowork is Anthropic's attempt to make an AI assistant that actually does your work instead of just talking about it. You hand it a messy multi-step job — sort these files, pull that data, write this report — and it goes off and does it. Until recently, though, it had one big limitation: it lived on your desktop, and if you closed your laptop, the work stopped. This week that changed in a fairly big way.

On Tuesday, Anthropic announced that Cowork has finished rolling out to mobile and web for everyone on a paid plan. That means you can kick off a task on your computer in the morning, close the lid, and check on it from your phone at lunch. The reason this works is a quieter change underneath: Cowork sessions can now run remotely, on Anthropic's servers, rather than only on your machine. Think of it less like a program you run and more like a colleague who keeps working after you leave the office. That rolls together with news from a week earlier, when Cowork moved into the Claude for Chrome extension — the browser side panel is now a full Cowork session, with your saved skills and connected apps coming along for the ride, and conversations saved to the same history. Max and Team subscribers got that first, with Pro plans following in the coming weeks. There was also a business-flavored update: Anthropic extended its Compliance API to cover both Cowork and Claude Code, so company security teams can pull session records for audits without turning off the tool entirely.

Put together, the theme of the past week is portability. Cowork started as a desktop app and is quickly becoming something closer to a service that follows you between devices. The practical upside is that long-running jobs — the kind that take twenty minutes of searching and file-shuffling — stop being tied to whether your laptop is awake. The trade-off worth watching is that more of your work is now running on someone else's computer, which is exactly why the compliance plumbing showed up at the same time.

None of this makes for a dramatic headline, but it's the pattern of a company shifting from demo to infrastructure. The exciting-voice-clone phase is over; what ElevenLabs is building now is the boring, reliable layer that businesses actually put in front of customers.

OpenAI Astra

Astra is OpenAI's next major model family, and it announced itself in an unusual way: instead of a demo video, OpenAI posted ten mathematical proofs to GitHub, each solving a problem that had gone unsolved for at least a decade. The problems span group theory, coding theory, quantum complexity, and high-dimensional geometry, and one of them settles a question the mathematician Mikhail Gromov posed back in 1999. What makes Astra different is its shape. Rather than one model thinking very hard, Astra is a team — a root agent splits a problem into pieces, spins up subagents to work on each piece, waits for them to report back, and stitches the results together. That design is built for problems that take hours or days rather than seconds. The proofs weren't just accepted on faith either: they were written in Lean, a formal proof language that mechanically checks every logical step, so a computer verified the reasoning rather than a human hoping it looked right. The token cost for all ten solutions came to roughly $2,000. OpenAI hasn't said when Astra ships, what it will cost, or whether it becomes GPT-6. But the message landed: AI is starting to produce new mathematics, not just recite old mathematics.

Anthropic Claude

This Week in Anthropic Claude

Claude is the AI assistant built by Anthropic, and it's the one people tend to reach for when they want careful writing, long documents, or actual work done rather than just a chat. This week was less about flashy new abilities and more about the plumbing — the stuff that decides whether big companies and governments are allowed to use it at all. And one very public hiccup.

The headline change is that Anthropic started watermarking Claude's text. Think of it like an invisible signature woven into the words — you can't see it, it doesn't make the writing worse or slower, and it costs nothing extra, but a detector can spot it later and say "yes, a Claude model wrote this." That's Anthropic getting ahead of Europe's AI Act, which is pushing every AI company to label machine-made content. A public detection tool is on the way, and older Claude models will get the same treatment over the next few months. In the same compliance spirit, Anthropic stretched its Compliance API to cover Cowork and Claude Code across desktop, web, mobile and the command line, so a company's security team can pull one clean record of everything an employee did with Claude — useful for audits, and frankly the kind of boring feature that unlocks giant contracts. Claude for Government also opened in beta, and the interesting wrinkle is that Anthropic bills agencies directly, so a government office doesn't have to sign a separate cloud deal just to get started.

Not everything went smoothly. On Saturday evening Claude went down hard — Claude.ai, Claude Code and Cowork all fell over around 21:58 UTC, and service didn't come back until roughly 22:40. Forty-odd minutes isn't catastrophic, but it was a reminder that a lot of people now treat Claude the way they treat electricity, and notice loudly when it flickers. Meanwhile the connector directory quietly crossed 950 MCP servers, which is the count of outside apps Claude can plug into.

Put it together and you get a picture of a product growing up. Watermarks, audit logs and government contracts aren't the fun part of AI, but they're what turns a clever tool into infrastructure — and the outage was a small preview of what it means when that infrastructure blinks.

Perplexity AI

This Week in Perplexity AI

Perplexity started life as a search engine that answers your question in a paragraph instead of handing you ten blue links, and it has spent the last year turning into something much stranger and more ambitious: an AI that actually does things on your computer. This week pushed further down that road.

The biggest move was in the Agent API, where Perplexity added support for a fresh batch of outside models — Claude Opus 4.7, GPT-5.5 and Grok 4.20 Reasoning. That's worth pausing on, because it says something about Perplexity's whole strategy: rather than betting everything on one homegrown brain, it acts as a switchboard, routing your question to whichever model is best at it. Alongside that, the Perplexity MCP server got one-click installation for Cursor, VS Code, Claude Desktop and Claude Code, bundling four tools — search, ask, research and reason. In plain terms, if you're working inside another AI app, you can now bolt Perplexity's research ability onto it in about ten seconds instead of fiddling with config files. Personal Computer also landed properly on Mac, with simpler controls and better memory of your own context, including a prebuilt "Personal CFO" that pokes through your financial life and reports back.

Hovering over all of this is the legal news from a couple of weeks back that still shapes everything: the Ninth Circuit threw out Amazon's injunction against Perplexity's Comet shopping agent, reasoning that when Comet browses Amazon on your behalf, it's really you visiting Amazon. It's the first appeals-court ruling on whether an AI agent counts as "accessing" a website, and for now it's a green light for the whole agentic browsing idea.

So Perplexity is quietly building the thing a lot of people assumed would take years: an assistant that reads the web, opens your files, works inside your apps, and now legally gets to shop on your behalf. Whether you find that thrilling or slightly unnerving probably depends on how much you enjoy delegating.

Google Gemini

This Week in Google Gemini

Gemini is Google's AI assistant — the thing that answers your questions, writes your emails, and increasingly lives inside almost every Google product you already use. This past week it hit a number that's hard to ignore: on August 11, the Gemini app crossed one billion monthly users. To put that in perspective, that's roughly one in eight people on the planet opening the app at least once a month. Two years ago it was a curiosity. Now it's plumbing.

Google also taught Live Transcribe to read American Sign Language through the Pixel camera and turn it into text on screen — point the phone at someone signing, and you get words. That's the kind of feature that quietly changes someone's day-to-day life rather than just showing up nicely in a demo.

Underneath the consumer polish, the engine got swapped out too. On August 13, a model called Gemini 3.7 Flash became the brains behind Gemini Spark for AI Pro and Ultra subscribers across more than 160 countries — "Flash" being Google's shorthand for the fast, cheap tier, the sports-car-that-sips-fuel of their model lineup. Meanwhile Gemini kept creeping deeper into Workspace: it now automatically turns a meeting into a Google Doc with a summary, action items, and full transcript, and there's a Gemini panel in Sheets where you can just describe the spreadsheet you want instead of hunting for the right formula. The billion-user milestone is less about Google winning a race and more about a threshold being crossed, where using AI stops feeling like a deliberate choice and starts feeling like using the internet.

Meta Llama

This Week in Meta Llama

Meta's Llama models made their name by being free — anyone could download the weights, the actual guts of the model, and run them on their own hardware. That made Llama the default for hobbyists, universities, and startups who couldn't or wouldn't pipe everything through someone else's servers. This year has been complicated for that story, because Meta's new Superintelligence Labs shipped a flagship model called Muse Spark and kept it closed. So the big question hanging over Llama has been whether Meta was walking away from open source altogether.

Last week gave a partial answer, and it was a genuinely interesting one. On August 10, Meta Superintelligence Labs released Muse Glimmer under the Apache 2.0 license — about as permissive as licenses get — with the weights sitting ungated on Hugging Face, meaning no forms, no waiting list, just download. It's a 30-billion-parameter multimodal model, so it reads both text and images, handles a 128,000-token context window (roughly a long novel's worth of material in one go), and works across 100-plus languages. The trick is that Glimmer was distilled from the much larger, closed Muse Spark: think of an expert teacher training a smaller student to imitate their answers, so you get most of the skill in a fraction of the size.

That size choice is the real story. Glimmer runs on a consumer graphics card with 24–32 GB of memory, or on a Mac if you squeeze it down to 4-bit precision — and it landed in Ollama almost immediately for local agent work on Apple Silicon. In other words, a coding assistant or personal agent that lives entirely on your laptop, with nothing leaving the machine and no per-token bill. Meta explicitly aimed it at agent workloads, the kind of AI that takes multiple steps on your behalf rather than just replying to a prompt. Zuckerberg also said Muse Spark 1.2's weights will be open-sourced, though as of August 13 nothing had actually appeared.

So Meta seems to be settling into a split strategy: keep the biggest model behind the curtain, give away the capable-but-compact version that anyone can actually run. For most people building things, that second category is the useful one — and it's worth noticing that the Llama brand itself is quietly fading into the Muse name.
