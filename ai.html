🔍 Tool Spotlight

Jais 3

Most big AI models are built mainly around English, and they can feel a bit clumsy in other languages. Jais 3 is a different kind of project. It is a family of six open AI models built to be truly at home in Arabic, released in mid-October by the Institute of Foundation Models at MBZUAI in Abu Dhabi and a company called Inception42, with chip maker Cerebras helping out. Think of it as a whole toolbox instead of one tool: the models run from a tiny 0.9 billion parameters, small enough for a phone, up to a giant 375 billion parameter version meant for serious GPU clusters. There is also Jais Speech, which turns spoken Arabic into text.

What makes it interesting is that these models are meant to act like assistants, not just chatbots. They can plan several steps, search the web, and work with documents, spreadsheets and slides in Arabic. The makers say the 7 billion parameter model jumped from a score of 56.4 to 79.6 on their Arabic tests, though that is their own testing. Schools, businesses and developers across the Arab world are the obvious users, and because the weights are reportedly open, anyone can study or build on them. The surprising part is how it was made: it adapts an earlier model family called K2 Horizon, showing that one strong base can be taught new languages.

🔍 Tool Spotlight

🔍 Tool Spotlight: Reflection Beam

Most of the world’s smartest AI models are locked behind a company’s servers. Reflection Beam is a rare exception. Released on October 16 by the AI lab Reflection, it is the company’s first open-weight model, meaning anyone can download the whole thing and run it themselves. It is aimed at coding, reasoning and long-running “agent” jobs where an AI works on a task for a while on its own.

The numbers are huge. Beam has about 501 billion parameters, but it is a “mixture of experts,” so only around 23 billion switch on for each word it writes, a bit like calling in only the specialists you need for a job. It can read a million tokens at once, roughly several novels’ worth. Reflection reports 81.6% on SWE-bench Verified, a test of fixing real software bugs, though those scores come from the company itself and have not been independently confirmed.

The surprise is the license. It is Apache 2.0, which lets people use it commercially with almost no strings attached, unlike some rivals that add revenue limits or credit requirements. The catch: the full-size files weigh nearly a terabyte, so you need a serious stack of GPUs. Most people will try it through hosted services instead. Developers and companies that want a powerful, American-built model they fully control are the obvious audience.

🔍 Tool Spotlight

🔍 Tool Spotlight: Mercury 3

Most AI chatbots write one word, or word-piece, at a time, left to right, like someone typing. Mercury 3, released October 19 by a company called Inception, does it differently. It is a "diffusion" language model, which starts with a rough draft of the whole answer and cleans it up in several passes, a bit like a photo coming into focus. That trick is why it is so fast: Inception says it produces about 1,068 tokens (small chunks of text) per second on Nvidia chips and handles a million tokens of context, roughly several long novels at once. The company also claims it beats rivals like Claude Haiku 5.5 while running four to eight times faster, but those numbers are self-reported and nobody outside has tested them yet. It is available only through an API, including via OpenRouter, Baseten and Vercel, so it is aimed at developers building things like web-browsing agents, live search and customer support, where waiting a few seconds feels forever. The surprising part is the bigger claim: Inception says Mercury 3 is more than 40 times larger than its first model, and argues that diffusion models improve with size just as ordinary ones do.
