Microsoft Copilot

This Week in Microsoft Copilot

Microsoft Copilot is the AI assistant baked right into Word, Excel, PowerPoint, Outlook, and the rest of Microsoft 365 — it's probably the AI most people at a typical office job are already sitting next to, whether they've noticed it or not.

This week Copilot picked up a genuinely handy shortcut: you can now @mention the Word, Excel, or PowerPoint agent directly inside Copilot Chat, and it'll go build the document, spreadsheet, or slide deck for you without you ever opening the actual app. It's a bit like texting an assistant "make me a slide deck on this" instead of opening PowerPoint yourself and staring at a blank page. Under the hood, Copilot also got quietly smarter, adding OpenAI's newest GPT-5.6 models to power its reasoning, which Microsoft says helps it handle multi-step tasks — the kind of "do these five things in this exact order" requests that used to trip AI assistants up halfway through. And it's not just text anymore, either: Copilot can now pull rich images straight out of your files and meeting recordings and show them inline in its answers, so if you ask what a chart looked like in last week's deck, it just shows you instead of trying to describe it in words.

On the business side, Microsoft also rolled out governed agent publishing (so companies can vet custom AI agents before they go live across a team), tenant-wide prompt galleries, and wider access to outside tools through MCP, the connector standard a lot of AI platforms are now building around. Put together, it's Microsoft nudging Copilot from "chatbot you ask questions" toward "coworker who quietly does the task while you're doing something else."

🔍 Tool Spotlight

Ideogram Object Remover

Ideogram, the AI image company, just launched a tool called Object Remover, and it does exactly what it sounds like: you upload a photo, brush over whatever you want gone — a stranger photobombing your vacation shot, a stray power line, a logo, a typo in a sign — and it vanishes. No typing a prompt, no cloning-and-stamping like old-school Photoshop. The tool looks at the pixels around the erased spot and rebuilds what should be there, matching shadows, reflections, grain, and even the way light falls across the scene, so the fix doesn't look like a fix.

What makes it a genuine standout rather than just another editing gimmick is that it's been benchmarked against the competition — including Google's Nano Banana 2, Adobe-adjacent tools like FLUX Erase, and OpenAI's image models — and came out on top for removal quality, while also being the cheapest option per use. That combination of "best and cheapest" is rare in AI tools, which usually make you pick one.

It's built with photographers and online sellers in mind — anyone who needs a clean product shot or a real-estate listing photo without clutter — but honestly, anyone with a camera roll full of almost-perfect vacation pictures will find a use for it. The surprising part is how invisible the edits are: there's no telltale blur or smudge, just an object that was there, and then isn't.

Microsoft Copilot

This Week in Microsoft Copilot

Microsoft Copilot is the AI assistant Microsoft has been stitching into basically everything — Word, Excel, PowerPoint, Outlook, Teams, even Windows itself. Alongside that, Microsoft rolled out governed agent publishing and tenant-wide prompt galleries, meaning a whole company can now share a library of pre-written prompts and vetted agents instead of everyone reinventing the wheel. Copilot also picked up broader access to MCP agents — a kind of universal plug for connecting AI to outside tools — across Word, Excel, PowerPoint, Outlook, and Catalyst, plus tighter controls over which connectors and permissions those agents can actually touch.

On top of that, Copilot Chat now serves up richer Bing web-answer cards directly in conversation, there's a new customizable landing page for an Employee Self-Service agent, fresh admin controls for AI video generation, and easier one-click sharing of agents straight into Teams. Microsoft even renamed its Copilot Studio certification track to "Microsoft 365 Copilot specialization," swapping out an old admin exam for new agent-building credentials, which tells you where the company thinks the real skill gap is right now.

Put together, this is Microsoft betting hard that the future isn't one giant AI, but thousands of small, purpose-built agents that regular employees build and share like office memos. If that bet pays off, using AI at work starts looking less like typing into a chatbot and more like browsing an internal app store for exactly the helper you need.
