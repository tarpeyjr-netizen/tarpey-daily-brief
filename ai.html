ChatGPT

This Week in ChatGPT

ChatGPT is the chatbot that started the whole AI boom — you type a question, it types back, and these days it can also write your code, read your files and click around the web for you. This week it got the biggest upgrade it's had in a long while, because OpenAI's brand-new flagship model, GPT-6 "Astra," finished rolling out to everyday users after landing on September 3rd.

Astra is built for a different kind of work than the old chatbots. Instead of just answering questions, it's designed to actually operate a computer — browsing sites, writing and debugging software, and grinding through long, multi-step projects without losing the thread. The benchmark numbers are the sort that make researchers sit up: it scored 98% on FrontierMath Tier 4, a test of genuinely brutal research-level math problems, and 99.9% on ARC-AGI-3, a puzzle benchmark specifically built to be easy for humans and hard for machines. Those tests were supposed to last years. Astra basically finished them. The practical version of that power is quieter but arguably more useful: Astra can now build documents, spreadsheets and slide decks that follow your templates and formatting rules, and it adjusts when you change your mind halfway through instead of starting over.

Around the big model launch, OpenAI shipped a handful of smaller things that matter if you actually live in the app. The desktop version finally arrived on Linux in public preview — Ubuntu, Debian and Fedora — which closes a gap that had annoyed developers for years. Projects got a memory switch you can flip after the fact, so you can tell ChatGPT to only remember things from inside a specific project and stay ignorant of everything else, which is handy when you don't want your work notes bleeding into your dinner-recipe chats. And Zendesk and OneNote connections went into beta, letting ChatGPT reach into your support tickets and your notebooks.

The bigger picture: the pitch for ChatGPT has officially shifted from "ask it things" to "hand it work." A model that can run a browser and produce a finished spreadsheet is less like a search engine and more like an intern. That's genuinely useful, and it's also the reason the safety and oversight conversation is getting louder — the more independently these systems can act, the more it matters that someone is checking what they did.

Mistral AI

This Week in Mistral AI

Mistral is the French startup that became Europe's answer to OpenAI, best known for releasing powerful models that anyone can download and run themselves rather than renting through someone else's website. This week it made headlines for money first and technology second — on September 8th it closed a €3 billion funding round that values the company at more than €21 billion.

That's not just a big number, it's a record: the largest equity raise any European tech company has ever pulled off, and Mistral is only three years old. Samsung Electronics led the round, joined by the Scaleup Europe Fund (managed by EQT) and existing backer PSG Equity. The Samsung involvement is the detail worth noticing — a company that puts chips and screens in hundreds of millions of phones and TVs now has a serious stake in a European AI lab. Mistral says the money goes toward research and infrastructure, which in this industry mostly means buying an enormous amount of computing hardware, since training frontier models is less like writing software and more like running a power-hungry factory.

The engineering team wasn't idle either. Mistral OCR 4.1 became generally available, meaning its tool for reading text out of scanned documents and photos is now stable enough for real business use. It also shipped Leanstral 1.5, a niche but fascinating model that writes formal mathematical proofs in a language called Lean 4 — proofs a computer can verify line by line, so there's no hand-waving. And it launched Agentic Search, a retrieval layer that helps AI systems dig through complicated documents more accurately while using fewer steps and fewer tokens, which in plain terms means faster answers that cost less.

Put together, it's a strong week for the argument that the AI race isn't purely an American story. Mistral now has record-breaking European money, a hardware giant in its corner, and a product line aimed squarely at businesses that want AI they can inspect and run on their own terms.

Anthropic Claude

This Week in Anthropic Claude

Claude is the AI assistant made by Anthropic, a company that has built its whole reputation on the idea that a chatbot should be careful, honest, and actually good at long, messy work rather than just quick answers. That reputation got a serious workout this week, because Anthropic shipped a new top-end model and quietly rewired how people pay for the old one.

The headline is Fable 5.1, which landed on September 1st and is aimed squarely at the hard stuff — long-running agents that grind away at a task for hours, heavy coding jobs, multi-step research, and the kind of document-drowning professional work that eats afternoons. It carries a one-million-token context window, which in plain terms means you can hand it something roughly the length of a bookshelf and it keeps the whole thing in mind at once, and it can write back up to 128,000 tokens in a single go. Its sibling, Mythos 5.1, arrived alongside it and is now the engine behind Claude Security, the tool that reads through a company's codebase hunting for vulnerabilities and proposing patches for a human to approve. Think of it as a very patient security guard who has read every line of the building's blueprints.

Money moved too, and not entirely in users' favor. The promotional pricing on Claude Sonnet 5 — $2 in, $10 out per million tokens — expired on August 31st, so September 1st brought the standard $3/$15 rate. Anthropic softened the blow elsewhere: the temporary 50% Claude Code discount running through September 14th is being swapped for something permanent, a 25% bump in Claude Code rate limits that doesn't expire. On the enterprise side, Anthropic announced Enterprise Frontier Safeguards, built with input from more than a hundred customers in banking, healthcare, manufacturing and government, pairing zero data retention with misuse detection across Claude Code, Claude Enterprise, the Claude Platform, Amazon Bedrock, Google's Agent Platform and Microsoft Foundry. And the desktop app picked up a built-in browser, a side panel where Claude can open a website, read it, click things and type — rolling out to Pro, Max, Team and Enterprise users.

The most interesting move might be the least commercial one. Claude for Teachers is a free Enterprise-grade offering for schools and districts, with centrally managed access, single sign-on, role-based controls, and teaching tools grounded in learning science. Crucially, it comes with a K-12 data processing agreement promising that student information stays protected and never trains a model. Taken together, the week reads like a company trying to be two things at once: the most capable engine for professionals who'll pay for it, and a trusted default for institutions that can't afford to gamble.

Perplexity AI

This Week in Perplexity AI

Perplexity started life as a search engine that answers your question in a paragraph instead of handing you ten blue links, always with sources attached so you can check its homework. Lately it has been busy becoming something much stranger and more ambitious — less a place you visit, more a thing that lives on your computer and works while you're not looking.

The clearest example arrived September 1st: Hybrid Compute on Mac. Instead of shipping every question off to a data center, Perplexity now splits the job, letting small models running directly on your Mac handle some of the subtasks while the cloud takes the heavy lifting. Supported local models include Gemma 4 for machines with 16GB of memory, and Qwen3.6 plus Perplexity's own model on 32GB machines. The practical upside is privacy — sensitive files and information can stay on the laptop instead of crossing the internet. It's a bit like having a decent cook at home who can manage the simple dishes and only calls the restaurant for the complicated ones.

That idea gets pushed to its logical extreme with Personal Computer, an always-on AI that lives on a dedicated Mac mini and stitches together your local files, apps and sessions with Perplexity's cloud. The pitch is a digital proxy that works around the clock, watching for triggers, kicking off tasks on its own and carrying work forward overnight so there's progress waiting when you sit down. Meanwhile Comet, Perplexity's AI-native browser, went Enterprise — IT departments can now deploy it through MDM and set browser policies, with the Comet Assistant doing in-page research, summarizing, and running autonomous multi-step errands. Perplexity's Computer also plugged into Microsoft 365, so Word, Excel, PowerPoint, Outlook and Teams get the same treatment, complete with a live context panel showing what the AI is currently doing and cited sources on every answer. There's even Computer in Email, letting you start or continue a session straight from an email thread.

Underneath all that, Perplexity widened its model bench — GPT-5.6 Terra and Luna, Grok 4.6, DeepSeek V4 Pro, Kimi K3 and NVIDIA Nemotron 3.5 Lightning are all now selectable — and reported that its Search as Code optimizations lifted execution reliability from 81.9% to 92.6% while cutting per-task cost by 8%. That last number is the unglamorous one, but it matters most: an assistant that runs unattended is only useful if it finishes what it starts. Perplexity is betting that the future of AI isn't a chat box you talk to, but a coworker quietly clearing your desk overnight.

Google Gemini

This Week in Google Gemini

Gemini is Google's AI assistant — the thing that answers your questions, writes your emails, and increasingly sits quietly inside Search, Gmail, and Android doing work you never asked it to do. It just crossed a billion monthly users, which makes it less a product and more a piece of plumbing. And this week Google kept laying pipe.

The headline is Gemini 3.7 Flash, the newest of Google's fast-and-cheap models, which is now sometimes powering the AI Mode answers at the top of Google Search. "Flash" is Google's word for the model that trades a little raw brainpower for a lot of speed, and 3.7 leans hard into coding and so-called agentic work — the kind where you hand the AI a goal instead of a question and it goes off and does several steps on its own. Google is offering it at an introductory price through the end of the year, which is the AI industry's version of a supermarket loss leader: get developers hooked while it's cheap. Alongside it came a small fleet of specialists. Lyria 3 writes music, either 30-second clips or full-length songs. Veo 3.1 Lite makes video on the cheap, aimed at people who need to generate a hundred drafts rather than one masterpiece. Gemini 3.5 Transcribe turns speech into clean, punctuated text in real time. And Gemini 3.1 Flash Live handles voice-in, voice-out conversation, which is the piece you need if you want an assistant that actually talks back without an awkward pause.

The more practical changes landed in Google Workspace, where Gemini has become less a chatbot and more an appliance. Ask Gemini is now built directly into Google Chat, replacing the old side panel, so you can search old conversations, draft replies, and catch up on a thread you ignored for three days without leaving the app. Google Meet's hardware controllers — the physical touch panels in conference rooms — can now start and stop the "Take notes for me" feature, meaning the people actually in the room finally control the AI note-taker instead of whoever dialed in from home. Small thing, but it's the sort of detail that decides whether people trust the tool.

Put it together and the direction is clear: Google has stopped selling Gemini as a destination you visit and started wiring it into places you already are. The interesting question for the rest of the year isn't whether the models get smarter — they will — but whether a billion people notice or care that the assistant is now handling their notes, their inbox, and the answer above their search results.

Meta Llama

This Week in Meta Llama

Llama is Meta's family of AI models, and its whole claim to fame was being open — you could download the thing, run it on your own machine, and nobody could take it away from you. That made it the backbone of an enormous amount of independent AI work. Then Meta reorganized, went quieter and more proprietary, and the open-source crowd started getting nervous. This week the story got more interesting again.

The center of gravity has shifted to Meta Superintelligence Labs and its Muse line, which is where the company's best work now lives. Muse Spark 1.3 shipped on September 2nd, the fourth version in five months — 1.0 in April, then July, then August, now September. That's a brutal release cadence, and it tells you Meta is sprinting. Spark 1.2, the previous version, hit 82.9% on Terminal-Bench, a test that measures whether a model can actually operate a computer terminal and finish a long, multi-step job rather than just talk about it. It also carries a million-token context window, which in plain terms means you can hand it an entire codebase and it keeps the whole thing in its head at once.

The part that matters most is a promise rather than a product. Mark Zuckerberg has said Meta will open the weights for Muse Spark 1.2, releasing it under a modified Llama Community License so anyone can download and run it. "Weights" are the model's actual learned parameters — the finished brain, not just a rented seat at the API. That's a real reversal from Meta's drift toward closed development, and Zuckerberg is framing it as a bet that America should lead on open AI. The catch: there's still no date. Meanwhile Meta did ship something you can grab today, Muse Glimmer, a 30-billion-parameter model released on Hugging Face under the genuinely permissive Apache 2.0 license, small enough to run on a single consumer GPU.

So Meta is currently running two strategies at once — a fast, closed, top-tier model you pay for, and an open one you can put on your own hardware. Whether the Spark 1.2 weights actually appear, and how quickly, is the thing to watch. A promise with no date attached is worth exactly as much as the company's willingness to keep it.

Microsoft Copilot

This Week in Microsoft Copilot

Copilot is Microsoft's AI assistant, and if you've used Word, Excel, Teams or Outlook in the last couple of years you've probably seen its little icon hovering in a corner waiting to help. The big story this week is that Microsoft has finally started fixing something that confused almost everybody: there were two Copilots. One was the free consumer app you'd download on your phone, and the other was "Microsoft 365 Copilot," the paid work version. Same name, same logo, different apps. As of mid-September the company has begun a worldwide rollout that merges them into a single unified app on Windows and Mac, with refreshed branding to match. Think of it like two nearly identical stores on the same street finally combining into one.

Underneath the rebrand, the features arriving this month are the practical kind rather than the flashy kind. Deep citations are reaching general availability, which means when Copilot tells you something it pulled from a Word document or a PowerPoint deck, you can click straight through to the exact paragraph or slide it used — no more taking the robot's word for it and hunting through a 60-page file yourself. Structured document generation is landing too, letting teams build contracts, proposals and HR paperwork from form-driven SharePoint templates, so instead of typing a prompt and hoping, you fill in a few boxes and get a properly formatted document out the other end. Planner support inside Copilot Cowork is also going live, meaning you can view and update plans, buckets, goals and tasks without leaving the chat. And carrying over from August, Copilot in Excel can now write and run Python, which quietly turns the spreadsheet into a genuine data-analysis tool for people who have never opened a coding editor in their lives.

There's a business number worth noting alongside all this: Microsoft closed out its 2026 fiscal year with roughly 30 million paid Copilot seats, and the count of customers buying 50,000-plus seats at a time grew more than sevenfold year over year. That's a lot of people whose workday now includes an AI. Not everything is growing, though — the Podcasts feature was retired from Copilot on August 18, a reminder that Microsoft is pruning as fast as it's planting. On the partner side, a limited-time "Copilot in 30" trial for Microsoft 365 Copilot Business is now available through cloud resellers until the end of December.

The through-line here is consolidation. Microsoft spent two years throwing Copilot at every surface it owns, and 2026 looks like the year it starts tidying up: one app instead of two, answers you can verify instead of just trust, and templates instead of blank-page prompting. For an ordinary user, the most useful change is probably the least exciting one — being able to click a citation and see where the answer actually came from.

Artlist

This Week in Artlist

Artlist started life as a subscription library for royalty-free music — the place YouTubers and small studios went to license a soundtrack without a lawyer. Over the past two years it has quietly turned into something much bigger: a full AI video production platform. That transformation is the backdrop to a very quiet news week.

Quiet week for Artlist — no major announcements in the past seven days, and nothing significant when we widened the search to fourteen days either. The only current item is a seasonal promotion offering discounted plans with two extra months free.

The last genuinely notable moves came earlier in the year and they were substantial. In April, Artlist launched Artlist Studio, an AI production platform built to mimic how a real film set works — you cast characters, pick locations and choose camera angles rather than just typing a prompt and hoping the machine gets it right. That approach targets generative video's most stubborn weakness: keeping the same face, the same room and the same lighting consistent from shot to shot. The Studio sits on top of an AI Toolkit that bundles image, video and voiceover generation in one workspace, wired to outside models including Veo 3.1, Sora 2 and the Kling family, alongside Artlist's own cinematic image model, Artlist Original 1.0.

The company also announced it had crossed $300 million in annual recurring revenue on the back of roughly 600% new-user growth in the first quarter. But growth came with a hard edge: in June, Artlist said it would lay off about 200 of its 500 staff as part of a shift to what it called an "AI-native operating model." It's one of the starker examples going of a company using AI to expand its product and shrink its payroll at the same time — and a story worth watching more closely than any single week's feature release.

SAP Joule

This Week in SAP Joule

SAP runs the boring-but-critical software that huge companies use to track money, inventory, and staff — and Joule is the AI assistant SAP bolted onto all of it. Think of it as a chat window that sits on top of a giant filing cabinet and actually knows where everything is. This week the story wasn't a flashy launch so much as a steady drumbeat of Joule quietly showing up in more places.

The biggest thread is Joule Studio, SAP's tool for building your own AI agents. SAP had a rough first swing at it — the original version leaned too hard on drag-and-drop and real developers found it limiting — so the company rebuilt it. The managed version now lets you build agents in plain Python, or right inside coding tools like Cursor and Claude Code, and then hand them to SAP to run. Popular agent frameworks like LangGraph and AutoGen plug straight in. It's in early access now with general availability targeted for this quarter, and SAP is giving away design-time access free through the end of 2026, which is a pretty loud way of saying "please, come try this."

Underneath all of it, Anthropic's Claude has been named the primary reasoning model powering Joule across SAP's Autonomous Suite — SAP essentially picked a brain for its agents and went with an outside one. Meanwhile the practical stuff keeps shipping: a batch of Joule assistants for procurement (category management, supplier management, requisitions, buying, receiving, services) is slated to hit general availability this month, and next-gen SAP Ariba Buying now bundles Joule agents that handle purchasing and policy checks instead of making someone click through five different screens. On the customer side, consulting firm ABeam — roughly 9,600 people across 29 offices — went public about using Joule for Consultants to speed up SAP project delivery.

The through-line is that SAP has stopped treating AI as a demo and started treating it as plumbing. If you work anywhere that runs SAP, the change you'll actually notice isn't a chatbot — it's that the purchase request you used to chase for three days starts moving on its own.

Einstein AI

This Week in Einstein AI

Einstein is Salesforce's AI layer — the thing that scores your sales leads, drafts your follow-up emails, and guesses which deals are about to fall apart. For years it was the quiet helper inside the world's biggest customer-database company. Lately it's been absorbed into something much more ambitious called Agentforce, and this week made that shift impossible to ignore.

The headline is Claudeforce, a big expanded partnership between Salesforce and Anthropic. It works in two directions, which is the clever part. Going one way, Claude becomes a reasoning model available across Salesforce's AI stack — the Atlas Reasoning Engine, Agentforce Vibes, Agentforce Coworker, and Agent Builder all get access to it. Going the other way, Salesforce shows up as a plugin inside Claude itself, with 37 prebuilt sales skills that let a salesperson pull up live deal data, update a pipeline, or take action without ever opening Salesforce. Pilot customers have it now and an open beta is expected this month. Salesforce is backing it with real money: roughly $300 million in Anthropic tokens across 2026, on top of an equity stake valued around $5 billion. Investors liked it — the stock jumped hard on the news.

It's worth being clear about what this means for Einstein specifically. Einstein isn't dead or deprecated, but Salesforce's roadmap money is flowing to Agentforce, which includes everything Einstein did plus autonomous agents, multi-agent orchestration, and human-in-the-loop controls. The next Agentforce Sales release lands this month, though Salesforce hasn't detailed the feature list yet.

The bigger picture: Salesforce is betting that the CRM of the future isn't a screen you log into, it's an agent you talk to. Putting a rival lab's model at the center of that bet — rather than insisting on homegrown AI — says a lot about how fast this field is moving.

🔍 Tool Spotlight

Anthropic's Model Hardware Standard (MHS)

Most AI agents live entirely inside a computer. The Model Hardware Standard is Anthropic's attempt to let them reach out and touch real machines — microscopes, robotic arms, liquid handlers, lasers. It's a shared rulebook, the way USB is a rulebook: instead of every lab writing custom code to connect an AI to every instrument, MHS gives them one common language. Anthropic says work that normally takes weeks or months of wiring drops to hours or minutes.

The early results are the surprising part. Researchers at Carnegie Mellon ran drug-testing experiments about three times faster than usual, with a single agent juggling a liquid handler, a plate reader, a robot arm, and monitoring cameras spread across three separate computers. At quantum computing firm QuEra, an agent built a laser-recovery controller that worked 99.3% of the time across 700 trials — compared with 58% for hand-written scripts — and cut recovery time from about 150 seconds to six.

It started as a collaboration with HHMI's Janelia Research Campus, and hardware names like Universal Robots, QIAGEN, Tecan, Doosan and Raspberry Pi are lining up integrations. It's a research preview limited to a first group of labs and manufacturers, with open-sourcing promised after safety reviews. If you've wondered when AI stops writing about science and starts doing it, this is what that transition looks like.

ChatGPT

This Week in ChatGPT

ChatGPT is the chatbot that started the whole craze — you type a question, it types back, and somewhere in there it also writes your emails, debugs your code, and explains your homework. Its maker, OpenAI, has spent this week doing the thing it does best: quietly shipping a lot at once, then dropping a very big model on top of the pile.

The headline is GPT-6 Astra, which started rolling out to a limited set of organizations in early September. OpenAI is pitching it as a step up in coding, research, and what it calls "computer use" — meaning the model doesn't just talk about a task, it actually clicks through one. The most concrete new trick is that Astra can build documents, spreadsheets, and presentations from a template, then keep revising them as your instructions change. Think less "AI that writes a paragraph" and more "AI that produces the whole deck and then fixes slide four when you change your mind." It's not generally available yet, and OpenAI has said broader access is coming over the following days, so most people are still reading about it rather than using it.

Underneath that, the smaller updates are arguably the ones you'll feel sooner. As of August 28, you can connect multiple accounts for the Gmail, Google Calendar, and Google Contacts plugins — so your work inbox and your personal inbox can finally sit in the same conversation, and asking "what does my day look like?" pulls from both calendars instead of just one. On September 1, ChatGPT for Healthcare added an Epic integration plus a Healthcare Public Data plugin that reaches into nine official sources at once, including ClinicalTrials.gov, PubMed, RxNorm, DailyMed, and Medicare coverage data. That's for clinicians in the US, not the general public, but it's a good example of where the product is heading: less general-purpose chat, more plumbing into the specific databases a profession actually uses. Developers also got mutual TLS and X.509 identity federation on the API — deeply unglamorous security wiring that big companies insist on before they'll let an AI near anything important.

Put together, the week reads like OpenAI building for two audiences at once. Astra is the flashy capability story; the account connections, healthcare data plumbing, and enterprise security are the boring-but-necessary work of turning a clever chatbot into something a hospital or a bank will sign off on. The interesting question for regular users is whether "create the spreadsheet, then keep editing it" holds up outside a demo — that's the promise that would actually change what a Tuesday afternoon looks like.

Mistral AI

This Week in Mistral AI

Mistral is the French AI company that built its reputation on releasing capable models that are small, fast, and often open for anyone to download — the scrappy European alternative to the American giants. This week it did something slightly unusual for an AI lab: it barely talked about chatbots at all.

Instead, Mistral shipped Robostral Navigate, an 8-billion-parameter model for robot navigation that runs on a single ordinary RGB camera — no lidar, no depth sensors, no expensive rig. It scored 76.6% on R2R-CE unseen, a benchmark that tests whether a robot can follow spoken directions through a building it has never visited before. The "single camera" part is the impressive bit. Most robots figure out where they are by throwing lasers at the walls; this one does it with roughly what a phone camera sees, which makes the whole idea a lot cheaper to put on real hardware. Alongside that came Leanstral 1.5, an update to Mistral's model for formal mathematical proofs in Lean 4, with a cleaner training mix and the ability to reason across much longer stretches of a proof — the difference between solving a puzzle and solving a puzzle you can only see two pieces of at a time.

On the more practical end, Mistral OCR 4.1 reached general availability, extending the document-reading system that handles scanned pages across a very wide range of languages. And the company introduced Agentic Search, a retrieval layer that lets an AI navigate, read, and verify complex documents — the pitch being better accuracy in fewer back-and-forth turns, using fewer tokens and less time. If you've ever watched a chatbot search the same thing four times and still get it wrong, that's the problem this is aimed at. Mistral Common, the open toolkit, also picked up fixes for multi-image ordering, chat template integration, and better audio handling.

The through-line is that Mistral seems less interested in winning the "smartest chatbot" argument and more interested in the layers underneath — robots that move, proofs that check out, documents that get read correctly. Those are the pieces other people build products on top of, and they're a good deal harder to fake in a demo video.
