Microsoft Copilot

This Week in Microsoft Copilot

Imagine if your computer's helpful assistant stopped being a chatbox in the corner and became a whole workspace you live in. That is basically what Microsoft just did with Copilot, its AI helper that lives inside Windows, Word, Excel and the rest of the Microsoft world. The September update rebuilt the Copilot app around a new "Home" screen, where chatting with the AI and handing it bigger jobs sit side by side, with Word, Excel and PowerPoint built right in.

Two new pieces stand out. "Code," which borrows tech from GitHub Copilot, lets you describe an app or dashboard in plain English and have the AI build it, a bit like telling a very fast intern what you want on a whiteboard. "Autopilot" is a personal agent that keeps working in the background with its own identity and permissions, and it can nudge you before you even ask. Microsoft also widened the model menu, adding Grok models from SpaceXAI for early testers and a new OpenAI reasoning model for deeper thinking, so Copilot is no longer tied to just one brain.

The less flashy news matters too. Microsoft added usage-based billing and "Copilot Credits," so companies can see what their AI is costing, like a phone plan with a meter. Copilot in SharePoint and OneDrive also hit general availability, with Copilot Search becoming the default search there. The big question now is whether people actually want one AI workspace for everything, and whether the bills stay friendly. Even tech writers are arguing about whether calling Copilot an "operating system" is fair, since on Windows 11 it is still largely a web app.

SAP Joule

This Week in SAP Joule

Joule is SAP's AI helper, a bit like a very well-trained intern who lives inside the business software that big companies use to run payroll, shipping and accounting. This week was less about shiny new tricks and more about rules, guardrails and who pays for what.

The big theme was safety. In a September 28 update, SAP explained how Joule Studio, the place where companies build their own AI agents, checks permissions, job roles and process context before an agent does anything. Think of a school hall monitor who checks your pass before letting you through any door. SAP is also building Nvidia's OpenShell into its Business AI Platform to fence in what autonomous agents can touch, and the Joule Studio runtime stays free for customers and partners through October. Then came the money part. The free ride for Joule for Developers ended on September 30, and the ABAP coding features became a paid product on October 1. People already using the generative AI tools in SAP Build keep access through the end of 2026.

The product keeps growing too, with September's release notes adding a Concur meeting planning agent and a way to report workplace safety observations just by describing them in plain language. The takeaway is that SAP is moving from the demo phase to the invoice phase, so expect the next stories to be about what AI at work really costs.

Salesforce Agentforce / Einstein

This Week in Salesforce Agentforce

Agentforce is Salesforce's platform for AI agents, little digital workers that handle jobs in sales, customer support and more. This week was the hangover after Dreamforce, the company's giant September conference, as everyone dug into what was actually announced.

Salesforce put out a roundup of 21 announcements, and a few stand out. The pattern is clear. Salesforce wants agents that act like named coworkers instead of mysterious chatbots, and it wants one control layer to keep them in line. The test now is whether real customers see the payoff.

GitHub Copilot

This Week in GitHub Copilot

GitHub Copilot is the AI pair programmer that suggests code, fixes bugs and now does whole chunks of work on its own. This week it got new brains, new hands and a few new chores it can take off your plate.

First, the brains. According to the weekly changelog report, Claude Sonnet 5.5 became generally available across Copilot on September 28, and GPT-6.1 Sol followed on September 29, aimed at long, multi-step coding jobs. Then came HydraFusion, which reached VS Code and the Copilot app in research preview. Instead of one model, it orchestrates several, like a group project where one person drafts and another critiques. Now the hands. On October 1, GitHub said Copilot can use desktop apps, reading what is on screen, clicking buttons, typing and moving between programs. It is in public preview in the CLI and app on macOS and Windows.

The September VS Code update added automations that run on a schedule and an agent merge feature that handles review feedback, failed checks and merge conflicts. Heads up as well that GitHub plans to retire some older models in mid-October. The direction is simple: Copilot is turning from autocomplete into a teammate that can finish the job.

🔍 Tool Spotlight

🔍 Tool Spotlight: Shopify Canvas

Imagine building a whole online store by simply chatting. That is Canvas, a new tool from Shopify that launched on October 1. Store owners talk to Sidekick, Shopify's AI assistant, and watch the shop redraw itself in front of them as they type. They can also click on any piece of the page, like a banner or a button, and change it directly. Shopify says a fully custom store can come together in about twenty minutes.

What makes it interesting is that it is not a flimsy mockup. The changes are the real code behind the store, because Shopify gave Sidekick direct access to theme files and simplified how themes are built so the AI can understand them. That matters because designing a shop has usually meant hiring a developer or wrestling with templates. Small business owners, side-hustle sellers and anyone with a product idea are the obvious users. The surprise is the fine print. It is desktop-only for now, it is rolling out over several days, and it does not yet support third-party themes, app blocks or translations. Shopify says those are coming.

ChatGPT

This Week in ChatGPT

ChatGPT is the chatbot that taught the whole world to talk to an AI, and OpenAI says about 1.2 billion people now use it every single week. That is roughly one in seven humans on Earth. This week the company used its big DevDay event on September 29 to show how it wants ChatGPT to grow up from a chat window into something closer to a coworker.

The headline is speed. OpenAI introduced a premium setting called Ultrafast for its new GPT-6.1 Sol model, which can spit out around 300 tokens (tiny chunks of words) every second. Think of it as the difference between a friend texting you back and a friend who finishes your sentences. To go with it, there is a new top-end plan called Pro 500 with the biggest usage allowance yet. ChatGPT can also now go looking for plugins on its own in the middle of a conversation, so if you ask for something it cannot do, it can hunt for a tool that can. And a new “Sign in with ChatGPT” button lets you log in to apps like Notion and spend your ChatGPT allowance there, a bit like using one gift card at lots of different stores.

Teams got a lot too. Business and Enterprise plans picked up shared Pages and Spaces, team tasks, collaborative Sites editing, Codex Cloud for coding work, and the ability to call @ChatGPT inside Slack and Microsoft Teams. It was not all smooth, though. ChatGPT had a rough patch of degraded performance on September 30 that OpenAI says it will explain in a root cause write-up, and news outlets also reported that the company pulled a newer model version after safety concerns came up in testing. Details were thin when we checked, so treat that one as a story still unfolding. The big question now is whether people actually want an AI sitting in every app they use, or whether faster and cheaper turns out to matter more.

OpenAI Codex

This Week in OpenAI Codex

Codex is OpenAI’s coding agent, basically a tireless junior programmer that you can hand a task to and check on later. This week was its big moment, because DevDay on September 29 was packed with Codex news.

The biggest change is where Codex works. Until now, each cloud task was like a disposable sandbox that got thrown away afterward. Now OpenAI is giving Codex reusable cloud environments that stick around and can be opened from any device, so it is more like having your own workshop that is always set up the way you left it. In the command-line version, you can now start and steer tasks with your voice, and a new /agents view lets you hand out work to several helpers at once and watch them all, like a kitchen manager checking on every station. Ultrafast mode also reaches Codex, generating code up to eight times faster, and GPT-6.1 Sol is now the default model.

There are a couple of grown-up additions too. A new code review experience in the ChatGPT desktop app lets you read summaries, explore changes, and ask Codex about worrying spots before you comment on a GitHub pull request or GitLab merge request. And Codex Security Cloud scans a project for security holes and prepares fixes on its own. Small updates shipped right behind the keynote, including instant interrupt so you can cut Codex off mid-thought and redirect it. The takeaway is that Codex is trying to stop being a tool you babysit and start being a teammate you can send off with a job.

DeepSeek

This Week in DeepSeek

DeepSeek is the Chinese AI lab that shocked everyone by building strong models on a shoestring budget, and it is still the one rival labs watch nervously. This week the news was less about a shiny new chatbot and more about how the company trains its AIs.

On September 23, DeepSeek published a huge paper, about 10,000 words with roughly 130 authors including founder Liang Wenfeng, on something called DeepSeek Elastic Compute, or DSec. Picture a school gym that can instantly turn into a hundred practice rooms so an AI agent can rehearse a task over and over. The idea is that agents learn faster and are less likely to go off the rails while they practice. Reports also say Liang told investors the company will lean harder on Huawei and other Chinese chips for training, with a fresh batch possibly arriving in the fourth quarter. DeepSeek is said to be training a 2-trillion-parameter model right now, with an 8-trillion-parameter one down the road.

On the product side, the recent V4.1 Flash model, which can look at images and read a million tokens at once, is still the everyday workhorse, and its cheap prices got even cheaper. The so-what: the next big DeepSeek model may be trained on home-grown chips, which would be a big deal for the whole AI race.

Google Gemini

This Week in Google Gemini

Gemini spent the past week proving it isn't just a chatbot anymore — it's turning into something that can actually go do stuff in the real world, for better and for worse.

The "for worse" part came first. On September 19, the Wall Street Journal reported that Gemini had broken into the protected systems of three companies during a security test run by a firm called Irregular. In one case it simply guessed passwords until one worked, and in the other two it found leaked credentials sitting out in public code repositories — basically the digital version of finding a spare key under the doormat. It was a controlled test, not a real attack, but it's a good reminder that an AI smart enough to help you code is also smart enough to find the cracks in somebody else's.

Then came the friendlier side of "Gemini as agent." Google started rolling out a feature called "Call for Me," which does exactly what it sounds like: Gemini actually phones a business for you, say to check store hours or make a reservation, so you don't have to sit on hold yourself. It's starting small, limited to Pixel 11 owners in the US who pay for a Gemini subscription, but it's a real step from an AI that talks to you toward one that talks for you. Google also began testing shopping through Gemini and AI Mode in India, letting people buy products from Walmart-owned Flipkart without ever leaving the chat window — another sign Google wants Gemini to be less of a search box and more of a place where you actually get things done.

Put it together and the theme of the week is pretty clear: Google is giving Gemini arms and legs, not just a bigger brain, testing how far it can go from answering questions to actually acting on your behalf out in the world.

Microsoft Copilot

This Week in Microsoft Copilot

Microsoft Copilot has basically been one thing after another for the last few years — a chat helper here, a coding sidekick there, a bunch of separate tools bolted together. This week, Microsoft decided enough was enough and rebuilt the whole thing from the studs up.

On Friday, Microsoft unveiled a completely redesigned Copilot organized around three simple ideas: Home, Code, and Autopilot. Home is the new front door — the place where your chats, your documents, your meetings, and your to-do list all show up in one spot instead of scattered across five different apps. Code runs on the same engine as GitHub Copilot, but sandboxed so a company can host it on their own turf and let people build little internal apps and dashboards just by describing what they want in plain English. The headline act, though, is Autopilot — the rebranded and leveled-up version of what used to be called Scout. Think of it less like a chatbot and more like a coworker you can actually assign a job to: give it a name, a role, and a goal, and it'll sit there watching your channels, following up on loose threads, and picking projects back up days later without you having to nudge it. Investors clearly liked the sound of it too — Microsoft's stock jumped more than 4% the day it was announced, making it the best-performing "Magnificent Seven" stock that day. Alongside the big reveal, Copilot's Cowork feature also became generally available to every Microsoft 365 Copilot user, bringing image generation, browser control, and a new "effort level" dial so people can trade speed for quality depending on how much they actually need done.

None of this lands in your hands overnight — Home and Code are rolling out gradually through Microsoft's early-access Frontier program over the coming weeks, and Autopilot is only expanding into private preview at the very end of September. But the direction is obvious: Microsoft wants Copilot to stop feeling like a tool you occasionally poke and start feeling like a teammate that's quietly working even when you're not looking.
