ChatGPT

This Week in ChatGPT

ChatGPT is the chatbot that started the whole craze — you type a question, it types back, and somewhere in there it also writes your emails, debugs your code, and explains your homework. Its maker, OpenAI, has spent this week doing the thing it does best: quietly shipping a lot at once, then dropping a very big model on top of the pile.

The headline is GPT-6 Astra, which started rolling out to a limited set of organizations in early September. OpenAI is pitching it as a step up in coding, research, and what it calls "computer use" — meaning the model doesn't just talk about a task, it actually clicks through one. The most concrete new trick is that Astra can build documents, spreadsheets, and presentations from a template, then keep revising them as your instructions change. Think less "AI that writes a paragraph" and more "AI that produces the whole deck and then fixes slide four when you change your mind." It's not generally available yet, and OpenAI has said broader access is coming over the following days, so most people are still reading about it rather than using it.

Underneath that, the smaller updates are arguably the ones you'll feel sooner. As of August 28, you can connect multiple accounts for the Gmail, Google Calendar, and Google Contacts plugins — so your work inbox and your personal inbox can finally sit in the same conversation, and asking "what does my day look like?" pulls from both calendars instead of just one. On September 1, ChatGPT for Healthcare added an Epic integration plus a Healthcare Public Data plugin that reaches into nine official sources at once, including ClinicalTrials.gov, PubMed, RxNorm, DailyMed, and Medicare coverage data. That's for clinicians in the US, not the general public, but it's a good example of where the product is heading: less general-purpose chat, more plumbing into the specific databases a profession actually uses. Developers also got mutual TLS and X.509 identity federation on the API — deeply unglamorous security wiring that big companies insist on before they'll let an AI near anything important.

Put together, the week reads like OpenAI building for two audiences at once. Astra is the flashy capability story; the account connections, healthcare data plumbing, and enterprise security are the boring-but-necessary work of turning a clever chatbot into something a hospital or a bank will sign off on. The interesting question for regular users is whether "create the spreadsheet, then keep editing it" holds up outside a demo — that's the promise that would actually change what a Tuesday afternoon looks like.

Mistral AI

This Week in Mistral AI

Mistral is the French AI company that built its reputation on releasing capable models that are small, fast, and often open for anyone to download — the scrappy European alternative to the American giants. This week it did something slightly unusual for an AI lab: it barely talked about chatbots at all.

Instead, Mistral shipped Robostral Navigate, an 8-billion-parameter model for robot navigation that runs on a single ordinary RGB camera — no lidar, no depth sensors, no expensive rig. It scored 76.6% on R2R-CE unseen, a benchmark that tests whether a robot can follow spoken directions through a building it has never visited before. The "single camera" part is the impressive bit. Most robots figure out where they are by throwing lasers at the walls; this one does it with roughly what a phone camera sees, which makes the whole idea a lot cheaper to put on real hardware. Alongside that came Leanstral 1.5, an update to Mistral's model for formal mathematical proofs in Lean 4, with a cleaner training mix and the ability to reason across much longer stretches of a proof — the difference between solving a puzzle and solving a puzzle you can only see two pieces of at a time.

On the more practical end, Mistral OCR 4.1 reached general availability, extending the document-reading system that handles scanned pages across a very wide range of languages. And the company introduced Agentic Search, a retrieval layer that lets an AI navigate, read, and verify complex documents — the pitch being better accuracy in fewer back-and-forth turns, using fewer tokens and less time. If you've ever watched a chatbot search the same thing four times and still get it wrong, that's the problem this is aimed at. Mistral Common, the open toolkit, also picked up fixes for multi-image ordering, chat template integration, and better audio handling.

The through-line is that Mistral seems less interested in winning the "smartest chatbot" argument and more interested in the layers underneath — robots that move, proofs that check out, documents that get read correctly. Those are the pieces other people build products on top of, and they're a good deal harder to fake in a demo video.

Anthropic Claude Code

This Week in Anthropic Claude Code

Claude Code is Anthropic's coding assistant that lives in your terminal — the black text window programmers type commands into. Instead of copying code back and forth into a chat window, you just tell it what you want and it edits your files directly. This week the big story wasn't a shiny new feature at all. It was math, and a lot of developers were unhappy about it.

Back in May, Anthropic handed out a temporary 50% boost to weekly usage limits, then extended it four separate times over the summer. People stopped thinking of it as a bonus and started treating it as normal. On August 29 the company announced that baseline limits would permanently rise 25% starting September 14 — which sounds generous until you do the subtraction. Compared to what people actually had all summer, it's a 17% cut. The original announcement led with the 25% number and buried the rest, and developers noticed fast enough that Anthropic deleted the post and reposted it with the reduction spelled out. The company's explanation is straightforward: the summer numbers were never sustainable, and keeping the servers stable costs real compute.

Underneath the pricing drama, the actual product kept moving. A new Restricted Mode, added August 27, lets you clamp the assistant down hard — no running commands, no fetching things off the web, and file edits confined to the folder you're working in. Think of it as handing someone your car keys but disabling reverse. There's also a new cost-optimize command that audits how much you're spending on API calls and walks you through fixes one at a time, a feedback tool that drafts a bug report for you when something goes sideways, and tip overrides so companies can pipe their own house rules into the little hints that appear while you wait. Version 2.1.252 landed August 31 with a pile of reliability fixes across sessions, terminals, and the VS Code integration.

The pattern here is a product growing up. Free-flowing capacity and loose defaults are what you offer when you're chasing adoption; usage caps, spending audits, and a lockdown mode are what you ship when serious companies are running you in production. Cheaper it is not — but it's a lot harder to accidentally break something with.

xAI Grok

This Week in xAI Grok

Grok is xAI's chatbot family — the one wired directly into X, with a reputation for a looser, snarkier personality than its rivals. Lately it's been trying to be taken seriously as a work tool, and this week's updates lean hard in that direction.

The headline is Grok 4.6, xAI's frontier model, which is now available to developers both on the xAI API and in Model Garden. It carries a 500,000-token context window, which in plain terms means you can hand it something the length of a long novel and it keeps the whole thing in mind at once. It takes text and images in, gives text back, and there's no cap on how long its answers can run. xAI is pitching it at roughly half the price of comparable frontier models, which is the kind of claim that gets a developer's attention faster than any benchmark score.

Alongside that, Grok Bot graduated out of beta. It's now bundled into SuperGrok Plus, SuperGrok Heavy, and several Cursor plans — an always-on assistant meant to work like a colleague who lives inside your apps and inbox rather than a chat window you visit. The X integration got noticeably tighter too: you can link your account, and paid users get free X API credits plus tools for posting, reading timelines, and handling mentions. On the coding side, Grok Build 1.0.13 shipped on August 30 with a genuinely useful quality-of-life fix — it now resumes interrupted responses and retries failed attempts on its own, so a long job doesn't die halfway through and force a restart. New confirmation prompts also let you approve actions before they run. Grok Imagine, the image tool, picked up automatic quality settings, support for up to five reference images, and cinematic aspect ratios.

Taken together it's less about raw intelligence and more about trust and plumbing: cheaper access, longer memory, agents that don't fall over mid-task, and a confirm button before anything irreversible happens. That combination — aggressive pricing plus grown-up controls — is xAI's clearest pitch yet to people who write code for a living.

Anthropic Claude CoWork

This Week in Anthropic Claude CoWork

Cowork is the part of Claude that stops chatting and starts doing. Instead of handing you an answer to copy and paste, it opens your files, runs the steps, and gives you back a finished thing — a spreadsheet, a report, a cleaned-up folder. Think of the difference between asking a friend how to change a tire and having them actually change it. This week the big news is that Cowork got a pair of eyes for the open web.

Anthropic started rolling out a browser built directly into the Cowork desktop app. When a job needs a website, a real browser opens in a side panel and Claude drives it — reading pages, clicking buttons, filling in forms, pulling numbers off a dashboard. The reason this matters is unglamorous but huge: most business software has no tidy connector you can plug into. It's a portal with a login and a bunch of menus. Until now, that was a wall. Now Claude can just walk through the front door like a person would, and you watch it happen in the panel. It's going out to Pro, Max, and Team plans, and if you already use the Claude Chrome extension, that stays your default — there's a setting under Cowork to switch between them.

The desktop release that landed on August 27 was mostly the unsexy follow-up work that makes a new feature actually usable: fixes for the browser stalling or timing out on Windows and Linux when the pane was hidden, a fix for Cowork running out of memory after a long run of scheduled tasks, and a fix for the app choking when a settings file got corrupted by a bad shutdown. There was also a real bug where scheduled tasks were being quietly marked "skipped" without ever running; those now get retried a few minutes later. If you rely on Cowork to do something every morning, that one was worth fixing.

Zoom out and the shape of the last two weeks is clear. Cowork went fully mobile and web on August 18, its memory got merged with regular Claude chat on August 25 so it stops forgetting what you told it in the other room, and on August 26 Salesforce and Anthropic announced "Claudeforce," which drops 37 prebuilt sales skills straight into Claude. Add the built-in browser and the pattern is obvious: Anthropic is trying to make Cowork something that reaches into wherever your work actually lives, rather than a clever box you have to carry things into.

Eleven Labs

This Week in Eleven Labs

ElevenLabs is the company that made synthetic voices stop sounding like a GPS unit. Its models read text aloud with pauses, emphasis, and breath, clone a voice from a short sample, transcribe speech, dub video into other languages, and increasingly run full voice agents that hold a phone conversation with a customer. Lately the interesting work has moved away from "listen to how real this sounds" and toward "here is how you run a hundred of these in production without losing your mind."

The August 24 release was the clearest sign of that shift. ElevenLabs shipped version 1.0 of its command-line tool, which means every single thing the service can do is now available as a typed command in a terminal, with proper output formats and pagination. More importantly, you can store an agent's whole configuration — its instructions, its tools, its tests — as plain files, push and pull them, and manage branches. That's the same workflow programmers use for code, applied to a talking robot. It sounds dry. What it actually means is that a voice agent stops being a thing you hand-tune in a web form and becomes something a team can review, version, and roll back when it goes wrong.

Alongside that, "Procedures" went generally available in ElevenAgents. The idea is neat: instead of cramming every possible instruction into one enormous system prompt, you write separate little playbooks — one for refunds, one for booking, one for angry customers — each with a trigger that decides when it applies. Mid-conversation, the agent loads just the relevant one. It's the difference between memorizing an entire employee handbook and knowing which tab to flip to. You can write them loosely when the agent should improvise, or as strict numbered steps when the order genuinely matters. The same update added triage tickets, so a human can flag a bad conversation and assign it to a teammate for review, and a live event that tells you how much of the model's context each turn is eating.

None of this will show up in a demo video. But it's what turns a party trick into infrastructure, and it's a reasonable bet that the voice on the other end of your next customer service call will have been built and reviewed exactly this way.

🔍 Tool Spotlight

Runway Solaris

On August 31, Runway — best known for AI video generation — announced something genuinely strange called Solaris, the first of what it calls an "Interface World Model." Here's the idea. Normally, when you use an app, a designer drew it, a programmer wrote code to make it work, and your device runs that code. Solaris throws out the middle step entirely. It generates the screen frame by frame, like a video, reacting in real time to wherever you click. There is no app underneath. There's a language model deciding what should happen next, and a world model painting the result, sixty times a second.

It's built on top of Runway's Gen-4.5 video model, and in the company's own preference testing people picked Solaris over conventionally coded interfaces 61% of the time on how well it followed instructions. That number deserves a raised eyebrow — it's Runway grading its own homework — but the concept is worth paying attention to. If software can be dreamed into existence as you use it, "installing an app" starts to look like a very twentieth-century idea.

The catch: Solaris is an early-access research project, not a product. There's no API, no download, no open weights — just a request form. Treat it as a glimpse of a possible future rather than something to use on Monday. But it's the most interesting glimpse anyone has offered in a while.

Source: Runway Research, August 2026

Anthropic Claude

This Week in Anthropic Claude

Claude is the AI assistant built by Anthropic, and it's best known for being the one people trust with long, messy, real work — reading a hundred pages, writing the thing, then actually doing the follow-up steps. This past week Claude got two upgrades that both point in the same direction: it's trying to stop being a goldfish, and it's trying to show up where your job already lives.

The goldfish problem first. Until now, Claude kept separate memories depending on where you talked to it — the chat you had in the app didn't carry over to Cowork, the file-and-task side of the product. Anthropic merged those, so anything Claude learns in one place it now knows in the other. Better still, it opened the box: you can read what Claude has remembered about you, edit it, or delete it, topic by topic. That's a small design decision with a big feel, like the difference between a friend who quietly takes notes and one who hands you the notebook and says "check this, is it right?" Alongside that, the desktop app picked up a built-in browser — a web panel Claude can open, read, click, and type into — which turns "here's a link, go look" into something it can just handle.

Then came the enterprise news. On August 26, Salesforce and Anthropic announced Claudeforce, a partnership that plugs Salesforce's customer data and business rules straight into Claude. It ships with a plugin carrying 37 prebuilt sales skills, so a salesperson can ask about live pipeline, update records, and take action without leaving the conversation. Anthropic also expanded on the education side, offering a free managed version of Claude to K–12 schools and districts — single sign-on, admin controls, classroom-ready material — with a year free for organizations that sign up by mid-2027.

Put those together and the theme is Claude trying to become less of a chat window and more of a coworker: one that remembers yesterday, can open a webpage itself, and knows what's in your company's systems. The interesting tension is that all three of those things require handing Claude more access — which is probably exactly why the memory controls arrived in the same week.

Perplexity AI

This Week in Perplexity AI

Perplexity started life as a search engine that answers your question in a paragraph with sources attached, instead of handing you ten blue links. It has been steadily mutating into something bigger, and this week it took the strangest and most ambitious step yet: it wants to live in a box on your desk.

The headline is Personal Computer, announced in late August — an always-on Perplexity that runs on a dedicated Mac mini and blends your local files and apps with its cloud service. The pitch is that it works around the clock as a kind of digital stand-in, watching for triggers, running tasks on its own, and pushing work forward while you're asleep. It's a genuinely odd idea in an era when everything else is moving to the cloud, and it drew some raised eyebrows in the tech press, which framed it as Perplexity muscling into the local-AI scene. Whether people want a second computer whose only job is to think for them is an open question, but it's a real answer to a real annoyance: cloud assistants can't see the files on your laptop.

Underneath that, the more useful upgrade is Brain, Perplexity's self-improving memory. Rather than storing a flat list of facts, Brain builds a private map of your sessions, connected accounts, files, and past decisions, and refreshes it overnight so the next task starts already knowing what worked and what flopped. Perplexity's own testing claims a 25% lift in answer correctness and 16% better recall while costing 13% less on tasks that had prior context — the AI equivalent of a colleague who's been on the project long enough to stop asking where the files are. The same stretch brought a faster Opus 4.8 mode, the ability to swap models mid-task, per-model usage stats, website publishing, and Comet — Perplexity's AI-first browser — opening up to enterprise customers.

The pattern here mirrors Claude's week almost exactly: memory that persists, a browser that acts, and a push into the workplace. Two companies that started at opposite ends — one an assistant, one a search box — are converging on the same product idea, which usually means the idea is right and the fight is about to get expensive.

Google Gemini

This Week in Google Gemini

Gemini is Google's flagship AI — the thing that answers your questions in Search, writes your emails in Gmail, and increasingly runs quietly in the background of nearly every Google product you touch. This week was less about one big splashy launch and more about Gemini seeping into places it wasn't before, which is arguably the more interesting story.

The headline is that Gemini 3.7 Flash is now sometimes powering AI Mode in Google Search. "Flash" is Google's word for its fast, cheap models — the sports car rather than the freight train — and putting it behind Search means billions of queries are getting a smarter engine without anyone clicking a button. Google also shipped 3.7 Flash with tightened safety guardrails around chemical, biological, and cyber-attack topics, which is the sort of unglamorous work that only makes news when someone skips it. Meanwhile, the Gemini API got two dedicated speech-to-text models called Gemini 3.5 Transcribe, which can handle 85+ languages, figure out who is speaking (that's "speaker diarization"), stamp a timestamp on every single word, and even learn your weird industry jargon so it stops mangling it. If you've ever fought with automatic captions on a recording of a noisy meeting, this is aimed squarely at you.

Workspace got its own share. On August 26 Google introduced "Ask Gemini in Google Chat," essentially a single command line that sits inside your work chat and lets you ask for things without hopping between apps. Then on August 31 Google began rolling out the ability to start and stop the "Take notes for me" feature straight from the touchscreen controller in a physical Google Meet room — a small thing, but it's the difference between an AI notetaker you have to remember to set up and one that's a button on the table. On the enterprise side, Gemini Omni 1.1 Flash entered public preview for video, image, and text tasks, while the older gemini-robotics-er-1.6-preview model was switched off today and the omni-flash-preview endpoint is scheduled to go dark on September 30.

The pattern here is worth noticing: Google isn't really selling you a chatbot anymore. It's wiring the same model into Search, Chat, Meet hardware, and the developer API, then quietly upgrading the engine underneath. For most people the upshot is that things you already use just get better without you ever downloading anything. For developers, the flip side is the deprecation notices — the models are improving fast enough that last year's endpoint has an expiration date on it.

Meta Llama

This Week in Meta Llama

Llama is Meta's family of AI models, and its claim to fame has always been openness: while most top-tier AI lives locked behind a company's servers, Meta published the actual model files so anyone could download them and run them on their own machine. That made Llama the backbone of a huge amount of independent AI work — and it's why the last few months have felt so strange to people who follow it.

Quiet week for Meta Llama — no major announcements in the past seven days.

The last genuinely notable move came on August 10, when Meta Superintelligence Labs released a model called Muse Glimmer under the permissive Apache 2.0 licence and put the weights on Hugging Face with no gatekeeping. It's a 30-billion-parameter multimodal model, meaning it reads both text and images, handles a 128,000-token context window and over 100 languages, and — the clever part — was squeezed down to roughly 4-bit precision so it fits under 20GB. Translated: it runs on a single decent gaming GPU or a well-specced Mac, offline, on your desk. That's a real capability sitting on consumer hardware rather than rented from a data centre.

The bigger question hanging over all of this is what happens to the Llama name itself. Meta's frontier model this year, Muse Spark, launched back in April as closed-weight and API-only — a sharp turn away from the open philosophy Llama was built on. Alexandr Wang, Meta's Chief AI Officer, has said an open-weight version of Muse Spark 1.2 is coming, but as of mid-August nothing had been published. So the story to watch isn't a feature release; it's whether Meta keeps its open-source promise, or whether Llama quietly becomes a brand it used to have.
