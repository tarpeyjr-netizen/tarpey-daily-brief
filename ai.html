DeepSeek

This Week in DeepSeek

DeepSeek is the Chinese AI lab that surprised everyone by building chatbots that rival the big American ones at a fraction of the price. Think of it as the scrappy underdog that keeps showing up to the race in a faster car than anyone expected, and this week it showed up again.

On October 17, DeepSeek released an update called V4.1-Flash-1017. Flash is the speedy, budget-friendly version of its model family, and this tune-up is all about coding. DeepSeek says it got better at building whole projects from scratch, like little games, 3D models and design work, instead of just fixing one line at a time. In plain English, apps built on DeepSeek can now look things up on the internet for a small fee. Add in earlier reports (not confirmed by the companies) that DeepSeek is raising a huge pile of money, with talk of 80 billion yuan or more, and it looks like the underdog is getting ready to play in the big leagues. Pricing details differ between sources, so check DeepSeek's own page before building anything.

🔍 Tool Spotlight

Jais 3

Most big AI models are built mainly around English, which can leave Arabic speakers with clunky answers. Jais 3 is a new answer to that. Released on October 15 by MBZUAI's Institute of Foundation Models and Inception42 in the United Arab Emirates, it is a family of six open language models built for Arabic and its many dialects. They range from tiny ones with 900 million parameters, small enough to run on a phone, up to a giant 375 billion parameter version meant for serious computer clusters. Think of it like a set of cars from scooter to freight truck, all built on the same engine design. Every model can handle a 512,000 token context window, so it can read a whole stack of documents at once, and they are designed to act as agents that can carry out multi-step tasks. A companion model, Jais Speech, turns spoken Arabic into text. The surprising part is how open it is: the weights are on Hugging Face, with reports of an Apache 2.0 license, plus training checkpoints and the tests used to grade it. The team says its 7B model jumped from 56.4 to 79.6 on its own Arabic tests, though that is self-reported. Developers, students and companies working in Arabic have the most to gain.

Microsoft Copilot

This Week in Microsoft Copilot

Think of Microsoft Copilot as a helpful assistant that lives inside the apps millions of people already use for school and work, like Word, PowerPoint, Teams and Outlook. This week the big story is less about one flashy launch and more about Microsoft tidying up the house: Copilot is getting a consistent look and a few new tricks, with much of it landing in October.

The headline idea is "Skills," which are basically saved recipes for Copilot. In PowerPoint you can now keep a repeatable workflow, like "Explain this presentation" or "Sharpen slide titles," and run it again whenever you want, a bit like saving a favorite macro in a video game. Typing @ in Copilot Chat pulls those Skills up quickly, and company IT teams can hand approved ones to everyone. Also coming in October is the ability for Copilot to keep chewing on a long job after you close the app or switch devices, then ping you when it is done. Meanwhile, Copilot's panel in Teams and Outlook has been redesigned to match the main Copilot app, and it has gotten better at reading text inside scanned PDFs, though it still is not perfect.

There is also a bigger picture behind all this. Late September brought a reshuffle that Microsoft calls its largest Copilot update so far, with Home, Code, Autopilot and Office pieces, plus a "Today" screen that is meant to show you what matters before you ask. A private preview of Today is slated for October. The so-what: Copilot is trying to move from a chatbot you visit to a helper that is simply there, but plenty of these features are still rolling out in stages, so do not be surprised if your copy looks different from your friend's. (Note: news from the last seven days was thin, so this draws on the past few weeks.)

🔍 Tool Spotlight

Mistral Large 4 ("Le Chonk")

Mistral, the French AI company, just released a preview of a giant new model that people are nicknaming Le Chonk. It has about a trillion parameters, which you can think of as the adjustable dials inside the model's brain. But it uses a trick called mixture of experts: for every word it handles, only about 49 billion of those dials switch on, like calling in just the right specialists instead of the whole company. It handles images as well as text and reportedly can read a million tokens at once, roughly several long novels. Mistral calls it the best open-weight model from the US or Europe on combined tests, although outside scores put it only close to DeepSeek's 552-billion-parameter model, and some of Mistral's coding results haven't been independently repeated. The surprising part is the promise: Mistral plans to release the full weights, meaning anyone could download and run it, around October 27, though it hasn't said which license will apply. For now it is available through Mistral's API. Developers, researchers and companies that want control over their own AI will care the most.

🔍 Tool Spotlight

🔍 Tool Spotlight: EmbeddingGemma 2

Here is a model most people will never chat with, yet it could quietly power apps you use. EmbeddingGemma 2 is a Google DeepMind model released on October 6, built on the Gemma 4 design and shared openly under an Apache 2.0 license. It is an embedding model, which means it turns things into lists of numbers so a computer can tell which ones mean similar stuff. Think of a giant library where books about the same idea get shelved together, no matter the language or format.

The surprise is how much it handles. Text, code, images, video and audio all land in one shared space, so a search for a phrase can find a matching video clip. At about 740 million parameters, the quantized version needs only around 567 MB of memory on a phone, and it now reads up to 8,000 tokens at a time. That makes private, offline search and smart assistants on your own device far more realistic. Developers building search, recommendation or note-taking apps are the obvious users.
