Google Gemini

This Week in Google Gemini

Gemini is Google's AI assistant, the one that lives in the Gemini app, in Search, in Android phones and in Chrome. Most of the big noise came a bit more than a week ago, so let's start there and then look at what Google has been doing since.

On October 13, Google made Gemini 4 Argon available to everyone who wants it: paying Google AI Pro and Ultra subscribers in the Gemini app, and developers through the Gemini API. Google says it was first handed in late September to a small group of trusted cybersecurity defenders, a bit like letting the locksmiths test a new kind of lock before the public gets the keys. Google also published a long note on how it tried to build the model safely. Since then the company has been showing it off: on October 23 it posted a roundup of five projects that builders made with Argon, and two days before that, on October 20, it rolled out new Gemini tools for small business owners, including a dedicated landing page. Earlier, on October 15, Google also launched Universal Cart in the U.S., a shopping cart that follows you across Search and the Gemini app.

The so-what is that Google is trying to make Gemini useful for ordinary chores, like shopping and running a small shop, not only for chatting. Keep an eye out for how people use Argon in the next few weeks. Note that these details come from Google's own blog feed, and I did not find independent confirmation of the benchmark claims floating around.

Microsoft Copilot

This Week in Microsoft Copilot

Microsoft Copilot is the AI helper Microsoft has stitched into Windows, Word, Excel, PowerPoint, Teams and Outlook. Think of it as an assistant that sits next to you at your desk, and this month Microsoft has been teaching it to do a lot more than answer questions.

The biggest theme in the October updates is Copilot Notebooks, which is getting the ability to hand off tasks, run recurring automations and edit Office files directly. In plain terms, you could ask it to do a chore once and then have it repeat that chore on a schedule, a bit like setting a recurring alarm that also does the homework. Reports say the scheduled tasks are coming over the next few weeks, while task delegation and model choice are available first to people in Microsoft's Frontier early-access program. Alongside that, write-ups describe new PowerPoint tricks such as Interactive Slides and a "Record a Skill" feature, plus interactive dashboards and workbook rules in Excel. Some of these reportedly need a paid Copilot Premium plan. In Teams, mentioning @Copilot is said to give it the shared context and permissions of that channel, chat or meeting, and a proactive "Today" command center is in private preview.

A word of caution: most of this comes from third-party write-ups rather than Microsoft's own release notes, and rollout dates vary, so your Copilot may not look like this yet. The big picture is that Microsoft wants Copilot to act more like a teammate who does work in the background than a chatbot you poke at, and over the next few weeks we will find out how much of that actually shows up on real screens.

🔍 Tool Spotlight

🔍 Tool Spotlight: Mercury 3

Most AI chatbots write one word, or word-piece, at a time, left to right, like someone typing. Mercury 3, released October 19 by a company called Inception, does it differently. It is a "diffusion" language model, which starts with a rough draft of the whole answer and cleans it up in several passes, a bit like a photo coming into focus. That trick is why it is so fast: Inception says it produces about 1,068 tokens (small chunks of text) per second on Nvidia chips and handles a million tokens of context, roughly several long novels at once. The company also claims it beats rivals like Claude Haiku 5.5 while running four to eight times faster, but those numbers are self-reported and nobody outside has tested them yet. It is available only through an API, including via OpenRouter, Baseten and Vercel, so it is aimed at developers building things like web-browsing agents, live search and customer support, where waiting a few seconds feels forever. The surprising part is the bigger claim: Inception says Mercury 3 is more than 40 times larger than its first model, and argues that diffusion models improve with size just as ordinary ones do.

Anthropic Claude Code

This Week in Anthropic Claude Code

Claude Code is Anthropic's AI coding helper that lives right in your terminal and can read a whole project, write code and fix bugs on its own. Imagine a very fast coding partner who never gets tired. This week it learned to double-check other people's work, too.

The big news landed on October 15, when Anthropic made Code Review generally available for Team and Enterprise customers. It works like a group of editors reading your essay at once: several AI helpers look over a pull request (a proposed change to a codebase) in parallel. Anthropic says reviews now finish about three times faster than during the test run, dropping from roughly 20 minutes to 6–10. The company also shared how its own Claude Code team handles reviews. It says Claude writes about 90% of Claude Code's code, and that after one review loop ran more than 50 rounds overnight, the team added a rule: after three rounds, rethink your approach. Those numbers come from Anthropic itself, not an outside check.

Meanwhile, the tool keeps getting small, steady upgrades. The newest releases (around version 2.1.307) added a guided /auto-mode-setup so the safety classifier learns your environment, new safety-event logging for blocked or prompted actions, and better recovery when the AI fumbles a tool call. Anthropic also opened applications for a Women in AI hackathon built around Claude Code. The so-what: coding tools are shifting from writing code to checking it, and that is where the trust gets built.

🔍 Tool Spotlight

Jais 3

Most big AI models are built mainly around English, which can leave Arabic speakers with clunky answers. Jais 3 is a new answer to that. Released on October 15 by MBZUAI's Institute of Foundation Models and Inception42 in the United Arab Emirates, it is a family of six open language models built for Arabic and its many dialects. They range from tiny ones with 900 million parameters, small enough to run on a phone, up to a giant 375 billion parameter version meant for serious computer clusters. Think of it like a set of cars from scooter to freight truck, all built on the same engine design. Every model can handle a 512,000 token context window, so it can read a whole stack of documents at once, and they are designed to act as agents that can carry out multi-step tasks. A companion model, Jais Speech, turns spoken Arabic into text. The surprising part is how open it is: the weights are on Hugging Face, with reports of an Apache 2.0 license, plus training checkpoints and the tests used to grade it. The team says its 7B model jumped from 56.4 to 79.6 on its own Arabic tests, though that is self-reported. Developers, students and companies working in Arabic have the most to gain.
