Anthropic Claude Code

This Week in Anthropic Claude Code

Claude Code is Anthropic’s coding helper that lives in your terminal. You type what you want in plain English, and it reads your project, writes the code and runs the commands for you. Last week it learned a new trick called Code Mode, where Claude writes a small program to use its tools instead of pressing one button at a time. This week, Code Mode left the lab and went to work, and Claude also picked up a surprising new skill. It can draw charts.

Four updates came out between November 19 and November 24, according to the release tracker Havoptic. Version 2.1.329 on November 19 turned /tasks into a kind of to-do board. It now shows your scheduled tasks, /loop wakeups and side conversations, and you can stop any of them. A new warning in the trust dialog also tells you where a folder’s hooks come from, so a project can’t sneak in automatic commands. The fun one came on November 20. Version 2.1.330 added “easel,” a built-in add-on that draws a chart when you ask for one. Think of a calculator that suddenly sketches the graph for you. It works on Pro and Max plans on Mac and Linux, and you can switch automatic charts off in /config. The same update let companies sign people in through Anthropic’s Enterprise Admin Console, so the right settings show up on their own. Then on November 23, version 2.1.331 brought Code Mode to the big cloud platforms: Amazon Bedrock, Google Cloud, Microsoft Foundry and Claude Platform on AWS. claude doctor, the built-in checkup tool, can now tell you if it can reach Anthropic’s servers at all. Finally, on November 24, version 2.1.332 let MCP servers, the plug-ins that connect Claude to other apps, bring their own skills. Those skills show up in the / menu as server:skill. That release also fixed a long list of freezes, like the terminal locking up when a plug-in floods it with text.

The so-what is that Claude Code is growing from a clever assistant into something closer to a workshop. It can schedule jobs, draw pictures of your data and borrow skills from other tools. One date to remember: fast mode for the older Opus 4.8 model switches off for good on November 30, so anyone still using it has four days to move.

Grok Build, xAI’s coding tool for the terminal, kept its fast pace too, with four new versions between November 19 and 25, from 1.0.91 to 1.0.94, according to Havoptic. The public notes don’t say what changed.

So the big question is when that soft rollout turns into a real launch. If Musk’s “week or two” holds, Grok 4.8 could arrive in early December. Then we’ll finally see if the rebuilt brain beats the old one.

Artlist AI

This Week in Artlist AI

Artlist started as a library of music and video clips that creators could use legally. Now it’s also an AI studio where you can make images, video, voiceovers and music with lots of different AI models in one place. After a few quiet weeks, it made a move this week. It connected chatbots to one of the most popular video-effects programs around.

On November 19, the news tracker Distill Intelligence reported that Artlist brought its MCP tool to Adobe After Effects. MCP is a plug-in standard that lets chatbots like Claude or ChatGPT use other apps. Artlist’s own MCP page explains how it works. Pair the Artlist MCP with Artlist’s After Effects plug-in, and you can ask a chatbot to set up a scene, add layers and text, animate things, and pull in AI clips or licensed music. Picture telling a helper, “make me a title card with snow falling,” and finding the project built and ready to tweak. It needs After Effects 2026 (version 26.0) or newer. Edits made this way don’t use up Artlist credits, though making new AI images or videos still does. Designers keep the final say, because everything lands as a normal project they can edit by hand. Then, right on time for the holidays, the same tracker reported on November 23 that Artlist launched a holiday sale. It’s 40% off yearly AI plans, plus double credits in your first month, and monthly subscribers can switch to a yearly plan to get the deal. Artlist’s MCP page is advertising the Holiday Sale too.

The bigger idea is that Artlist doesn’t want to be just a website you visit. It wants to sit inside the tools editors already use and the chatbots they already talk to. If you’ve been curious about AI video, a sale week is a pretty low-risk time to give it a try.

Eleven Labs

This Week in Eleven Labs

Eleven Labs makes AI voices that sound strikingly human, and it builds voice agents that answer phones and help customers. The past seven days were quiet after its big New York summit, so we looked back two weeks. The most interesting news is tucked inside its November 17 developer changelog, the place where companies post the small print that often matters most.

First, turning speech into text got cheaper. Every Scribe transcription model now costs $0.20 per hour of recorded audio and $0.22 per hour for live audio on self-serve and Business plans. That is less than a candy bar for an hour-long podcast. Second, there is a new tool for telling real voices from fake ones. A beta Content Provenance API checks an audio file for a hidden Eleven Labs watermark, like a secret stamp only a special scanner can see. If it finds one, it can often say which product and model made the clip and when, without revealing the prompt or the account behind it. Third, the company added 39 new tools that let other AI assistants build Eleven Labs voice agents directly, with every change kept as a draft until someone presses publish. Developers also got a way to write agent tools as code instead of clicking through a dashboard.

One heads-up for builders: starting November 26, agent requests can time out during traffic spikes and return an error, so apps should be ready to try again. The big picture is that as AI voices get harder to tell apart from real ones, a watermark checker may end up being the most important thing Eleven Labs shipped this month.

🔍 Tool Spotlight

MiniMax H3-Image

MiniMax H3-Image is a new image model from MiniMax, a Chinese AI company, and its weights appeared on Hugging Face around November 12. One model does two jobs. It can create pictures from a text prompt, and it can edit pictures using up to 16 reference images, like handing an artist a stack of photos and saying “combine these.” The headline trick is size. It makes images of about 16 megapixels, sharp enough to print as a poster, without a separate upscaler to blow them up. It is also fast for its size, needing just six drawing steps where many models take dozens. You can type a short prompt in almost any language, and a helper model rewrites it into a detailed English description first. The catch is that it is enormous: the main model alone is a 68 GB download, plus another big file for the text reader. And it uses MiniMax’s own community license, not a standard open-source one, so it suits hobbyists, researchers and developers more than casual users. MiniMax calls it the best open model at following instructions, but the page shows no test scores to back that up.

This Week in Anthropic Claude

Claude is Anthropic’s AI assistant, the one that chats, writes, codes and now does whole jobs on its own. Last week we said the company was about to sell shares on the stock market for the first time. This week it actually happened, and it happened in a very big way.

The same day brought a quieter but important update for big companies, called Enterprise Frontier Safeguards. It is in beta, and it is built for places like hospitals, banks and law firms that cannot let their data be stored anywhere else. It watches for sneaky misuse that gets split into lots of small, harmless-looking requests, like a puzzle where no single piece looks like the picture. The so what? Claude now belongs to a public company, so every launch will be watched by investors as well as users. And for anyone who writes code, a robot that tidies up your work before a human ever looks at it is about to feel pretty normal.

Google Gemini

This Week in Google Gemini

Google Gemini is the AI that lives inside Google’s apps and phones, and it also powers the tools coders use to build their own AI projects. Last week Gemini moved deeper into your phone. This week it learned to run errands for you, and to use your computer like a person would.

The biggest change landed on November 17, according to Google’s Gemini release notes. Tasks, the feature that lets Gemini chew on a multi-step job in the background and ping you when it is done, is now free in the US, India, Canada, Australia, Japan and Korea. And Tasks got a lot more hands. You can now text Gemini from Google Messages or iMessage to start a job, or just forward it an email, like a school calendar you want copied into Google Calendar. In the US, Gemini can watch for a sold-out item to come back in stock or a price to drop within your budget, then line up a Google Pay checkout. It only buys once you say yes. It can even phone several local shops at once to compare quotes or check what is on the shelf. It tells them it is an AI, and you approve the call plan first. Then on November 19, paying subscribers in the US got computer use in the Gemini app for Mac. Gemini can click, type and fill in forms across apps and websites in its own separate window, and it stops and waits for you whenever a login or payment comes up. Think of it as a helper borrowing your keyboard while you sit beside them.

Builders had a busy week too. Google’s Gemini API changelog shows Gemini 4 Neon going generally available on November 18. It is a heavy-duty model aimed at tough coding, science and office work, and it can hold about a million tokens in mind at once (a token is roughly a chunk of a word). The same day, apps gained the power to read Word, PowerPoint and Excel files directly, and to slice out just the pages or spreadsheet tabs you need, so you only pay for what you use. On November 19, Genie 4, Google DeepMind’s world-maker, became generally available to developers. You describe a place, or show it a picture, and it streams back a world you can steer with your arrow keys, with sound included. It costs about ten cents a second, which makes it a pricey video game, but a magical one.

So what is the big picture? Gemini is turning from something you talk to into something that does things for you, from texting a shop to filling in a form. The safety rule is the same everywhere: it checks with you before it spends money or does anything big. Expect more of these “do it for me” powers to spread beyond the US over the coming months.

This Week in Microsoft Copilot

Microsoft Copilot is the AI helper that lives inside Word, Excel, PowerPoint, Outlook and Teams. Last Sunday we said Microsoft Ignite was the week to watch. It delivered. The big conference ran November 17 to 20 in San Francisco, and Copilot was the star of almost every keynote.

The headline is that Copilot now has its own permanent sidekick. Microsoft did not publish prices for Copilot Credits, the meter that some of these always-on features run on. The Today Feed, which updates all day, eats those credits.

So what does it all mean? Copilot is changing from a chatbot you ask questions into a coworker that keeps going on its own. The next test comes in December, when Copilot Code is due and we may finally learn what the meter costs.

Canva AI / Magic Studio

This Week in Canva AI / Magic Studio

Canva is the drag-and-drop design app that millions of students use for posters, slides and social posts. It has packed in a lot of AI lately, from its Canva Craft image model to Canva Brush for editing designs. This week it went the other way. It gave people a way to turn the AI off.

On November 20, Mumbrella reported that Canva launched two free “AI Controls” for Canva Free and Pro users. The first is a simple switch. Flip it, and the AI features that make new pictures and text disappear from Canva. Flip it back any time. It sits under Profile, then Settings, then Permissions. Handy everyday tools like Background Remover and Magic Grab still work, because they only edit what you already made. The second control trims how much AI-made stuff shows up when you browse Canva’s library of graphics. Some backgrounds and illustrations are tagged as AI-made, and this setting pushes them down in search results. It is a bit like telling a music app to play fewer remixes. Canva says the goal is to “put creative choice back in your hands,” and notes its AI tools have been used more than 38 billion times.

The timing is interesting. Earlier this month Canva announced a restructure that hit about 22% of its staff, and it has been betting heavily on AI. Now it is also listening to people who would rather draw with their own hands. Expect other creative apps to face the same question: how do you sell AI while respecting the folks who want less of it?
