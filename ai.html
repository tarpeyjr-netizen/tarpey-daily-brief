ChatGPT

This Week in ChatGPT

ChatGPT is the chatbot that started the whole craze — you type a question, it types back an answer, and these days it can also read your files, talk out loud, and go do errands on the internet for you. It just crossed a billion people using it every week, and this past week OpenAI spent that momentum on one theme: give everybody more, and take the fences down.

The biggest change is that the free version got a brain transplant. A new model called GPT-5.6 Luna is now the default for everyone, including people who pay nothing, and OpenAI says it makes factual mistakes about 62% less often than the model it replaces. That number is worth sitting with — the single most annoying thing about chatbots has always been how confidently they get things wrong, and this is the free tier getting the fix. Alongside it there's a new "Think" button, which is basically a gear shift: tap it and the model slows down and reasons harder on a tricky question instead of blurting out the first thing it has. Paid Plus and Pro users get a faster sibling model called GPT-5.6 Sol for everyday research and writing, plus a slider to dial reasoning depth up or down themselves.

Then OpenAI did something it has resisted for years: it removed message limits on text chats entirely, for everyone. No more hitting a wall mid-conversation and being told to come back in three hours. The rest of the week's news was smaller but points the same direction — ChatGPT can now search OpenTable, Resy, and Yelp and actually book you a dinner table without leaving the chat; Voice mode learned to accept file uploads; and "Sign in with ChatGPT" went into beta on sites like Notion, HubSpot, GitLab, and Vercel, the way "Sign in with Google" works today. A little housekeeping too: the old DALL·E GPT retires on August 30 (use ChatGPT Images instead) and the o3 model gets switched off August 26.

Put it all together and you can see the strategy. OpenAI isn't just selling a smarter answer machine anymore — it's trying to become the place you start, the button you log in with, and the thing that books the table. Removing limits and upgrading the free tier is how you get a billion weekly users to stop thinking of it as a tool they visit and start treating it as somewhere they simply are.

Mistral AI

This Week in Mistral AI

Mistral is the French AI company that plays the role of the scrappy European alternative to the American giants. Its trademark move is releasing "open-weight" models — meaning it hands out the actual guts of the model so companies can run it on their own computers instead of renting it from someone else's. For businesses that can't legally ship their data overseas, that's the whole ballgame.

This week itself was on the quiet side. The one concrete release was Leanstral 1.5, an updated model that specializes in formal mathematical proofs written in a language called Lean 4 — niche, admittedly, but the kind of thing that matters if you care about AI that can actually prove it's right rather than just sounding right, and the update improves how much context it can hold while reasoning through long arguments.

The more interesting story is the shape of the last few weeks, which is where Mistral's real ambition shows. Le Chat was rebranded to Mistral Vibe and turned into a proper agent that can catch up on your inbox and calendar, run deep research, draft documents, and take a coding task all the way from request to a merged pull request. There's Voxtral TTS, an open-weights text-to-speech model; Shieldstral for safety filtering; Forge, which lets a company train a frontier-grade model on its own private knowledge; and Robostral Navigate, which lets a robot find its way around a building using one camera and plain-English instructions. Microsoft expanded its partnership with Mistral in July aimed squarely at regulated industries, and Mistral is building a 10-megawatt data center near Paris while reportedly poking at designing its own chips.

That's a company deliberately trying to own every layer — the chips, the data center, the models, the agent on top — rather than renting any of it. For anyone outside the US watching a handful of American labs consolidate power, Mistral is the bet that a credible alternative can exist. This week was a breather; the trajectory is anything but.

Anthropic Claude CoWork

This Week in Claude CoWork

If you haven't met Cowork yet, think of it as Claude's version of a personal assistant that can actually touch your files and run tasks in the background, not just chat with you. It's the mode that lets Claude organize folders, build documents, and keep working on things even after you've closed the laptop.

This week the story is mostly about Cowork growing up for the workplace. Anthropic quietly rolled out richer admin dashboards for Claude Enterprise, so IT teams can now see exactly who's using what, how much it costs, and get alerts before spending gets out of hand — basically a fuel gauge for AI usage. Right alongside that, Claude Code (Cowork's coding-focused cousin) picked up a public beta for self-hosted environments, meaning bigger companies can now run their AI coding sessions on their own servers instead of Anthropic's, which matters a lot if you work somewhere with strict security rules. Anthropic also added security scanning for skills and plugins, automatically checking any third-party add-on for shady code before it gets installed, plus new "inference hooks" that let compliance teams inspect what Claude is doing in real time across chat, Cowork, and Claude Code. None of this is flashy, but it's the plumbing that lets bigger, more cautious organizations actually trust an AI agent with real work.

The bigger picture: this all builds on July's news that Cowork is expanding beyond your desktop to the web and mobile apps, running as remote sessions that keep working even when your device is off. Put together, Anthropic is clearly building toward a world where Cowork isn't just a neat trick on your computer — it's infrastructure a whole company can lean on.

Eleven Labs

This Week in Eleven Labs

Eleven Labs is the company behind some of the most realistic AI voices around — the tech that can read text aloud, dub a video into another language, or transcribe a meeting, all sounding shockingly human. Lately they've been pitching themselves less as a fun voice-cloning toy and more as a serious tool businesses actually run on.

The most useful update this week is under the hood of their live transcription tool, called Scribe. It now supports "keyterms," which lets you tell the AI ahead of time about specific names, jargon, or brand words so it stops mishearing them — handy if your industry has words spellcheck has never heard of. There's also a new "no verbatim" mode that automatically strips out the "ums," false starts, and stumbles from a transcript, so what you get reads like a clean script instead of a messy recording of how people actually talk. On the developer side, Eleven Labs stretched the timeout for their MCP tool connections from 120 seconds to a full 5 minutes, which sounds technical but basically means the tools that plug Eleven Labs into other AI agents are now far less likely to time out and fail on longer tasks.

None of this is a headline-grabbing new voice or model, but it's the kind of quiet reliability work that matters most once a tool moves from "cool demo" to "thing my business depends on every day."

🔍 Tool Spotlight

OpenAI Astra

Astra is OpenAI's next big model, and instead of launching it with a flashy demo, they introduced it by having it solve ten math problems that had stumped human mathematicians for years — one of them, a puzzle about "non-sofic groups," had been open since 1999. That's a big deal, because math proofs are usually the last place people expect AI to shine; you can't fake your way through one, it either holds up or it doesn't.

What makes this genuinely impressive is that OpenAI didn't just say "trust us." They published the entire proofs as machine-checkable files in a system called Lean, plus a 249-page manuscript, all free on GitHub. That means any mathematician, or honestly anyone with the right free software installed, can independently verify every single proof line by line without taking OpenAI's word for it. And it reportedly cost around $2,000 in computing power to find all ten answers — pocket change for this kind of result. Fields Medal-winning mathematicians have already started reacting positively, while noting the work still needs careful digestion.

Astra itself isn't public yet, so you can't use it. But it's a preview of AI tackling genuinely novel, unsolved problems in serious fields, not just remixing what's already known — worth watching for anyone curious where AI reasoning is actually headed next.

Anthropic Claude

This Week in Anthropic Claude

Claude is Anthropic's family of AI models, the ones you chat with for everything from homework help to writing code, and this past week the company kept tinkering with both the brains and the wallet side of things. The biggest headline is Claude Opus 5, which landed in late July as the new top-tier model, and it's still making waves this week because of what it can quietly do in the background: switch tools mid-conversation, catch itself before making a mistake and fall back to a safer model automatically, and even generate visual output. Think of it like a chef who can swap knives mid-recipe without stopping to explain why.

On the money side, Anthropic confirmed that Claude Sonnet 5's discounted launch pricing is ending August 31, after which it jumps from $2/$10 per million tokens to $3/$15. That's the kind of detail that mostly matters to developers and businesses building on top of Claude, but it's a reminder that even AI companies eventually stop offering "grand opening" deals. Meanwhile Claude Code, the coding-focused version of Claude, got a temporary usage boost extended through August 19, plus a new public beta letting companies run Claude Code sessions on their own private servers instead of Anthropic's cloud, useful for teams with strict security rules. Anthropic also rolled out a security scanner for Enterprise customers that checks third-party plugins and skills for anything malicious before they get installed.

Put together, the week reads like Anthropic tightening the bolts rather than launching anything flashy: better safety nets, more deployment options, some bug fixes to Claude Code's interface, and a pricing clock quietly ticking down. Nothing here changes what Claude can do for the average person today, but it signals a company focused on making its tools more trustworthy and flexible for the businesses building serious things on top of them.

Perplexity AI

This Week in Perplexity AI

Perplexity started life as an "AI search engine" that answers questions with cited sources instead of just a list of links, but lately it's been racing to become something bigger: a full AI assistant that can act on your behalf. This week's news fits that pattern perfectly, with most of the action happening inside "Perplexity Computer," the company's agent that can actually use apps and websites for you rather than just answering questions about them.

The standout update is that Computer now has Projects with shared files, a "Brain" feature, a Windows version of Personal Computer, a Model Council mode that appears to let multiple AI models weigh in together, and support for the Kimi K3 model, alongside better voice conversation handling. Perplexity also expanded which underlying models it can tap into, adding GPT-5.4 and GPT-5.4 Thinking for sharper reasoning and coding, plus GPT-5.6 Terra and Sol, while GPT-5.5 quietly became the default brain running Computer. On the developer side, Perplexity's Agent API picked up support for outside models like Claude Opus 4.7 and Grok 4.20 Reasoning, and its MCP Server, a kind of plug that lets other apps borrow Perplexity's search tools, now installs in one click inside Cursor, VS Code, Claude Desktop, and Claude Code.

The throughline is that Perplexity is betting its future less on "answer my question" and more on "handle this for me," stacking in shared projects, memory-like features, and cross-app installs at a fast clip. For everyday users, that means the assistant is getting noticeably more capable at multi-step tasks, though it also means the product is changing shape quickly enough that it might be worth checking in on what's new every so often.

Google Gemini

This Week in Google Gemini

Gemini is Google's flagship AI, the brain baked into everything from Search to Android to Google Docs. If you've talked to an AI inside a Google product recently, odds are Gemini answered you. And this week, Gemini leaned hard into one thing: making video feel less like a rendering job and more like a conversation.

The headline move is Gemini Omni Flash, a new video model that lets you build and edit clips just by describing what you want, whether you're starting from text, a photo, an old video clip, or even a snippet of audio. Think of it like editing a movie by chatting with a very fast, very literal film crew instead of dragging clips around a timeline. It's already showing up across the Gemini app, Google's Flow video tool, AI Studio, the developer API, and the Enterprise Agent Platform, so it's not a one-off demo, it's being wired into everything at once. Alongside that, Gemini is quietly getting smarter about the physical world too: Google opened up its WeatherNext forecasting models, including a new one built specifically for tracking cyclones, giving Gemini-powered tools sharper storm predictions built from thousands of simulated scenarios rather than a single best guess. And back in the classroom, Gemini is rolling out tools that let teachers generate grading rubrics instantly from an assignment, plus new study aids for students like flashcards and practice quizzes tied directly to their class materials.

The common thread here is Gemini pushing outward from "chatbot you type into" toward "assistant baked into whatever you're already doing," whether that's editing a video, checking the weather, or grading homework. For regular users, that mostly means less friction: fewer separate apps, more of the AI just showing up where you need it.

Meta Llama

This Week in Meta Llama

Llama has been Meta's answer to "what if the best AI models were free for anyone to download and tinker with?" For a few years, that open approach made Llama the go-to foundation for developers who didn't want to be locked into a paid API. But this week brought a real plot twist in that story.

Meta just launched Muse Spark, a brand-new AI model, and unlike Llama, it's proprietary, meaning you can't just download the weights and run it yourself. That's a notable shift for a company that built its AI reputation on being the open alternative to the likes of OpenAI and Anthropic. The backdrop makes it easier to understand why: open-weight competitors from Chinese labs like Alibaba and DeepSeek have been eating into Llama's dominance, reportedly accounting for a huge share of open-model downloads by late last year, with newer releases like Alibaba's Qwen and Zhipu's GLM even out-benchmarking Llama 4 on some tests. Meta says it still plans to open-source future versions down the line, so this isn't necessarily Llama's funeral, more like a hedge while the company regroups. On a smaller note, Meta also wrapped up its Llama Impact Grants, handing over $1.5 million to ten international teams using Llama for things like nonprofit and public-good projects.

The bigger picture: the "open vs. closed" AI race is getting messier, and Meta blinking toward a closed model, even partially, is a sign of just how competitive that open-source lane has become. Worth watching whether Muse Spark stays a one-off experiment or becomes the new default.
