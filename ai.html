Anthropic Claude CoWork

This Week in Anthropic Claude CoWork

Claude Cowork is Anthropic's answer to a very ordinary complaint: nobody wants to babysit a chatbot through a task. You describe what you need done, walk away, and come back to a finished folder of documents, cleaned-up files or a real piece of research, because it runs on the same agent engine that powers Claude Code but without asking you to touch a terminal. This week it grew up in a way that's very on-brand for Anthropic — less flash, more plumbing for people who actually run businesses.

The headline is Claude for Financial Advisors, a plugin that launched September 14th and bundles connectors straight into custodians, asset managers and wealth-tech platforms like Schwab and BlackRock, alongside skills built around an advisor's actual daily grind: prepping for a client meeting, checking a portfolio, running a compliance pass. The plugin itself is free through the Cowork plugin browser for firms with an Enterprise license, though a seat with Cowork attached still runs somewhere in the $70 to $120 range per person per month, and Anthropic is dangling a one-time usage credit for any firm that signs up before the end of September. It's a pointed bet that the boring, regulated, paperwork-heavy jobs are where an AI agent earns its keep fastest.

Anthropic also kept tightening the machinery underneath. Smart Reports, a beta feature for Claude Enterprise, now turns raw usage data into an actual readable report — prose and charts on what kind of work got done, what it cost, and where employees kept getting stuck — instead of dumping a spreadsheet of session counts on an IT manager and calling it a day. Organizations get ten of those reports free each month during the beta, though someone with owner-level access has to flip it on first, since it ships off by default. Meanwhile the built-in browser inside Cowork, the side panel that lets Claude click around a website on your behalf, quietly switched from opt-in to on-by-default for Enterprise customers starting September 10th, and the Compliance API's session endpoints came out of beta, which is the unglamorous kind of update that lets a company's legal team actually sign off on letting an agent loose.

None of this is the sort of thing that trends on social media, and that's sort of the point. Cowork's story this month is about becoming boring enough — auditable, budgeted, permission-gated — that a bank or a wealth-management shop will actually let it near real client work.

Eleven Labs

This Week in Eleven Labs

ElevenLabs made its name cloning voices convincingly enough to fool your own grandmother, and it's since branched out into dubbing videos into other languages and turning spoken audio into clean text. This week's news skewed toward the parts of the product that developers and businesses touch rather than anything you'd notice as a casual listener, but the direction is clear: ElevenLabs wants to be infrastructure, not just a fun demo.

The biggest piece is the ElevenLabs CLI, which hit version 1.0.0 on September 6th and puts the company's entire API into a terminal window — every operation as a command, with JSON, table, YAML or CSV output and shell autocomplete built in. The part that matters most is that it lets you manage voice agents "as code," meaning an agent's settings, tools and tests live as ordinary files you can save, share and roll back, rather than click-through settings buried in a dashboard somewhere. It even ships with a dry-run mode so you can preview a change before it actually touches anything live, the same safety net programmers expect from real software but rarely get from AI tools.

Right alongside that, Procedures inside ElevenAgents (the company's voice-agent builder) reached general availability. A procedure is basically a specific cheat sheet with its own trigger, so instead of cramming every possible instruction into one giant system prompt, an agent can load just the relevant playbook the moment a conversation calls for it — a customer-service bot handling a refund pulls up the refund playbook, and a completely different one for a password reset. Then on September 11th came Scribe v2 Medical, a version of ElevenLabs' transcription model fine-tuned specifically on clinical audio, cutting errors on drug names and medical terms by about 15% compared to the standard model, and it's eligible for HIPAA business agreements, so hospitals and clinics can actually use it on real patient recordings. Smaller additions rounded out the week too: phone-based agents can now handle DTMF keypad input (the beeps you make pressing 1 for English), transcripts can carry file attachments, dubbing projects got better pagination, and there are now waveform visuals for music generation.

Taken together, it's a week of ElevenLabs shoring up the boring-but-essential edges — developer tooling, medical-grade accuracy, phone-system compatibility — that decide whether a voice AI stays a novelty or ends up answering your doctor's office phone.

🔍 Tool Spotlight

Siri AI

Siri has been around since 2011, but for most of that time it's felt frozen — great at setting a timer, hopeless at almost anything more complicated. That changed this week, when Apple shipped a genuinely rebuilt Siri AI alongside iOS 27, the biggest overhaul the assistant has had in over a decade. The new Siri can actually see what's on your screen and act on it, dig back through your texts, emails and photos to surface something a friend mentioned weeks ago, and chain actions across more than 300,000 third-party apps — so asking it to book the restaurant your friend texted you about isn't a punchline anymore, it's the whole idea. It also got its own dedicated app, with conversation history that syncs privately across your iPhone, iPad, Mac and Watch, so you can start a chat on one device and pick it back up on another. There are catches: it's still officially in beta, it only speaks English at launch with more languages landing in October, and the deepest features need a chip no older than an iPhone 15 Pro's A17 Pro, which quietly splits Apple's own customers into who gets the good version and who doesn't. Anyone who's ever snapped "that's not what I asked" at Siri will notice the difference fast.

ChatGPT

This Week in ChatGPT

ChatGPT is the chatbot that started the whole AI boom — you type a question, it types back, and these days it can also write your code, read your files and click around the web for you. This week it got the biggest upgrade it's had in a long while, because OpenAI's brand-new flagship model, GPT-6 "Astra," finished rolling out to everyday users after landing on September 3rd.

Astra is built for a different kind of work than the old chatbots. Instead of just answering questions, it's designed to actually operate a computer — browsing sites, writing and debugging software, and grinding through long, multi-step projects without losing the thread. The benchmark numbers are the sort that make researchers sit up: it scored 98% on FrontierMath Tier 4, a test of genuinely brutal research-level math problems, and 99.9% on ARC-AGI-3, a puzzle benchmark specifically built to be easy for humans and hard for machines. Those tests were supposed to last years. Astra basically finished them. The practical version of that power is quieter but arguably more useful: Astra can now build documents, spreadsheets and slide decks that follow your templates and formatting rules, and it adjusts when you change your mind halfway through instead of starting over.

Around the big model launch, OpenAI shipped a handful of smaller things that matter if you actually live in the app. The desktop version finally arrived on Linux in public preview — Ubuntu, Debian and Fedora — which closes a gap that had annoyed developers for years. Projects got a memory switch you can flip after the fact, so you can tell ChatGPT to only remember things from inside a specific project and stay ignorant of everything else, which is handy when you don't want your work notes bleeding into your dinner-recipe chats. And Zendesk and OneNote connections went into beta, letting ChatGPT reach into your support tickets and your notebooks.

The bigger picture: the pitch for ChatGPT has officially shifted from "ask it things" to "hand it work." A model that can run a browser and produce a finished spreadsheet is less like a search engine and more like an intern. That's genuinely useful, and it's also the reason the safety and oversight conversation is getting louder — the more independently these systems can act, the more it matters that someone is checking what they did.

Mistral AI

This Week in Mistral AI

Mistral is the French startup that became Europe's answer to OpenAI, best known for releasing powerful models that anyone can download and run themselves rather than renting through someone else's website. This week it made headlines for money first and technology second — on September 8th it closed a €3 billion funding round that values the company at more than €21 billion.

That's not just a big number, it's a record: the largest equity raise any European tech company has ever pulled off, and Mistral is only three years old. Samsung Electronics led the round, joined by the Scaleup Europe Fund (managed by EQT) and existing backer PSG Equity. The Samsung involvement is the detail worth noticing — a company that puts chips and screens in hundreds of millions of phones and TVs now has a serious stake in a European AI lab. Mistral says the money goes toward research and infrastructure, which in this industry mostly means buying an enormous amount of computing hardware, since training frontier models is less like writing software and more like running a power-hungry factory.

The engineering team wasn't idle either. Mistral OCR 4.1 became generally available, meaning its tool for reading text out of scanned documents and photos is now stable enough for real business use. It also shipped Leanstral 1.5, a niche but fascinating model that writes formal mathematical proofs in a language called Lean 4 — proofs a computer can verify line by line, so there's no hand-waving. And it launched Agentic Search, a retrieval layer that helps AI systems dig through complicated documents more accurately while using fewer steps and fewer tokens, which in plain terms means faster answers that cost less.

Put together, it's a strong week for the argument that the AI race isn't purely an American story. Mistral now has record-breaking European money, a hardware giant in its corner, and a product line aimed squarely at businesses that want AI they can inspect and run on their own terms.
