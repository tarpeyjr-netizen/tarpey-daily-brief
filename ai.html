Google Gemini

This Week in Google Gemini

Google Gemini is the AI that runs through Google’s apps, its Pixel phones and the tools coders use to build their own AI projects. This week Gemini had two big jobs at once. It moved deeper into your phone, and it shipped two new Gemini 4 models for the people who build apps.

The phone news came first. The November Pixel Drop, which started rolling out on November 10, brings “Gemini Intelligence” to the Pixel 10 family (but not the cheaper Pixel 10a). The fun part is Rambler, a voice-typing tool that cuts out every “um” and “like” and hands you a clean message to check before you send it. Gemini can now also run errands in Japan, the UK and India, like ordering groceries, booking a ride or grabbing a restaurant table. You can even describe a home-screen widget, say a countdown to your next trip, and Gemini builds it for you. On the Pixel 11 in the US, Call Screen got smarter too. Gemini now chats with unknown callers to find out what they want, a bit like a polite receptionist who screens your calls.

Under the hood, the developer side was even busier. Google’s Gemini API changelog shows Gemini 4 Carbon going generally available on November 10, at an introductory price of $2 per million input tokens (a token is roughly a chunk of a word) through November 30. Two days later came Gemini 4 Hydrogen, a cheaper model that even works on the free tier and adds an extra-hard “xhigh” thinking setting for tough problems. In between, on November 11, Google launched Gemini Decision 1, a model that does one thing: it makes a call. Show it text, a photo, audio, a video or a PDF and ask a yes-or-no or pick-a-category question, and it answers without writing a single word of chatter. Think of it as a referee instead of a commentator.

So what does it all add up to? Gemini is turning from a chatbot you visit into a helper that quietly does stuff, on your phone and inside other people’s apps. The Pixel Drop keeps rolling out over the next few weeks, so if your phone hasn’t picked up the new tricks yet, it probably will soon.

Cursor

This Week in Cursor

Cursor is a code editor with AI agents built in. You describe what you want, and the agents write, test and fix the code with you. This week brought one fun new feature and one serious bit of homework.

The fun part landed on November 10, when Cursor Projects went multiplayer. Projects, which launched in September, used to be a solo space. Now you can invite teammates. Everyone gets a shared task board showing who is working on what and what each person’s agents are up to. Each person still has a private chat, but decisions and design docs live in a shared layer every agent can read. The neat twist is that the agents talk to each other. If yours and a teammate’s are about to change the same database, they can agree on an order first, like two movers deciding who carries the couch through the door. When someone new takes over the on-call shift, their agent can pick up the work already in progress. It is in beta and rolling out to everyone.

The serious part came on November 13, when Cursor posted an update on a recent security incident. An attacker got hold of an access key Cursor had given to Fireworks, one of its AI partners, and copied stored coding-session records, mostly from 2025. Cursor says users in Privacy Mode were not affected. Fewer than 0.05% of other users had their records exposed, and those people were contacted directly. The attacker also slipped fake copies of Anthropic’s Claude Code extension into Cursor’s extension store for about 11 hours on October 29. Cursor says the last attacker activity it saw was on November 1. Since then it has swapped out its passwords and keys, rebuilt the extension store and added scanning that catches leaked passwords.

The takeaway is simple. If Cursor emailed you, change the keys it listed. If you installed Claude Code versions 2.1.312 or 2.1.313 from Cursor’s store, follow Cursor’s October 29 cleanup steps. Then go enjoy coding with your friends, and your friends’ robots.

Microsoft Copilot

This Week in Microsoft Copilot

Microsoft Copilot is the AI helper built into Word, Excel, PowerPoint and the rest of the Office apps that schools and offices run on. Last week we said the big September redesign was moving in. This week the boxes got unpacked, and the new stuff is a pile of small, practical “skills” that do the boring parts of homework and office work for you.

The handiest one lives in Excel. A new “clean data” skill, covered by Geeky Gadgets on November 10, hunts through a messy spreadsheet for duplicates, mixed-up capital letters and dates typed three different ways. Then it suggests fixes, and nothing changes until you say yes. Think of it as a tidy friend who asks before they rearrange your room. PowerPoint gets a “clarify my thinking” skill that quizzes you about what your talk is really for before it drafts slides. Word can now line up a jumble of pictures into a neat grid. Microsoft’s own release notes add more: Copilot can now borrow the look of a deck you attach, so new slides match your style. Excel answers now link straight to the cells Copilot changed. And Cowork, the mode where Copilot actually does tasks for you, can now click through websites in a separate Edge tab while your passwords stay on your own computer.

There is a catch for grown-ups paying the bills. Microsoft is rolling out usage-based billing in Copilot Chat and the Office apps this month, which works like a phone plan with a meter, per a November roundup from A Guide to Cloud. Autopilot, the always-on agent that keeps working after you close your laptop, still has no firm launch date. Microsoft’s notice, updated November 9, says the timing will come later.

That makes this coming week the one to watch. Microsoft Ignite opens Tuesday, November 17, in San Francisco and runs through the 20th. If Autopilot gets a real launch date, that is probably where we will hear it.

🔍 Tool Spotlight

Tencent Hy4

Hy4 is the newest flagship AI model from Tencent, the Chinese tech giant behind WeChat. Tencent released the full version on November 4. An earlier preview could only handle text, and this version adds two big skills. First, it can see. It reads detailed images, makes sense of charts and works through math problems that come with pictures. Second, it can use a computer. Hy4 can run command-line tools and click around regular apps, and Tencent says it trained the model on professional software like Blender, the 3D animation program, and CAD design tools. Picture an assistant that can actually open the software and do the work, not just explain the steps.

It is huge, reportedly a 770-billion-parameter model that can hold about a million tokens in its memory at once, roughly a stack of several long novels. Developers can use it through Tencent Cloud and OpenRouter. The surprising part: Tencent promised to release the open weights soon, but as of the latest reports they were not public yet. Its impressive benchmark scores come from Tencent itself, so wait for independent tests.

This Week in ChatGPT

ChatGPT is the chatbot most people picture when they hear "AI": you type, or talk, and it answers. This week, though, OpenAI pointed it at something new. It wants ChatGPT to be a place where you meet businesses too, not just a place where you ask questions.

It also remembers saved reference pictures of people, pets and characters, so you don't have to upload them again.

The rest of the week was quieter housekeeping. On November 12, admins for school and company accounts got a way to change users' email addresses. OpenAI also posted new help docs on giving custom instructions to "dot," the assistant it brought to Enterprise and Edu workspaces earlier this month. If Business Hub takes off, asking ChatGPT "where should I buy new sneakers?" might soon put you in a chat with the shop itself. That's handy, but it's also a good reason to keep asking who's really answering you.

OpenAI Codex

This Week in OpenAI Codex

Codex is OpenAI's coding agent. Picture a helper that lives in your terminal, the text window programmers type commands into, and can read, write and fix code for you. This week it shipped updates almost every other day, and together they make the terminal feel a lot less like a plain black box.

Then today brought version 0.168.0, the most playful one yet. It even shows image previews and "pets" inside tmux on the Ghostty and Kitty terminals. That's a lot of polish for one week. The takeaway: OpenAI wants Codex to feel like a full workspace, not just a code robot, and it's adding pieces fast.

Anthropic Claude Code

This Week in Anthropic Claude Code

Claude Code is Anthropic’s coding helper that lives in your terminal. You describe what you want, and it reads your project, writes the code, and runs the commands. It doesn’t do big launch events. It ships small updates every few days, and this week brought three of them, one with a brand-new brain inside.

The big one came on November 9. Version 2.1.321 made Claude Fable 5.2, the model Anthropic announced that same day, the new default Fable model in Claude Code. It can hold a 1-million-token context window, which is roughly like keeping a whole shelf of textbooks open at once, so it can see a lot of your project at the same time. That update also closed several security gaps. One let login secrets for connected tools follow a redirect to a different website. Another let a file copied with cp or mv slip past rules meant to block it. Those are the kind of quiet fixes you never notice, which is exactly the point. A few days earlier, on November 6, version 2.1.319 let tasks from connected tools run in the background and report back when they finish, like starting the laundry and getting a ping when it’s done. It also added a setting that simply says “no” instead of stopping to ask permission, and the /usage screen started showing spending totals for teams. Then on November 10, version 2.1.322 brought “fast mode” to Google’s Vertex cloud in the global, US and EU regions. If Vertex turns it down, Claude Code just drops back to normal speed.

The so-what is that the tool is getting a stronger brain and tighter locks at the same time. One date to watch is November 30, when fast mode for the older Opus 4.8 model switches off for good. Anthropic is pointing people to Opus 5.5 instead.

ElevenLabs

This Week in ElevenLabs

ElevenLabs is the company best known for AI voices that sound uncannily human, but this week it was all about the opposite job: listening. On November 10 it launched Scribe v3 and Scribe v3 Live, two new models that turn speech into text. One handles recordings, like a podcast you upload. The other works in real time, like live captions during a call.

The numbers are the story here. ElevenLabs says Scribe v3 makes 27 percent fewer word mistakes than the version before it, tested across 102 languages. The live version cuts “meaning-changing” mistakes by 43 percent, which matters because hearing “can’t” as “can” is a lot worse than missing a comma. The live model can now also tell who is talking in real time, and it can follow you if you switch languages mid-sentence. The biggest jumps came in trickier languages, with Greek improving by 77 percent in live mode. One energy company said its phone agent got Dutch postcodes right on the first try 40 percent more often. The same day, Adobe named ElevenLabs as one of the partners whose models creators can use inside Firefly, so its voices are showing up in even more places.

Why should you care? Every voice assistant is only as smart as its ears. If the AI mishears you, even a brilliant answer is an answer to the wrong question. Better listening means smoother customer-service bots, better captions and fewer “sorry, I didn’t catch that” moments. Paired with the voice upgrades listed in its November 2 changelog, ElevenLabs is clearly building toward AI you can just talk to.

MiniMax H3-Image

H3-Image is a brand-new AI image model from MiniMax, a Chinese company that also runs the Hailuo AI video app. It launched on November 11, and it can create pictures from a text description, edit pictures you already have, and borrow style or details from up to 16 reference images at once. One catch: the launch post doesn’t say which license comes with the weights, so check the fine print before building anything serious with it.

Perplexity AI

This Week in Perplexity AI

Perplexity is the AI-powered answer engine that searches the web and hands you a summary with sources, instead of a list of blue links. This week it tried to become more than a search box by launching Teammates, AI agents meant to do ongoing work rather than answer one question and disappear.

Here is how it works. You give a Teammate a job, such as watching bug reports, tracking numbers, or researching a market. It plans the work, does it, and brings results back for you to check, then keeps monitoring afterward. Big goals can be split among extra agents, each running on its own isolated computer, and agents in the same project can share files and message each other like coworkers in a group chat. They plug into Slack, GitHub, Linear and Gmail, and you can jump in and take over whenever you like. You can pick which AI model powers them, and new Teammates default to Claude Opus 5.5. For now it is a research preview on the web for individual Max subscribers only, and heavier work burns through monthly credits faster. Reports also mention a smarter Comet Assistant in the company’s browser, announced around November 6, though sources disagree on the timing of a related patent-search feature.

The big idea is that Perplexity wants to compete with OpenAI and Anthropic on workplace automation, not just search. Teammates is early and pricey, so expect it to change a lot before it reaches everyone. Perplexity also has a legal fight with Dow Jones and the New York Post still going, which is worth watching.
