Nashville, TN

Government & Development

Nashville's Metro Council took the unusual step of pointing eminent domain at a data center, voting 27–5 in the early hours of Aug. 5 to authorize Metro to acquire the 23.5-acre South Nashville parcel that DC BLOX bought for roughly $23 million next to the Nashville Zoo. Mayor Freddie O'Connell's administration backed the measure, saying the site would serve public uses such as offices, warehouse space and training facilities, while the zoo and neighborhood groups had campaigned loudly against a 69,200-square-foot, 10-megawatt facility rising on their doorstep. The Nashville Banner reported the council first authorized negotiations with DC BLOX, with condemnation as the fallback if a deal can't be reached. The catch is price: WSMV notes Metro would likely owe at least $37 million, well above what the company paid. The vote is a notable marker in the national fight over where data centers get built, and Nashville has now planted a flag on the side of the neighbors.

Economics & Public Finance

A second big-money item moved at the council's Aug. 18 meeting: legislation to refinance Music City Center debt and route $300 million in excess tourism tax revenue toward East Bank infrastructure. The companion resolution passed 31–6 and the ordinance cleared second reading, with a final vote set for Sept. 1. The mechanics matter more than the headline number — refinancing releases Metro's backstop on the convention center debt, freeing borrowing capacity that city officials want to aim at an affordable housing bond program, and the package also clears the way for a Music City Center expansion that could run to $1 billion. The $300 million redirect traces back to state legislation moving the tourism surplus to the East Bank and lower Broadway, a running source of friction between Metro and Republican leaders on Capitol Hill. Separately, Council Member Sandra Sepulveda is pressing ahead with a bill to ban right turns on red across much of the city, citing 21 pedestrian deaths on Nashville roads so far in 2026 — double last year's pace at the same point.

Boston, MA

Crime & Public Safety

A string of violent incidents in and around Downtown Crossing has pushed public safety to the top of Boston's civic agenda this month. After assaults and a stabbing rattled merchants and commuters, civic leaders and the Wu administration convened a series of emergency meetings on downtown safety in early August, with retailers describing juvenile theft, vandalism, and assaults as increasingly untenable for street-level businesses. The Boston Police Department followed with a targeted shoplifting operation in Downtown Crossing on August 6 that produced multiple arrests and summonses, and the department has since expanded patrols across the district at the urging of the downtown neighborhood association. The pressure has not let up: police are now investigating a series of overnight commercial burglaries that began August 21, in which a suspect used a pry tool to force entry into businesses. For a district that anchors both the city's retail tax base and its tourism economy, the question heading into fall is whether a heavier police footprint alone can restore confidence, or whether the underlying issues around juvenile services and the downtown's post-pandemic foot-traffic mix require a broader response.

Business & Development

The Red Sox are leaving Fenway Park — at least as an office address. The club has leased the top three floors of a 13-story Boylston Street building steps from the ballpark and will vacate the cramped offices tucked inside Fenway, consolidating staff from the team, Fenway Sports Group, and Fenway Sports Management under one roof. The move is more than a real estate shuffle: the 250,000-square-foot building is the opening phase of Fenway Corners, the roughly $1.6 billion mixed-use project the team is developing with WS Development, which envisions eight new buildings ringing the ballpark. Landing an anchor tenant — even a related one — is a meaningful signal in a Boston office market still working through elevated vacancy and soft demand for older Class B space. For the Fenway neighborhood, it accelerates a decade-long transformation from game-day parking lots into a year-round district, though the pace of the remaining phases will depend on financing conditions and city permitting.

Baltimore, MD

Government & Utilities

Baltimore's long-running fight with its dominant utility over who pays to maintain the city-owned underground conduit system came to a head this month. Mayor Brandon Scott proposed a new franchise rate of $4.05 per linear foot beginning January 1, 2027, indexed to inflation, replacing both the 2023 capital-investment deal struck with Baltimore Gas & Electric and the $2.20 rate other users have paid since 2019. City officials point to the underground fires of 2024 and 2025 as evidence that the roughly 700-mile network needs sustained funding rather than one-off construction commitments. BGE pushed back hard, arguing the existing arrangement — under which it is on track to deliver about $120 million in capital improvements by the end of 2026 — is already producing better results at lower cost, and warning that any increase will land on ratepayers' bills. The Board of Estimates approved the hike over the utility's objections, setting up a revenue stream the city says is essential but that consumer advocates will be watching closely.

Crime & Public Safety

Baltimore's violence-reduction work drew national recognition again this week, as the city placed second among 100 cities in the 2025 Violence Prevention Index for the second consecutive year, cited for its public-health-centered approach to intervention. The ranking lands alongside city data showing homicides down an additional 22.9% and nonfatal shootings down 6.7% year over year as of August 19 — declines stacked on top of the historic drops recorded in 2023, 2024 and 2025. The strategy leans on the Group Violence Reduction Strategy, community violence intervention teams, and the Mayor's Office of Neighborhood Safety and Engagement rather than enforcement alone. The picture is not uniform: the Baltimore Sun's homicide tracker logged multiple killings in the first half of August, a reminder that aggregate improvement still leaves individual neighborhoods absorbing loss. For a city that spent decades as shorthand for American urban violence, the sustained multi-year trend is the more consequential story.

Business & Development

The roughly $1 billion remake of Harborplace is finally moving from renderings toward shovels, with MCB Real Estate's David Bramble targeting a fall 2026 start on the parks and promenade phase. The master plan, approved by more than 60% of Baltimore voters in a 2024 charter referendum, replaces the largely vacant Inner Harbor pavilions with four buildings holding about 900 apartments and more than 200,000 square feet of commercial space, plus a 2.3-acre park between Pratt and Light streets planted with roughly 500 trees. Gensler's Baltimore office is leading design on the signature nine-level "Sail" building, which is slated to include a public market, food hall and rooftop viewing park. Full buildout is not expected before 2031, and financing for the residential towers remains unresolved — the gap between an approved plan and a funded one. Whether the project resets downtown's trajectory or becomes another delayed waterfront promise is the question Baltimore will be answering for the next five years.

Charlotte, NC

Government & Transportation

Charlotte's standoff with Raleigh over the proposed Interstate 77 South toll lanes hardened this month, with council members signaling they will not reverse their earlier vote against the project. After the council rescinded its support, the General Assembly attached a penalty in early July requiring Charlotte and neighboring local governments to repay roughly $60 million in state design costs if the managed-lane plan is not approved — a threat that drew a room full of toll opponents to the transportation committee's August meeting, where members remained defiant. The dispute is now the defining early test for interim Mayor Rob Harrington, the Robinson Bradshaw partner and North Carolina Bar Association president named in June to finish Vi Lyles' term after her resignation. Harrington used a public appearance this week to lay out his thinking on I-77, the city's pending data center rules and downtown teen gatherings. City staff, meanwhile, briefed the Transportation, Planning & Development Committee on a possible data center moratorium, recommending incremental steps — site assessments and community engagement requirements — rather than sweeping reform, with a council-appointed task force taking up the question. How the I-77 fight resolves will determine whether Charlotte absorbs a nine-figure hit or accepts tolling on one of its busiest commuter corridors.

Economics & Business

Charlotte's corporate recruiting run continued through the summer, with several large employers committing to new operations in Mecklenburg County. SMBC Group, one of Japan's largest banks, selected the county for a second U.S. headquarters expected to create roughly 2,000 jobs by the end of 2032, while Capital Group — among the world's biggest active asset managers — chose the region for an East Coast operations hub, reinforcing Charlotte's standing as a financial-services center beyond its legacy banks. On the industrial side, Averitt announced a new regional campus near Charlotte Douglas International Airport, and Siemens Energy said it would further expand locally as part of a $421 million North Carolina expansion adding 500 statewide jobs. Mecklenburg County and the city also handed out the 2026 Crowns of Enterprise Awards to seven local firms, a reminder that the growth story runs alongside a small-business base. Transit planners added a wrinkle of their own, confirming the Red Line commuter rail will include a stop at Camp North End just outside Uptown — a decision that ties the region's largest transit project to one of its most closely watched redevelopment districts.

Orlando, FL

Government & Politics

Orange County's race to replace term-limited Mayor Jerry Demings will go to a second round after none of the four candidates cleared 50% in Tuesday's primary. Clerk of Courts Tiffany Moore Russell finished first with roughly 53,000 votes (32%), and businessman Chris Messina took second with about 45,900 (28%), sending the two to a Nov. 3 runoff. The night's biggest surprise was former U.S. Rep. Stephanie Murphy, widely seen as a front-runner, finishing third at 26%, with County Commissioner Mayra Uribe trailing at 15%. The seat is formally nonpartisan, but Moore Russell is a Democrat and Messina a Republican, so the fall campaign is likely to sharpen along familiar lines in a county that has trended blue in recent cycles. Whoever wins will inherit decisions on transit funding, growth management and the county's tourist-tax revenue — the levers that shape most of metro Orlando's budget.

Development & Preservation

A three-year pause on Orlando's downtown historic-preservation review took effect this month, and developers filing new downtown applications can now skip the certificate of appropriateness normally required by the Historic Preservation Board. For the next 36 months that authority shifts to the Appearance Review Board and the Community Redevelopment Agency. City commissioners approved the moratorium in a contentious 4–2 vote in June, arguing the existing process had made investing in aging downtown buildings slow, costly and unpredictable at a moment when the core badly needs new residential and retail activity. Preservationists counter that removing the board's gatekeeping role invites demolition of century-old structures that cannot be replaced, and several have pressed the city for guardrails before the window closes. The practical test will be what applications land at the CRA over the next few months — adaptive reuse, or teardowns.

Denver, CO

Government & Politics

Denver's City Council has spent the first half of August absorbing a sudden vacancy and wrestling with two contentious policy questions. At-large member Sarah Parady stepped down from her seat on August 5, citing an undisclosed illness, and the council promptly passed a resolution declaring the seat vacant and scheduling a special election for November 3 to run alongside Colorado's general election. In the meantime, the council declined to advance a proposal that would have let bars and entertainment venues serve until 4 a.m., leaving the city's 2 a.m. last call untouched for now. Separately, a council committee began reviewing a request from the Department of Housing Stability to rewrite performance metrics in its 2018 intergovernmental agreement with the Denver Housing Authority — a change that would lower promised unit counts under the DHA Delivers for Denver program, remove the Westridge development, and push some deadlines out by nearly a decade. Taken together, the moves point to a council recalibrating expectations on affordable housing delivery while an at-large seat sits empty heading into the fall budget season.

Economics & Business

Halfway through 2026, the economic picture around Denver is decidedly mixed. Colorado recorded more than 64,000 new business filings in the second quarter — up roughly 25% year over year and running counter to the usual midyear slowdown — but delinquencies climbed nearly 10% over the same period and renewals slipped, suggesting the surge in formation isn't translating cleanly into durable businesses. Inflation remains the sharpest local pressure point: Denver's annual rate is running above the 4.2% national figure and ranked second-highest among twelve tracked metro regions in the most recent Bureau of Labor Statistics reading. Economists convening at the state's midyear economic forum forecast continued growth through year-end but at a slower clip, pointing to decelerating job growth and elevated oil prices tied to geopolitical disruption. On the capital-spending side, Denver International Airport leaders are asking the City Council to approve three contracts worth a combined $248 million, a reminder that public infrastructure remains one of the metro's most reliable sources of economic activity even as private-sector signals wobble.

Tampa–St. Petersburg, FL

Government & Politics

St. Petersburg voters go to the polls tomorrow in a six-way mayoral primary that has become the most closely watched municipal race on Florida's Gulf Coast. Incumbent Ken Welch, elected in 2021 as the city's first Black mayor, is defending his seat against a field that includes former governor and congressman Charlie Crist, sitting City Council member Brandi Gabbard, former fire chief Jim Large, neighborhood association president Kevin Batdorf, and corrections administrator Maria Scruggs. Polling ahead of the vote has shown Crist with a lead over Welch, though a large undecided bloc leaves the outcome unsettled. Because no candidate is expected to clear 50 percent, both frontrunners have publicly prepared for a November 3 runoff. The race has turned on property taxes, storm recovery and the pace of downtown redevelopment — issues that the candidates sparred over at a recent public forum — and the result will shape how the city handles its next round of infrastructure and waterfront decisions.

Economics & Business

Downtown Tampa is absorbing a wave of new small businesses as business applications hit a record high, with Florida ranking third nationally both in applications per capita and in the share of new firms likely to hire multiple employees. Several ventures that began as pop-ups and kiosks at Sparkman Wharf have graduated into permanent storefronts, a pattern the Tampa Downtown Partnership points to as evidence that the district's post-pandemic recovery is now producing durable tenants rather than churn. Partnership CEO Kenyetta Hairston-Bridges has framed the moment as the start of downtown's next growth phase, with ground-floor retail filling in alongside the residential towers delivered over the past few years. The trend is running in parallel with continued suburban expansion — developers are adding housing and a shopping center to the West Port master-planned community and Manatee County Habitat for Humanity is using state funds to build infrastructure for a planned Palmetto townhouse project. Together the activity suggests the region's growth is broadening beyond the large-scale projects that dominated headlines earlier in the decade.
