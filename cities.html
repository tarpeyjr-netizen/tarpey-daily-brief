Boston, MA

Business

The Fish Pier building in South Boston that housed the No Name Restaurant for 102 years will get a second life as a marine robotics and tech facility run by MassRobotics, Massport announced Thursday, November 19. The startup incubator, whose 12 Channel St. headquarters a ten-minute walk away already houses about 100 startups, was chosen from three interested organizations. Massport CEO Rich Davey framed the move as part of the authority's Blue Edge initiative to keep and attract “blue-tech” companies on the waterfront, and MassRobotics executive director Tom Ryden said direct harbor access is the draw, since getting on the water is otherwise hard for underwater-robotics firms. Massport is renovating the two-story building, which has sat empty since the family-run restaurant closed in bankruptcy in 2019, and the hub is expected to open in early 2028.

Collegiate Sports

The 141st edition of The Game came to Fenway Park on Saturday, and Yale beat Harvard 20–17 on a 2-yard Dante Reno touchdown pass to Graham Smith with 3:37 left, then sealed it with Billy Eastep’s interception of Harvard’s final throw. Harvard opened the scoring on Damien Henderson’s 28-yard interception return and got a 115-yard day from DJ Gordon, but Yale picked off Charlie Smith three times. The loss was bittersweet for Andy Aurich’s Crimson (8–2, 5–2 Ivy), who still won the Ivy League title outright thanks to results elsewhere and earned an automatic NCAA playoff berth. Yale took the Big Three title and its fifth straight win in the rivalry, its longest run against Harvard since 1907. Across town the same day, Boston College closed its home schedule with an ACC win over Syracuse.

St. Louis, MO

Government

The St. Louis Board of Aldermen voted 10-2 on Thursday to send Mayor Cara Spencer a bill that would permanently fund the city's Code Blue winter outreach with marijuana tax revenue. Spencer created Code Blue by executive order last year and paid for it with $3 million in Rams settlement money. Last winter the protocol was activated more than four dozen times, adding shelter beds, transportation and outreach whenever temperatures fell below 32 degrees. Lead sponsor Alderwoman Alisha Sonnier (7th Ward) said the goal is to give city departments "the predictability they need" to plan early. City officials expect the 3% tax on recreational marijuana sales to raise about $1.4 million this fiscal year, and the city will also spend $2 million in general revenue on Code Blue this winter.

Crime & Justice

St. Louis Police Chief Robert Tracy signed a new contract Wednesday that keeps his $295,000 salary and his $110,000 raise, even though Mayor Spencer and the police union have both criticized the raise. The Board of Police Commissioners approved the deal in a closed session. It is largely the same as the contract the board approved in October, but it runs about two years shorter and drops a $24,500 retirement contribution. The board says it had to raise Tracy's pay to counter outside job offers. Spencer says she doesn't believe that, and she argues the money comes "at the expense of being able to spend that on other police officers." The Missouri Attorney General is still investigating whether the hastily called October meetings violated the state's Sunshine Law.

Baltimore, MD

Business

Mayor Brandon Scott's administration has reached a community benefits deal with Harborplace developer MCB Real Estate that is tied to a tax break that could abate $366 million over 25 years. Under the deal, MCB would pledge $10 million to the Baltimore Civic Fund or a similar third party and set up a $5 million equity fund so residents can invest in the project. It also sets hiring goals: city residents would work 35% of construction hours and fill 55% of post-construction jobs, 10% of new apartments would carry affordable rents, and 50 YouthWorks participants would be hired each year for a decade. The Board of Estimates is expected to vote on the tax break, the ground lease and the profit-sharing terms at a special hearing on Dec. 2. The financing is still uncertain. Private construction costs have climbed from an estimated $500 million to $820 million, MCB has not yet secured financing, and demolition of the old pavilions has slipped to early 2027.

Read more: Baltimore Banner: Harborplace tax deal

Government

Scott announced Thursday that Baltimore's long-delayed municipal ID, the "410 Card," is expected to launch early next year. City officials authorized the program roughly a decade ago. The card will be free to all residents, and every city agency, including law enforcement, will accept it. Scott also signed a bill that expands the documents residents can use to apply, including school and social services records, and adds privacy protections for those documents. Council member Odette Ramos has pushed the issue since 2022. Supporters say the card will help people without other photo ID, such as residents experiencing homelessness and immigrants without passports, get services like library cards and rental assistance.

Charlotte, NC

Crime & Justice

On Monday, Nov. 16, the Charlotte City Council approved a $1.725 million settlement with protesters who say Charlotte-Mecklenburg Police officers trapped them in uptown during the June 2020 George Floyd protests and hit them with tear gas and pepper balls. Police call the tactic "kettling." The city says each of the 67 claimants will receive $25,746, ending a class-action lawsuit that had dragged on for about five years. The city says the deal avoids a long and expensive trial and is not an admission of liability or wrongdoing by the city or CMPD. An attorney for the protesters told WFAE he hopes the payout leads to more changes within the department. A separate 2021 settlement with civil rights groups had already limited CMPD's use of chemical agents and kettling at protests.

Read more: WFAE: $1.725M to 67 protesters WBTV: Settlement approved WSOC: Kettling lawsuit

Economics & Business

Atrium Health has announced a $387 million expansion of Levine Children's Hospital, which CEO Gene Woods called "one of the largest investments in children's care anywhere in the state to date." The plan adds more than 100 pediatric beds, expands neonatal intensive care, doubles the size of the emergency department, and adds child life and family support services. Atrium says patient volume in greater Charlotte has grown 43% in recent years, and the hospital already treats more than 250 children a day. Construction will be phased and is expected to start in 2027. The announcement came during National Prematurity Awareness Month, with the March of Dimes noting that North Carolina got a D grade for its preterm birth rate for the fourth year in a row. For a fast-growing region, the expansion is a major bet on specialized pediatric care in the city.

Orlando, FL

Government & Politics

Orange County will have its first new mayor in eight years after Clerk of Courts Tiffany Moore Russell declared victory in the November 3 runoff over Winter Park tech entrepreneur Chris Messina. Unofficial AP results showed her with about 63% of the vote (roughly 267,000 votes) to Messina's 37%. She replaces term-limited Mayor Jerry Demings in the nonpartisan race and, according to News 6, becomes the county's sixth mayor and its first Black woman mayor. The campaign centered on Amendment 3, the property-tax cut that voters approved: Moore Russell had opposed it, citing county projections of about a $165 million budget hit next year, while Messina backed it. Her first move is a transition team that includes a subcommittee on county finances, charged with absorbing the Amendment 3 impact without cutting essential services.

Economics & Business

On Wednesday the Greater Orlando Aviation Authority approved a plan to revive the Colonial Promenade, a mostly empty shopping plaza on East Colonial Drive near Orlando Executive Airport. Local developer The Wideman Company will rebrand it as "The Landings at Baldwin Park" under a 30-year lease with two 10-year renewal options. The deal requires the developer to invest at least $20 million in improvements, while the authority keeps ownership and pays nothing toward the work. The plan keeps the west building and its signature arches, with an '80s-inspired color scheme, new landscaping, street art and shade features; the east building would be demolished and could be replaced by a big-box store. The authority first sought a redeveloper in 2018, and the plaza has lost tenants including Sam Ash, Sweet Tomatoes and an Orange County library branch. Its last two tenants are expected to leave by the end of 2026.

Denver, CO

Government

The Colorado Department of Transportation's plan for Bus Rapid Transit on Colorado Boulevard, one of Denver's most congested roads, has been put on hold. CDOT has dropped the option of a center-running bus lane and says it will start a further review in 2028, using performance data from other corridors, Colfax Avenue among them. A coalition of residents from Park Hill, Hilltop and Cherry Creek North has asked for a pause, and about 19 neighborhood associations have signed its resolution. The group is led in part by former state Sen. Penfield Tate III, who questions whether enough people would ride the bus and called Colfax BRT "a mess." On the other side, street safety advocates and RTD point to a CDOT report counting 89 crashes on the boulevard from 2018 to 2022 that killed or seriously injured someone, and they warn that waiting will cost lives. Both sides agree the corridor needs more study, so a decision on one of the city's busiest north-south roads is still years away.

Read more: CBS Colorado: Coalition opposes BRT CBS Colorado: Advocates back BRT

Business

The National Western Center has brought back a plan from more than a decade ago to replace the Denver Coliseum with a 10,000-seat arena. The new arena would sit north of I-70 in the Triangle, a 92-acre area that makes up the final phase of the campus master plan. Mike Bouchard, who runs the city's Office of the National Western Center, says the plan would rebuild bridges, connect the campus to the neighborhoods around it and include affordable housing. Residents of Globeville and Elyria-Swansea, organized as the GES Coalition, want the arena kept south of the highway and housing built on the land to the north. They object to proposed 8- to 12-story buildings, to added traffic and to pollution. Voters turned down funding for the arena in 2021, so the plan is likely to meet strong resistance again.

Tampa-St. Petersburg, FL

Government

St. Petersburg Mayor-elect Charlie Crist has laid out the "Five Guiding Lights" for his transition, labeled Open, Steady, Grow, Ready and Build. They cover an Office of Open Government on day one, protecting core services, housing and the future of the Trop site, hurricane resilience after Helene and Milton, and investment in Midtown, Childs Park and the skilled trades. The biggest problem he faces is money: the city expects to have about $35 million less in its next budget, largely because voters passed Amendment 3, which expands homestead property-tax exemptions. Crist had campaigned on cutting the property tax rate, and he has dropped that promise. He also opposed the $600 million stormwater, wastewater and flood-protection bond that voters approved, but he has said he will carry it out and will appoint a 12-member advisory committee for council to confirm. His first major personnel decision was to keep Anthony Holloway as police chief, a job Holloway has held since 2014.

Business

The St. Petersburg Housing Authority has proposed 47 new apartments at Clearview Park, a property on 37th Avenue North that has offered affordable family housing since 1982. Most of the units in the planned four-story building would go to seniors, and about 650 households are now on the waitlist for affordable senior housing. Four existing units would be torn down to make room for road access, and the other 18 would be renovated, which brings the site to 65 apartments. The authority is working with Trouble Creek Development and expects the new units to serve households earning up to 60% of area median income. No cost has been set and no financing has been secured yet. If funding comes through in early 2027, construction could start in late 2027 or early 2028.

San Diego, CA

Government

A group led by attorney and former mayoral candidate Genevieve Jones-Wright has launched an effort to recall Mayor Todd Gloria. Its notice of intent was published in the San Diego Daily Transcript on November 3, Election Day. The organizers, who include Terrie Best, Dr. Kathleen Harmon, Donnetta Moore and Shelby Huffaker, accuse Gloria of housing policies that favored developers, an ineffective response to homelessness and “fiscal mismanagement.” Gloria has two years left in his term and has until November 17 to file a formal response. Axios San Diego reports that Harmon also cited trash and parking fees, and said the tipping point was Gloria backing a challenger over incumbent District 4 Councilmember Henry Foster. After Gloria responds, organizers will have 120 days to collect valid signatures from 15% of the city’s registered voters to force a special election.

Read more: NBC 7 San Diego Axios San Diego ABC 10News

Science & Environment

San Diego County voters approved Measure B on November 3 with about 52% of the vote, according to unofficial results. The measure adds a half-cent sales tax that supporters expect to raise about $425 million a year. About $95.6 million a year is set aside for the Tijuana River sewage crisis, which has fouled South Bay beaches for years. The rest will go to health care, child care, wildfire preparedness and 9-1-1 emergency response. A coalition including the county’s largest labor union backed the measure, partly to offset expected federal budget cuts. Voters also narrowly passed Measure A, which gives the Board of Supervisors more power. Voice of San Diego and KPBS write that the county now owns the sewage cleanup and has to deliver on it. The supervisors are expected to adopt the tax ordinance in early December, and the tax takes effect April 1, 2027. Results are unofficial until the canvass is certified on or before December 3.
