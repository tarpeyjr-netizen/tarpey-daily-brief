Washington, DC

Crime & Justice

The District's Office of Inspector General has released a scathing report describing a "prolonged, systemic breakdown" in the internal controls meant to keep D.C. police from manipulating crime statistics. Investigators found that an independent crime-review unit was eliminated back in 2013, after which no dedicated team was left to check reports before they became official numbers, and that approved reports could later be edited with little oversight. The report stops short of concluding MPD leadership intentionally cooked the books, but it describes a culture where employees say supervisors influenced how incidents were classified. The findings have fueled a separate House Oversight investigation into the department's former chief and a parallel probe by the U.S. Attorney's office, and they land as the city's police department already faces intense scrutiny over its public safety data.

Read more: Washington Post WTOP

Government & Politics

A left-leaning bloc called the Free DC Slate swept to power on the D.C. Democratic State Committee, winning 36 of the 48 seats up for grabs in the June election. The slate ran on progressive priorities and campaigned against party leadership's lawsuit challenging Initiative 83, the 2024 ballot measure that established ranked-choice voting in the District. With results certified in mid-July, the newly seated members are now moving to call fresh leadership elections and reshape the committee's direction, a shift that could ripple into how the District's Democratic Party engages with Congress and City Hall on home-rule issues going forward.

Houston, TX

Crime & Justice

Houston is still grappling with the fallout from the fatal shooting of Lorenzo Salgado Araujo, a 35-year Houston resident and construction worker with no criminal record, who was killed by an ICE agent during a July 7 traffic stop in Magnolia Park. Amid public outcry and calls for an independent probe, Mayor John Whitmire initially said the city lacked authority to investigate because Houston police were not involved. A week later, in a notable reversal, Whitmire told City Council that HPD is in fact conducting an investigation into the shooting. The case has become a flashpoint over how much oversight the city can exert on federal immigration enforcement operating within its limits, and council members continue to press for answers on behalf of Salgado Araujo's family and the Magnolia Park community.

Read more: Texas Tribune Houston Public Media KPRC 2

Government

City Hall has an accountability fight brewing over one of its own. City Controller Chris Hollins opened an investigation in mid-July into Chris Brown, the former city controller who has served as Mayor Whitmire's senior financial adviser since January 2024, after Houston Chronicle reporting suggested Brown may be doing little actual work in the taxpayer-funded, six-figure position. The probe lands at an awkward moment for Whitmire, who has been touting a "balanced" city budget built on tightened spending, and it sets up an unusual public clash between the city's elected fiscal watchdog and the mayor's office. Hollins' office says residents deserve to know what they are getting for the salary; the mayor's office has defended the advisory role.

Dallas, TX

Government & Politics

Dallas is heading into a tense budget season as City Manager Kimberly Bizor Tolbert prepares to unveil her recommended FY 2026-27 and FY 2027-28 biennial budget to the City Council on August 11, kicking off a run of public town halls through late August ahead of a final council vote in September. Updated forecasts show the roughly $2.05 billion General Fund is out of balance, squeezed by slower revenue growth and rising costs, even as the full city budget is expected to total around $5.39 billion. Mayor Eric Johnson has staked out a clear position ahead of the presentation, pushing colleagues to protect recent public safety gains, trim City Hall spending, and expand non-tax revenue while still delivering another property tax rate cut. How the council squares those competing demands will shape city services and the tax bill for homeowners well into next year.

Crime & Justice

The City of Dallas has rolled out a new Crime Analytics Dashboard, a joint effort between the Office of Data Analytics and Business Intelligence and the Dallas Police Department meant to make crime and response-time data easier for residents and city leaders to track. The tool spans four pages covering crime overview, victim demographics, timing patterns, and DPD response times, refreshing daily with two years of historical data. The launch comes as violent crime in Dallas continues an overall decline, though a Child Poverty Action Lab analysis found a small number of areas still account for a disproportionate share of the city's violence in the first half of 2026, underscoring why officials are betting on more granular, public-facing data to sustain the trend.

Chicago, IL

Government & Politics

Mayor Brandon Johnson is heading into the second half of 2026 confronting a fresh $130 million budget shortfall after revenue the City Council had counted on for its 2026 spending plan failed to materialize. Johnson has pinned the gap on aldermen who rejected his push for new taxes on large corporations, arguing they sided with businesses over residents. The shortfall complicates an already tense budget picture heading into next year and comes as Illinois Secretary of State Alexi Giannoulias has entered the race for mayor, adding political pressure ahead of February's ballot. Even so, Johnson has been touting record tourism and a drop in crime as evidence his administration's approach is working, while declining to say whether he will seek reelection himself.

Crime & Justice

Federal prosecutors have charged twelve people following a nearly two-year investigation into a large-scale open-air fentanyl and heroin market that operated "essentially dawn till dusk every day" on Chicago's West Side. Investigators say the operation moved more than 16 pounds of fentanyl and heroin along with smaller quantities of crack cocaine, much of it purchased through undercover buys. One alleged operator, Barry "Big Barry" Spires, was arrested after wiretaps indicated he was heading out to pick up a fresh heroin supply. The case underscores the scale of the opioid trade still running through West Side neighborhoods even as the city touts an overall drop in violent crime, and it lands just as Chicago ramps up security for a busy summer weekend that includes Lollapalooza and major sporting events.

Los Angeles, CA

Economics & Business

More than 40 California state lawmakers are warning that a provision buried in this year's state budget could unravel the film and television tax incentive program credited with pulling production back to Hollywood. The provision, part of Senate Bill 122, caps the tax credit any single company can claim in a year at $5 million, effectively cutting the value of credits for large productions that had qualified for as much as $35 million. Assemblyman Rick Chavez Zbur said many members who voted for the budget didn't realize the cap was in it, calling it a reversal of progress made just last year, when the expanded incentive program drove $6.6 billion in production spending and nearly 35,000 cast and crew jobs statewide. Studios and unions are now pushing lawmakers to fix the cap before year's end so productions don't lose credits they were counting on.

Read more: CBS Los Angeles Variety Los Angeles Magazine

Science & Environment

More than a month after an eight-day fire tore through a Lineage Logistics cold-storage warehouse in Boyle Heights, neighbors are still contending with the stench of roughly 30 million pounds of decomposing food left inside the ruined facility. The South Coast Air Quality Management District has been pushing the company to adopt stronger odor-control measures as cleanup crews work through the rotting contents, which residents say have drawn rats and flies in the summer heat. The city has set an August 14 deadline for Lineage to finish clearing the trash and food waste, and air quality violations have piled up in the meantime. The episode has become a flashpoint for residents near the port-adjacent industrial corridor in Vernon and Boyle Heights, who are pressing regulators for stricter oversight of the cleanup.

New York, NY

Government & Business

Mayor Zohran Mamdani unveiled details this week for the city's first government-owned grocery stores, promising a 30% discount on core staples — including all fresh produce, meat, seafood, dairy and pantry basics — that will apply equally to shoppers at every income level rather than fluctuating like private retail pricing. The city plans to open five municipal stores by 2029, with an additional location announced for Hunts Point in the Bronx targeting a 2027 opening. Officials project the discounts could cut the average New Yorker's grocery bill by roughly 15%, or about $1,000 a year, framing the plan as a response to a citywide affordability crisis. The proposal has drawn sharp pushback from bodega and independent grocery owners, who argue that a subsidized municipal competitor undercutting retail prices could put small shops out of business. Economists remain divided on whether the untested model can be sustained at scale without significant ongoing public subsidy.

Read more: NYC Mayor's Office announcement NY1 coverage Forbes analysis

Crime & Justice

New York lawmakers are pressing the city and state to compensate thousands of residents whose SNAP food benefits have been drained through "skimming," a form of electronic theft in which thieves attach hidden devices to card readers to harvest EBT account information. The push follows reporting by THE CITY documenting the scale of the problem, prompting officials to publicly demand a reimbursement fund and a shift to more secure chip-enabled benefit cards. New Yorkers filed more than 150,000 stolen-benefits claims during the period when federal reimbursement was available — roughly one in five claims nationwide — before Congress let that program lapse in December 2024. Governor Hochul has so far resisted calls for a state-funded compensation program, telling affected residents to file police reports instead, while a group of victims has filed a lawsuit against the state over its outdated card technology.

Jacksonville, FL

Government & Politics

On July 20, Mayor Donna Deegan delivered her final budget address before the 2027 mayoral election, unveiling a $2.026 billion general fund plan that holds the property tax millage rate steady while growing overall spending by 1.1%, or roughly $22.6 million, driven largely by raises for police and fire personnel. The proposal boosts road resurfacing by $13.2 million to nearly $36 million and sets aside $25 million to replace aging septic tanks in historically underserved neighborhoods, along with funding for waterway bulkheads and sidewalk repairs. Deegan is now taking the plan on the road with eight town hall meetings scheduled across the city before Council votes. New Council President Nick Howland, a Republican, stood alongside Deegan at the address but told reporters afterward to expect a "respectful" but "very Republican versus Democrat" debate over spending priorities in the weeks ahead.

Science & Environment

Helicopter crews began stringing new high-voltage cable across the St. Johns River in late July as part of the $117 million Fulton Cut project, a joint effort by JEA, JAXPORT and the City of Jacksonville to raise transmission lines between Blount Island and Reed Island from 175 feet to 225 feet above high tide. The added clearance is meant to let larger container ships reach JAXPORT's Blount Island Marine Terminal, a key piece of the port's push to compete for bigger vessel traffic. Lineworkers are using aerial operations to install the new wiring ahead of a September start on connecting the lines to taller towers, with the full project expected to wrap by the end of 2026.

Columbus, OH

Government & Economics

Columbus is scrapping its long-planned new Franklin County Municipal Courthouse, with Mayor Andrew Ginther announcing the city will renovate the existing courthouse instead of building new. Citing rising construction costs and strained economic conditions, Ginther said abandoning the new-build project — once estimated near $200 million back in 2018 — frees up money he wants redirected toward neighborhood investments, which he called his "first priority." The reversal caps years of planning for a replacement courthouse and reflects a tighter fiscal posture at City Hall heading into the second half of 2026. Officials are framing the pivot as pragmatic cost management rather than a retreat from justice-system infrastructure needs.

Read more: ABC6: Courthouse Plan Scrapped NBC4: Budget Breakdown

Crime & Justice

Columbus has surpassed 50 homicides in 2026, a grim milestone that arrived after July became the deadliest month of the year with 14 killings, according to police data. The pace marks a sharp reversal from earlier in the year, when WOSU reported homicides trending lower than in previous years at the halfway point of 2026; the toll now matches or exceeds last year's pace over the same period. In response to renewed safety concerns, the city installed new motion-sensing "smart lights" along a walking path and parking lot at Fairwood Park and hosted a community cookout on July 14 to mark the upgrade. Neighbors welcomed the change, with some noting lighting in the area hadn't worked properly in decades.
