Phoenix-Mesa, AZ

Crime & Justice

The family of Christian Diaz, who was shot and killed by a Phoenix police officer in January, has filed a wrongful death lawsuit against Officer Jason Valenzuela and the City of Phoenix, and is demanding a jury trial. According to court records, Valenzuela shot Diaz in the head while Diaz was holding down a home-invasion suspect police had been called to arrest, and the suit alleges the officer fired within one second of issuing a command. The Maricopa County Attorney declined to charge Valenzuela and publicly called Diaz a hero for defending his family, and the police chief has apologized to the family. The suit follows a $25 million notice of claim filed a month after the shooting, and it will keep pressure on the city over use-of-force training and accountability.

Read more: KJZZ

Government & Politics

Phoenix is preparing to launch a city-run, on-demand rideshare pilot called PHX Link, with flat $2 rides in three parts of the city. Service zones cover parts of South Phoenix, North Phoenix and West Phoenix, and riders will book by app or phone and meet drivers at virtual stops. The three-year pilot is funded mostly by grants plus money from the Transportation 2050 plan, and it is meant to fill gaps in areas where fixed bus routes are thin. The city plans informational pop-up meetings October 20 to 22, and the program is slated to begin later this month.

Philadelphia, PA

Education & Government

The School District of Philadelphia said this week that it found 720 students were “inappropriately dropped” from school rosters because they lacked supporting documentation. The announcement came two days after an Inquirer report in which principals said students who had left or moved were added back to rolls before the October 1 enrollment reporting deadline to the Pennsylvania Department of Education, a count that feeds state funding. Superintendent Tony Watlington said the district takes enrollment accuracy seriously, and an attorney at the Public Interest Law Center told PhillyVoice the 720-student gap would likely have little short-term effect on state aid, which is based on multi-year averages. The district says its enrollment office will work with principals to prevent withdrawal errors; the district received about $2.5 billion from the state last year and served 198,405 students.

Read more: PhillyVoice – roster story

Justice & Higher Education

A federal appeals court has vacated a March order that required the University of Pennsylvania to give the EEOC names and contact details for Jewish studies faculty and for students and staff tied to Jewish campus groups. The EEOC had sought the information since July 2025 for an antisemitism probe and sued after Penn did not comply; five campus organizations and the ACLU of Pennsylvania intervened to block it. Because the Trump administration agreed to drop the suit while the appeal was pending, the ruling changes little for Penn in practice, but the court sent the case back with instructions to dismiss it, which means the district court’s earlier opinion can no longer serve as precedent.

Miami-Fort Lauderdale, FL

Government & Business

Miami-Dade commissioners are being asked this week to approve up to $650 million in Housing Finance Authority bonds to fund below-market mortgages for buyers earning no more than 115% of area median income. Neither the county nor the authority is responsible for repaying the bonds, and the Florida Housing Finance Corporation sets the authority’s annual bonding capacity. The proposal lands as U.S. mortgage rates jumped to 7.28% last week, their largest increase in four years, which sharpens the affordability squeeze in one of the country’s priciest housing markets. Investors, meanwhile, are enthusiastic about county paper: PortMiami bonds of up to $700 million drew more than $5.5 billion in orders.

The long-troubled Shops at Sunset Place are nearing demolition for housing after Miami-Dade approved a special district to help finance infrastructure for the redevelopment. The tourism economy is also running hot: CoStar data shows Miami-Dade’s average daily hotel rate rose 19.8% to $177.65 for the week ended Sept. 26, the largest increase in the nation.

Atlanta, GA

Government

The Atlanta City Council voted unanimously in late September to back light rail along the entire 22-mile Beltline, a project estimated at $3.5 billion that would link 45 neighborhoods. The resolution, presented by Councilmember Kelsea Bond, directs Mayor Andre Dickens, MARTA and Beltline Inc. to return with a financial plan, with a report due by January 26, 2027. Councilmember Byron Amos added provisions meant to protect Westside residents. Supporters call transit an original promise of the Beltline vision, while critics such as Better Atlanta Transit warn the money could be wasted on an extension of the streetcar serving mainly upscale neighborhoods. Officials have not yet said how the $3.5 billion would be raised, so the coming four months will determine whether the vote becomes a funded plan.

Read more: GPB FOX 5 Atlanta

Crime & Justice

The Georgia Bureau of Investigation is investigating the death of a woman at Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International Airport on October 1, at the request of the Atlanta Police Department. Around 5 a.m., a MARTA Police detective encountered a visibly distressed woman who asked for an ambulance and access to a restroom. The detective found her with multiple apparent self-inflicted stab wounds, and she was pronounced dead at the scene. An autopsy will be performed at the Clayton County Medical Examiner's Office, and the investigation remains active. The GBI is asking anyone with information to contact its Atlanta Regional Investigative Office at 770-388-5019 or the GBI Tipline at 800-597-TIPS.

Washington, DC

Government

A federal jury could not reach a verdict in the bribery trial of Ward 8 Councilmember Trayon White Sr., and a judge declared a mistrial on September 28 after three days of deliberations. Prosecutors say hidden-camera footage shows White accepting about $35,000 in cash from an FBI informant in exchange for pushing government contracts, and jurors reportedly described their positions as cemented. U.S. Attorney Jeanine Pirro has vowed to retry the case before a new jury. The Council voted 12-0 to expel White after his 2024 indictment, but Ward 8 voters returned him to office in a special election. Council Chairman Phil Mendelson signaled there would be no immediate action, so the District heads toward the November 3 election with an unresolved question over one of its eight ward seats.

Houston, TX

Government

Houston City Council has had a turbulent September, and the fallout is still unfolding. Controller Chris Hollins opened a probe into Mayor John Whitmire's senior financial adviser Chris Brown after a June report found Brown had swiped his badge only 13 times since starting in January, and Hollins refused a city attorney's demand that he sign a nondisclosure agreement before receiving documents. At the September 30 council meeting, Brown defended his work and acknowledged working about 15 hours a week for a $127,000 salary. Earlier, on September 2, council approved a $50 million payout over three years to The Woodlands to head off future annexation, a deal Hollins says costs the city roughly $130 million in recurring revenue over 30 years. The tension between the mayor's office and the city's chief financial watchdog now shapes how council debates spending and transparency.

Read more: Houstonia: Council September 2026 Houston Public Media: City Council

Science & Environment

Restaurants across Houston, Clear Lake and Galveston are helping the Galveston Bay Foundation rebuild oyster habitat, according to Houston Public Media. The foundation collects used shells from roughly 40 restaurants, cures them for about six months and returns them to the bay as a home for baby oysters. Its crews pick up around 200 tons of shell a year, and the program has recovered more than 980 tons since 2011. The effort comes amid growing need for oyster restoration along the Texas coast, where regulators closed a Galveston Bay harvest area earlier this year.

Dallas, TX

Government

Downtown Dallas is trying to chart a future after AT&T's planned exit, and the fate of the city's own headquarters is part of that debate. City staff estimated that renovating City Hall could cost at least $152 million and as much as $345 million, driven by garage repairs, water infiltration and HVAC upgrades. The council adopted a resolution directing the city manager to explore relocation and redevelopment options, while also weighing repair scenarios. Developer Ray Washburne has pitched the site as a possible Mavericks arena and entertainment district, an idea that has split council members, some of whom worry the land would effectively be given away. How the city resolves the question will help shape whether the core of downtown rebounds after losing one of its largest employers.

Read more: WFAA: downtown in limbo Texas Monthly Council rift

Business

In the northern suburbs, Plano voters will decide in a Nov. 3 special election whether to approve a package of venue taxes to help pay for a proposed Dallas Stars arena at the site of The Shops at Willow Bend. The options include a hotel occupancy tax increase of up to 2%, a rental car tax of up to 5%, event parking fees and an admissions tax. City leaders estimate those taxes plus revenue from the Willow Bend tax increment zone could contribute up to $700 million to the project. Competing campaigns have formed, with one group backing the arena and another, Stop the Stars Arena, citing traffic, noise and suburban character. The outcome will signal how much appetite the region has for publicly supported entertainment districts.

Chicago, IL

Crime & Justice

Mayor Brandon Johnson's administration has proposed a $260.8 million settlement covering 17 men who were exonerated after being wrongfully convicted in cases tied to former police detective Reynaldo Guevara. The men spent between 18 and 30 years in prison, more than 383 years combined, after being coerced into confessions. If the City Council approves it, the deal could become the largest single police misconduct settlement in U.S. history, with payments spread over two years starting in 2027. Johnson's office argues that resolving the cases together will save taxpayers an estimated $300 million to $400 million compared with the risk of going to trial, while critics are already objecting to the size of the payout. The city's announcement frames the deal as closing one of the most costly chapters of the Chicago Police Department's history, and it now heads to the Finance Committee and the full Council.

Read more: Chicago Sun-Times CBS Chicago ABC7 Chicago

Government & Politics

Early voting for the November 3 general election opened Thursday at Chicago's new downtown supersite at 137 South State Street, which replaces the longtime Clark and Lake location. The space, a former CVS on the ground floor of a cooling plant building at State and Adams, can handle 80 voters at a time and offers an audio ballot and materials in 12 languages. It is open Monday through Friday from 9 a.m. to 6 p.m., Saturday until 5 p.m. and Sunday from 10 a.m. to 4 p.m., with weekday hours stretching to 7 p.m. from October 26. Early-vote sites open in all 50 wards starting October 19, which the Board of Election Commissioners hopes will ease Election Day crowds. Details are on the Board of Elections site.
