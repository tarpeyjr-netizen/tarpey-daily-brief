Tampa-St. Petersburg, FL

Government

St. Petersburg Mayor-elect Charlie Crist has laid out the "Five Guiding Lights" for his transition, labeled Open, Steady, Grow, Ready and Build. They cover an Office of Open Government on day one, protecting core services, housing and the future of the Trop site, hurricane resilience after Helene and Milton, and investment in Midtown, Childs Park and the skilled trades. The biggest problem he faces is money: the city expects to have about $35 million less in its next budget, largely because voters passed Amendment 3, which expands homestead property-tax exemptions. Crist had campaigned on cutting the property tax rate, and he has dropped that promise. He also opposed the $600 million stormwater, wastewater and flood-protection bond that voters approved, but he has said he will carry it out and will appoint a 12-member advisory committee for council to confirm. His first major personnel decision was to keep Anthony Holloway as police chief, a job Holloway has held since 2014.

Business

The St. Petersburg Housing Authority has proposed 47 new apartments at Clearview Park, a property on 37th Avenue North that has offered affordable family housing since 1982. Most of the units in the planned four-story building would go to seniors, and about 650 households are now on the waitlist for affordable senior housing. Four existing units would be torn down to make room for road access, and the other 18 would be renovated, which brings the site to 65 apartments. The authority is working with Trouble Creek Development and expects the new units to serve households earning up to 60% of area median income. No cost has been set and no financing has been secured yet. If funding comes through in early 2027, construction could start in late 2027 or early 2028.

San Diego, CA

Government

A group led by attorney and former mayoral candidate Genevieve Jones-Wright has launched an effort to recall Mayor Todd Gloria. Its notice of intent was published in the San Diego Daily Transcript on November 3, Election Day. The organizers, who include Terrie Best, Dr. Kathleen Harmon, Donnetta Moore and Shelby Huffaker, accuse Gloria of housing policies that favored developers, an ineffective response to homelessness and “fiscal mismanagement.” Gloria has two years left in his term and has until November 17 to file a formal response. Axios San Diego reports that Harmon also cited trash and parking fees, and said the tipping point was Gloria backing a challenger over incumbent District 4 Councilmember Henry Foster. After Gloria responds, organizers will have 120 days to collect valid signatures from 15% of the city’s registered voters to force a special election.

Science & Environment

San Diego County voters approved Measure B on November 3 with about 52% of the vote, according to unofficial results. The measure adds a half-cent sales tax that supporters expect to raise about $425 million a year. About $95.6 million a year is set aside for the Tijuana River sewage crisis, which has fouled South Bay beaches for years. The rest will go to health care, child care, wildfire preparedness and 9-1-1 emergency response. A coalition including the county’s largest labor union backed the measure, partly to offset expected federal budget cuts. Voters also narrowly passed Measure A, which gives the Board of Supervisors more power. Voice of San Diego and KPBS write that the county now owns the sewage cleanup and has to deliver on it. The supervisors are expected to adopt the tax ordinance in early December, and the tax takes effect April 1, 2027. Results are unofficial until the canvass is certified on or before December 3.

Minneapolis-St. Paul, MN

Government

On November 12, the Minneapolis City Council rejected Mayor Jacob Frey’s nomination of interim chief Bill Peterson to lead the police department permanently, with eight members voting no. Peterson has been with the department since he was 21. He became interim chief after Brian O’Hara resigned in May amid allegations that he interfered with an internal investigation, and Frey nominated him for the permanent job in June. Opponents cited the department’s history of biased policing against Black and Native American residents. Council Member LaTrisha Vetaw said she preferred north side precinct commander Charlie Adams, while Elizabeth Shaffer backed Peterson as the most experienced candidate. The vote is the latest clash between the council and Frey over public safety leadership. Earlier this year the council declined to reappoint the community safety commissioner, and last month it voted to eliminate the Office of Community Safety. All of this comes as the department works through its 2023 reform agreement with the state Department of Human Rights and struggles with staffing and overtime costs.

Business

The same night, the council voted 7–6 to approve permanent data center rules before the city’s temporary moratorium expires on November 21. New data centers would be limited to existing buildings in two downtown zoning districts. A provision added on the day of the vote would also require the council to approve each project individually. Council Member Aurin Chowdhury called case-by-case approval “the next best thing” to a ban. Elizabeth Shaffer and Jamal Osman opposed the measure, and Shaffer argued that a few small facilities in vacant downtown buildings could add tax revenue and jobs. Council members said Frey opposes the case-by-case requirement and may veto the ordinance, and seven votes would not be enough to override a veto. The council plans to reconvene on November 20. The debate has been heated: a developer abandoned plans for the former Star Tribune printing plant in the North Loop after public opposition, and activists rallied outside City Hall on November 10.

Detroit, MI

Government

On November 9, Mayor Mary Sheffield and Detroit Land Bank Authority director Tammy Daniels stood outside a Dexter-Linwood home and announced a package of land bank reforms meant to answer years of resident complaints about the agency. The city will release its holds on 7,500 land bank properties so they can be sold, and a new tracking tool will show the status of all 56,000 DLBA parcels, including which ones are held and why. Buyers will be vetted for financial means and offered free property tours. Two new $1.5 million programs will help with renovations and help occupants complete the Occupied Buy Back path to ownership. The DLBA will also open a field office at the Butzel Family Recreation Center, adopt a 72-hour response standard and make nuisance abatement referral-only. It is committing $6 million to restore 75 homes as temporary supportive housing with the Homeless Action Network of Detroit, which adds more than 170 beds.

Business

Bedrock and General Motors' $2.2 billion plan to remake the Renaissance Center reached City Council's Planning and Economic Development Committee, which held a public hearing on November 12. The plan would demolish two of the RenCen's five towers. The developers are seeking $548 million in tax breaks and incentives, and they say the project cannot go forward without them. A Neighborhood Advisory Council has already approved what has been called the largest community benefits package ever, and the agreement now goes to the full council. Mayor Sheffield's administration supports the project. Many residents who have spoken oppose it, citing worries about privatizing the riverfront and about roughly a decade of construction, and some have asked council to slow down and release a confidential Law Department memo on revenue sharing.

San Francisco-Oakland, CA

Crime & Justice

The federal corruption trial of former Oakland Mayor Sheng Thao, now in its fourth week, has turned to the testimony of the government's key witness, Mario Juarez, a onetime business partner of the Duong family. New evidence shows Juarez secretly lobbied for ABC Security's City Hall contract without registering as a lobbyist, after the firm ranked last of six bidders in a 2022 renewal. City records show that Allied Universal's top-scoring proposal was pulled weeks after Thao took office, and the council soon received a proposal to extend ABC's contract by up to $4 million. Prosecutors have not accused ABC or its owner of wrongdoing. Meanwhile, defense lawyers for Thao, her former partner Andre Jones and David and Andy Duong are pressing Juarez to name the politicians who allegedly got bags of cash from the Duongs after their 2014 waste contract.

Crime & Justice

The San Francisco Sheriff's Office has admitted that an "administrative breakdown" led it to record confidential jail calls between detainees and their lawyers and hand them to prosecutors over two years. The public defender's office says at least 20 of its calls were recorded and shared, and at least 38 phone numbers tied to the office were never properly added to the do-not-record list. Deputy Public Defender Kwixuan Maloof says three of the recorded calls from his number involved homicide cases and covered legal strategy, and that a prosecutor "cannot unhear" them. Since Aug. 27, the sheriff has stopped routinely sharing recordings, and prosecutors must now subpoena any calls they want. Intentionally recording attorney-client conversations is a felony in California, but no charges or discipline have been reported.

Seattle, WA

Government

Progressive advocate Nilu Jenks, former leader of the nonprofit FairVote Washington, won the District 5 special election for Seattle City Council by a wide margin over educator Julie Kang, holding a 17-point lead on election night that grew in later ballot drops. The seat opened when Cathy Moore resigned, and Jenks will take office once results are certified later this month instead of in January. She replaces interim Councilmember Debora Juarez, a frequent critic of Mayor Katie Wilson, so the council's progressive bloc grows to four members and Wilson's agenda should have an easier path. Jenks has said she will push for safer designs on Aurora Avenue N and Lake City Way, a larger civilian-run CARE Department, and on-bill financing for heat pumps. The same election saw the Seattle Transit Measure renewal headed to victory, and Jenks must run again next year when all seven district seats are on the ballot.

Crime

On Nov. 10, Mayor Katie Wilson lifted her pause on new police CCTV cameras in three neighborhoods. Cameras in the Stadium District go live immediately, and installations near Garfield High School in the Central District and in the Capitol Hill nightlife district will follow on a timeline she could not yet give. The decision came a day after the release of a surveillance audit she ordered from NYU's Policing Project, which called for tighter data-retention and privacy guardrails and warned that no agency can guarantee data won't be shared, including with the federal government. Public Safety Committee Chair Bob Kettle called the move "pragmatic," while Councilmember Alexis Mercedes Rinck said she would rather fix internal issues before expanding. The Downtown Seattle Association and the Greater Seattle Business Association back the expansion, and the Community Not Cameras Coalition opposes it, noting that Wilson opposed it during her 2025 campaign.

Cleveland, OH

Crime & Justice

Mayor Justin Bibb’s administration wants to replace Cleveland’s roughly 100 Flock Safety license plate readers with Axon’s system, asking City Council to approve a one-year contract whose cost has not been disclosed. The move follows council’s refusal in May to renew Flock without competitive bids; after two hearings, members granted only a six-month extension on the condition that the city seek other proposals. The administration is also proposing a Skydio “drone as a first responder” contract and a one-year ShotSpotter extension. Opponents in the Flock No Cleveland group say a vendor swap ignores public objections to surveillance, while Safety Committee chair Mike Polensek backs the readers but wants limits on who can access the data. Complicating matters, the Community Police Commission has passed a resolution calling for police to stop using the readers on Jan. 1 until its policy review is done, and it is unclear whether the city will comply.

Government

Cuyahoga County voters approved almost $111 million in additional property tax money for social services last week, passing Issue 11 for seniors, children, homeless residents and food aid, and Issue 10 for the Board of Developmental Disabilities. County Executive Chris Ronayne, re-elected to a second term with 98% against a write-in challenger, said the levy was “first and foremost” about staving off further cuts after a $20.5 million reduction to the 2027 health and human services budget, and the county says it will move slowly on spending the new money. Two big money questions still loom: Greater Cleveland RTA is weighing its first sales tax increase in half a century, possibly on the May ballot, though Ronayne wants a community transportation plan first. Meanwhile, Gateway Economic Development Corp. is out of money for repairs at Progressive Field and Rocket Arena, the Cavaliers are seeking $37 million for upgrades, and the county, city and teams have not identified a revenue source.

Phoenix-Mesa, AZ

Government & Politics

Phoenix voters went to the polls on November 3 to fill four City Council seats, and the early count points to two incumbents winning outright and two open-seat races heading to a March 9, 2027 runoff. In District 6, Kevin Robinson leads Michael Del Prete 64.5% to 35.5%, while District 8's Kesha Hodge Washington holds about 72.5%. The open seats are District 2, where Jim Waring is termed out, and District 4, where Laura Pastor is termed out. In District 4, attorney and school board member Ed Hermes leads with roughly 40% in an eight-candidate field, with the second runoff slot still tight. In District 2, Matt Evans, Julie Read and Danny Mazza sit within about 1,100 votes of one another, and Turning Point Action endorsed all three. Results are unofficial; the council is expected to certify them on November 18, and winners take office in April 2027.

Read more: KTAR: council results KJZZ: District 2 runoff Phoenix City Clerk: election page

Economics & Business

Phoenix is preparing to end a six-year pause on water shutoffs for seriously delinquent residential accounts. ABC15 reports the restart is set for November 16 for accounts more than four months behind, covering about 9,000 accounts that owe nearly $24 million across water, wastewater, solid waste, taxes and fees. The city says the amount owed and how long an account has been delinquent will set the order, with advance notice and payment plans offered. The move, first announced in early October, comes as the city also weighs tougher conservation steps amid Colorado River shortages. Phoenix has announced resumptions before and then reversed course, so the date is worth confirming with the city.
