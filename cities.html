Nashville, TN

Government

A Thursday-night crash in which an SUV rear-ended a downtown pedal tavern, sending nearly a dozen people to the hospital, has put Nashville's pedal tavern rules under a spotlight. Metro police said the driver was distracted by his radio. The Transportation Licensing Commission oversees the vehicles through a 53-page rulebook that gives pedal taverns a single page, covering permit fees, background checks, downtown and Midtown operating zones, and rush-hour route limits, but nothing on seatbelts or other equipment to keep riders from falling off. Seven companies hold licenses for 30 pedal taverns, and the rules date to 2015, when Freddie O'Connell, now mayor, sponsored the original bill as a council member. O'Connell says the commission reviews safety concerns, but that new state tourism-development-zone legislation led the city to pause some downtown safety projects.

Read more: WSMV: pedal tavern rules

Business

Opry Mills' head of security, Richard Siegelman, told the Metro Council this week that the mall's license plate readers share data with repossession companies, which tow about three or four vehicles from the lot each week. Tennessee law bars government entities from keeping plate-reader data more than 90 days outside an investigation, but that limit does not apply to private operators such as the mall's owner, Simon Property Group. Mayor O'Connell called the technology a useful but imperfect safety tool and said private parking operators' readers have falsely flagged him, a problem he raised with the state Attorney General. An Electronic Frontier Foundation privacy expert warned that retention periods vary from days to years and that the data could be monetized. WSMV said Simon and Metro police had not answered questions about data sales and police access.

Boston, MA

Government & Politics

The Boston City Council took the unusual step this month of formally summoning MBTA General Manager Phillip Eng over the controversial Blue Hill Avenue bus lane project, a move Mayor Michelle Wu's administration has criticized. The dispute pits councilors who say the project is being pushed on neighbourhoods without enough say against an administration and transit agency that see dedicated lanes as a way to speed up some of the city's slowest bus routes. Councilors have also floated an Orange Line extension as an alternative response to the pushback. Separately, the council's recent actions include support for a conservation fund tied to ballot Question 6, a Boston Harbor coastal storm risk plan and tenant first-right-of-refusal legislation, all dated October 13.

St. Louis, MO

Crime & Justice

Federal officials closed out Operation Show Me Justice, an eight-week, ATF-led violent crime surge in St. Louis, at an October 15 press conference. The Justice Department reported 435 arrests and 166 firearms seized, along with about 81 kilograms of drugs, six machine gun conversion devices and more than $1.25 million in currency. Most of the arrests were tied to existing warrants, with 412 warrants cleared, and the operation targeted several local gangs alongside the U.S. Marshals Service and the Eastern District of Missouri U.S. Attorney's Office. The results land as the city's own numbers show homicides still being tracked closely: a police analysis dated October 9 counted 89 homicides so far this year. What happens once the surge teams leave, and whether local agencies can sustain the pace, is the open question for city leaders.

Read more: DOJ release St. Louis Post-Dispatch Fox News

Economics & Business

BJC HealthCare and Washington University Medicine said they will leave the Medicare Advantage networks of both Aetna and Anthem on January 1, 2027, after failing to reach agreements before the current contracts expire December 31. The move could affect thousands of older adults in the region; one estimate puts the combined pool of Aetna and Anthem Medicare Advantage members at roughly 85,800, though not all use BJC facilities. The Post-Dispatch reported that WashU physicians are covered under a separate contract that is also ending. Medicare open enrollment runs through the end of the year, so seniors have a window to switch plans, but the dispute puts pressure on the region's largest health system and two major insurers to return to the table. Separately, developer TerraWatt has proposed a 200-megawatt data center on the shuttered Alton Steel site across the river, adding to the metro area's data center debate.

Baltimore, MD

Crime

Mayor Brandon Scott called police command staff into a closed-door meeting on Tuesday after five people were killed and as many as ten injured in shootings in under 48 hours. The incidents included two separate Sunday shootings in West and Northwest Baltimore, one on Muth Avenue and another on Belair Road on Monday night, with police saying multiple gunmen were involved in several of them and no arrests announced for the West Baltimore BP station attack. Scott said the city “cannot have another two weeks like the two weeks we’ve been having,” and Police Commissioner Richard Worley said the department will return to basics such as vehicle stops and approaching groups on corners, targeting the small groups driving retaliatory violence. Community partners such as Safe Streets sites are working to head off further retaliation. Despite the spike, homicides stood at 101 as of Tuesday morning, down from 105 at this point in 2025, while shootings are slightly up at 253 versus 247.

Crime

A Baltimore judge declared a mistrial on Tuesday in the case of James Barnett after jurors could not reach a unanimous verdict following nearly a week of deliberations; a juror told CBS News Baltimore the panel split 6 to 6. Barnett was charged over the January 24, 2022 Stricker Street rowhome fire in which Lt. Kelsey Sadler, Lt. Paul Butrim and firefighter-EMT Kenny Lacayo died when the building collapsed, and firefighter John McMaster was injured. The judge had dismissed the murder charges the week before, leaving second-degree arson, involuntary manslaughter and reckless endangerment. Prosecutors may retry the case, and the judge told them to schedule any retrial as soon as possible, leaving the families of the three firefighters and the fire department waiting nearly five years after the tragedy for a resolution.

Charlotte, NC

Crime & Justice

The Charlotte-Mecklenburg Police Department on Monday publicly identified seven officers tied to a racist text message scandal. The messages surfaced during a State Bureau of Investigation probe into one officer's alleged misuse of the Flock surveillance network, and sources told WBTV they included a contest over who could pull over the most Black drivers. Six officers were slated for termination on Oct. 9; five have since resigned, one resigned earlier, and Jeremiah Todman remains suspended without pay pending a Civil Service Board review. Chief Estella Patterson called the situation “deeply troubling,” said her internal review of arrests, stops and body-camera footage has so far found nothing targeting Black drivers, and is seeking to revoke the officers’ state certifications. The U.S. Attorney is weighing a possible civil rights investigation, while the local NAACP is pressing for stricter hiring, training and psychological screening.

Government & Politics

Records obtained by WBTV show that out-of-state agencies searched the UNC Charlotte campus license plate camera network far more than local police did. Houston police ran searches that included the network more than 183,000 times from March through May, ranking first, while campus police ran 229 and CMPD ranked 30th. The cameras are Flock readers in a shared network of about 1,600 agencies; UNC Charlotte says officers must enter case numbers and that it has found no misuse. Appalachian State and UNC Chapel Hill have narrowed their sharing, and critics call the statewide system mass surveillance with few reporting requirements in North Carolina.

Orlando, FL

Government

Orlando’s City Council was scheduled to vote on October 12 on a construction contract for the Pulse Memorial with Gomez Construction Company, carrying a guaranteed maximum price of $12,624,837. The vote would move the long-planned memorial for the 49 people killed on June 12, 2016 from design into construction, which the city says should begin this fall and finish by the end of 2027. Philanthropic partners have committed $1.75 million, including the DeVos Family Foundation, Dr. Phillips Charities, Orlando Health, Universal Destinations & Experiences, Wilf Family Foundations and Walt Disney World Resort, building on earlier support from Orange County and the State of Florida. The design grew out of the 18-member Pulse Memorial Advisory Committee of families, survivors and community members. The city’s release was published before the vote, so the outcome should be confirmed with the council’s records.

Denver, CO

Government

Denver City Council voted 11-1 on Tuesday, Oct. 13, to approve use and lease agreements with 10 airlines at Denver International Airport, including Key Lime Air, reversing course from a December 2025 vote that rejected the company's expansion over its role in ICE deportation flights. The approval followed about 90 minutes of debate and two postponements over three weeks, and Councilmember Stacie Gilmore cast the lone no vote, arguing it undercuts the city's commitment to protecting people's rights. Supporters led by Councilmember Kevin Flynn said the agreements cover scheduled passenger service rather than ICE charters, and that rejecting them would not stop those flights while putting roughly $159 million in expected federal airport grants and subsidized service to 30 small communities at risk. A 9News investigation found Key Lime accounts for about one in five ICE flights out of Denver. The contract was not changed, but airport officials agreed to return to the council's transportation committee before year's end with an update on monitoring and transparency talks with immigrant advocates.

Read more: Colorado Politics Denverite (Dec. 2025 vote)

Economics & Business

The Regional Transportation District unveiled a proposed 2027 budget that still carries a $148 million deficit, down 39% from $242 million in the 2026 amended budget. Spending would fall to $1.34 billion from $1.5 billion, with about $20 million in service reductions or modifications that RTD has not yet specified, and a 5% staffing cut that includes 57 vacant transit police and emergency management positions. Revenue is projected at $1.19 billion, with sales and use tax expected to rise 4.7%. The agency blames ridership still below pre-pandemic levels, the end of federal COVID relief and rising operating costs, and its chief financial officer called the remaining gap a significant structural deficit that could draw down reserves and increase future borrowing. The board is expected to vote on the budget in December.

Tampa-St. Petersburg, FL

Government

The Tampa City Council on Thursday kept alive the city's financing plan for the new Rays ballpark, but only after a procedural scramble. Members first rejected the measure on a 3-3 tie because Councilman Bill Carlson was out sick; once he was allowed to join virtually, the council approved the potential use of bonds for the city's $80 million contribution by a 4-3 vote, with Councilwoman Lynn Hurtak opposed. The money would be paid to the Rays over four years toward roads, sidewalks, parking and other public infrastructure at the site on Hillsborough College's campus on the city's west side. The vote does not by itself commit Tampa to borrowing, and Axios Tampa Bay reported that a judge's approval and a further council vote would still be required before any bonds are issued. The council has set a special meeting for Oct. 29 to take up a community benefits agreement, the project still needs a Hillsborough County Commission vote, and the Rays are aiming for Opening Day 2029.

Read more: FOX 13: Council approves bond Axios Tampa Bay: Bond preview

Business

Mosaic, the Tampa Bay-area fertilizer giant, said it will lay off about 199 workers as it idles its South Fort Meade operations in Polk County starting Dec. 8, with employees receiving 60 days' notice under federal WARN Act rules. The company blames a global sulfur shortage and sharply higher sulfur costs; sulfur is a key input for phosphate fertilizer, and a USF supply-chain professor noted that a large share of the world's sulfur normally moves through the Strait of Hormuz. A core crew will remain for maintenance, safety and environmental compliance, and Mosaic expects the shutdown to last more than six months. The hourly workers are represented by UFCW Local 814C, and it is not yet clear how many will use bumping rights to move into other jobs. For Polk County's phosphate economy, the idling is a significant hit and a reminder of how global supply disruptions reach local payrolls.
