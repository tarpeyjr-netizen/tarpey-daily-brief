Tampa–St. Petersburg, FL

Government & Politics

St. Petersburg voters go to the polls tomorrow in a six-way mayoral primary that has become the most closely watched municipal race on Florida's Gulf Coast. Incumbent Ken Welch, elected in 2021 as the city's first Black mayor, is defending his seat against a field that includes former governor and congressman Charlie Crist, sitting City Council member Brandi Gabbard, former fire chief Jim Large, neighborhood association president Kevin Batdorf, and corrections administrator Maria Scruggs. Polling ahead of the vote has shown Crist with a lead over Welch, though a large undecided bloc leaves the outcome unsettled. Because no candidate is expected to clear 50 percent, both frontrunners have publicly prepared for a November 3 runoff. The race has turned on property taxes, storm recovery and the pace of downtown redevelopment — issues that the candidates sparred over at a recent public forum — and the result will shape how the city handles its next round of infrastructure and waterfront decisions.

Economics & Business

Downtown Tampa is absorbing a wave of new small businesses as business applications hit a record high, with Florida ranking third nationally both in applications per capita and in the share of new firms likely to hire multiple employees. Several ventures that began as pop-ups and kiosks at Sparkman Wharf have graduated into permanent storefronts, a pattern the Tampa Downtown Partnership points to as evidence that the district's post-pandemic recovery is now producing durable tenants rather than churn. Partnership CEO Kenyetta Hairston-Bridges has framed the moment as the start of downtown's next growth phase, with ground-floor retail filling in alongside the residential towers delivered over the past few years. The trend is running in parallel with continued suburban expansion — developers are adding housing and a shopping center to the West Port master-planned community and Manatee County Habitat for Humanity is using state funds to build infrastructure for a planned Palmetto townhouse project. Together the activity suggests the region's growth is broadening beyond the large-scale projects that dominated headlines earlier in the decade.

Detroit, Michigan

Government & Public Safety

Eight months into her tenure as Detroit's first woman mayor, Mary Sheffield is being judged on an unconventional public-safety experiment: instead of flooding downtown with officers to break up the "teen takeovers" that snarled the central business district this spring, her administration threw parties. The Detroit News reported this week that the city spent more than $22,000 on two summer events designed to give young people somewhere to go, and that the mayor, the police chief and even a police commissioner who had criticized her earlier handling of the crowds now describe the money as well spent. The approach dovetails with the city's longstanding youth-employment push: Sheffield and City Council launched the 12th annual Grow Detroit's Young Talent program this summer, placing roughly 8,000 residents ages 14 to 24 in paid jobs that wrap up in mid-August. Whether the strategy holds once GDYT ends and school returns is the open question, and it arrives as the council settles into new leadership with James Tate as president and Coleman Young II as president pro tem. For a mayor who campaigned on community investment rather than enforcement, the fall will be the first real stress test.

Crime & Justice

Detroit is now prosecuting its own misdemeanor cases for the first time in the city's history, a structural shift that took effect July 1 and is only beginning to show results. Under an ordinance the City Council passed 8-1 last fall, the city law department took over a slate of offenses from the Wayne County Prosecutor's Office, including larceny, embezzlement, domestic violence and carrying a firearm while intoxicated. The handoff came with roughly $700,000 in added budget to hire four attorneys and two paralegals, and it is meant to let County Prosecutor Kym Worthy's office concentrate on violent felonies while giving Detroit direct control over diversion, deferral and expungement options for lower-level cases. Notably, then-Council President Sheffield cast the lone dissenting vote, arguing the city was taking on cost and risk without a guarantee of better outcomes — a position she now has to administer as mayor. Backers, including county officials who supported the transfer, say the payoff should be faster case resolution for Detroiters stuck in a backlogged system.

Seattle, WA

Government & Politics

Seattle's city government went through a leadership shake-up in early August after Mayor Katie Wilson accepted the resignation of Police Chief Shon Barnes, just days after a mass shooting disrupted the Bite of Seattle festival at Seattle Center. Barnes, roughly a year into the job, was out of town at a law-enforcement conference in Dallas when the shooting occurred, and several councilmembers said the mayor's office pushed him out over that absence and the department's slow release of information afterward. Wilson has since named Deputy Chief Andre Sayles as interim chief, and Sayles says his early priority is tightening communication between SPD and City Hall. The abrupt departure drew pushback from council members who called the process "unacceptable," even as the mayor defended it as a necessary response to a department under strain just months into her first term.

Crime & Justice

New citywide data shows shootings down more than 20% and homicides down over 30% in the first half of 2026 compared with a year earlier, a trend city officials point to as evidence that public safety investments are paying off. But the citywide gains obscure sharp neighborhood-level disparities, with violent and property crime still climbing in several pockets of the city even as it falls elsewhere. The uneven picture has become a flashpoint at City Hall over how patrol resources should be distributed, a debate now playing out as SPD adjusts to interim leadership under Chief Andre Sayles. Recent high-profile arrests — including a Belltown grocery-store robbery in which a suspect shot at a delivery driver, and a downtown attack police are investigating as a hate crime — have kept public safety near the top of the local news cycle even as the broader numbers trend down.

Cleveland, OH

Government & Policy

Cleveland City Council voted 14-to-1 to approve a temporary three-month pause on new standalone data center projects, scaling back an initial one-year moratorium proposed by Councilman Charles Slife. The freeze, in effect through mid-October, applies only to ground-up construction and expansions of standalone data center buildings, not to server farms folded into multi-tenant or mixed-use developments. City leaders say the pause is meant to buy time to write zoning guardrails for a use the city's nearly century-old code never anticipated, as data center proposals have surged region-wide. Council may extend the pause for up to three more months if the rulemaking isn't finished by then.

Infrastructure & Environment

The Public Utilities Commission of Ohio issued Cleveland Electric Illuminating Company a notice of probable noncompliance following a string of power outages that hit neighboring Lakewood last month. Regulators identified eight major FirstEnergy equipment failures on the same circuit and substation, contributing to more than 18,000 customer outages over a two-week span; PUCO's call center fielded over 800 complaints as Lakewood officials sought help directly. Investigators found the utility failed both to invest in needed equipment upgrades and to maintain an adequate customer-communication plan, marking the second consecutive summer of prolonged Lakewood outages. The finding could lead to penalties or mandated fixes for CEI, whose service territory covers much of Greater Cleveland.
