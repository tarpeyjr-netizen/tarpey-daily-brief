Los Angeles, CA

Government

Los Angeles votes tomorrow in a mayoral runoff that has tightened to a dead heat. A UC Berkeley Institute of Governmental Studies poll co-sponsored by the Los Angeles Times, fielded among 2,197 likely city voters, found incumbent Mayor Karen Bass and Councilmember Nithya Raman each at 40 percent, with 19 percent undecided. In September the same pollster had Raman ahead 39 to 28, so Bass has recovered ground, though 65 percent of voters still view her unfavorably. Raman, a former Bass supporter, edged out reality-TV figure Spencer Pratt for the second runoff slot after a slow mail-ballot count in the June primary. Pratt voters now look like the swing bloc, with 41 percent leaning Bass, 14 percent Raman and 45 percent undecided. Voters named homeless encampments as their top priority (43 percent), ahead of public safety and affordable housing.

Crime & Justice

Los Angeles County's election office has pushed back on a federal list of roughly 20,000 California voters that the Justice Department calls “likely” noncitizens. Only 7,035 of the names had Los Angeles County addresses, and some were duplicates, according to Registrar Dean Logan. In a review of 200 records, county officials called the list “unreliable”: among records that reported a birthplace, 38.9 percent said they were born in the United States, and about 70 percent had no voting history. The Justice Department, which has acknowledged some names may be citizens, nonetheless charged 28 people from the list, all green-card holders, and is asking California to segregate the associated ballots. The state called the request unfeasible so close to the election and a “reckless political stunt,” leaving the county at the center of a legal fight on the eve of the vote.

Government

Government

With Election Day days away, Republican Beaver County Sheriff Tony Guy is trying to unseat Democratic Rep. Crime

A judge ruled this week that a 15-year-old will stand trial on three counts of attempted criminal homicide in the shooting that wounded three teens outside Cupples Stadium on the South Side after a Westinghouse versus University Prep football game. After hearing from nearly 10 prosecution witnesses and viewing surveillance video of a fight shortly before the shots, the Pittsburgh Municipal Court judge let the charges stand. Police identified the teen partly through testimony from a school counselor and principal. His attorney argues the victims' injuries were to the lower extremities and that there was no specific intent to kill, and says he will challenge the identification once discovery arrives. The teen remains in Allegheny County Jail without bail, and the case highlights ongoing concern about violence around high school sporting events.

Austin, TX

Economics & Business

A Southeast Austin grocer is warning that the city's light rail plans could put it out of business. Austin Transit Partnership, the agency building the largest piece of Project Connect, may use eminent domain to take part of the Riverside property of JD's Supermarket near East Riverside and Montopolis drives. The current design would leave the building standing but take the land holding the gas pumps, which store representatives say bring in about 30% of sales and whose loss could force closure within one or two years. The store has employed about 90 people since opening in 2023 and serves roughly 2,250 EBT customers a month. ATP says the design does not displace the store and is not final, and JD's is scouting nearby sites that could keep the fuel business alive, but no timeline for any condemnation or final route decision has been set.

Read more: Austin Chronicle

Government & Politics

Austin City Council is facing questions about how it approved about $4.1 million in extensions for 14 nonprofit social service contracts on Sept. 24 without the full picture. Records obtained by the Statesman show a staff memo flagging compliance concerns at five of the organizations and a performance shortfall at a sixth reached council nine minutes after the vote. Council Member Marc Duchen called it an unacceptable administrative mistake and is worried about the roughly $101 million the city plans to spend on social services amid budget shortfalls. A February presentation found Austin spends about 85% of its General Fund on social services, far above similar cities. The council is considering a directive for geographic data in future reporting, and the city expects a broader strategic reset of its social services portfolio in December.

Richmond, VA

Government

Mayor Danny Avula has unveiled a draft immigration "Trust Policy" that sets rules for how city government deals with federal immigration enforcement, following a City Council vote in August directing the administration to write it. The policy would bar city employees from requesting or disclosing personal information unless required by law or authorized, prohibit police from detaining or questioning people solely to determine immigration status, and forbid any agreement granting immigration-enforcement authority to city departments. It covers only agencies reporting to the mayor or chief administrative officer, so officials do not expect the Sheriff's Office, which runs the jail, to adopt it; the city will instead request annual data from the sheriff. The policy, which resembles those in Arlington and Fairfax counties, now goes to City Council, and Avula hopes to have it approved and fully implemented by year's end.

Business

State Corporation Commission staff have recommended rejecting the proposed $67 billion merger of Richmond-based Dominion Energy and Florida's NextEra Energy as currently structured. Staff witness Carol Myers concluded the deal does not meet Virginia's Utility Transfers Act, arguing it would expose Dominion's Virginia utility to the risks of NextEra's unregulated operations, and proposed 57 conditions, including a $100 million workforce fund and keeping Dominion Energy Virginia a separate legal entity. Gov. Abigail Spanberger and Clean Virginia have also filed opposing testimony. Public hearings, including an in-person session in Richmond on Nov. 5, come first, with an evidentiary hearing beginning Nov. 17 and a commission decision due by Jan. 11, 2027.

San Antonio, TX

Government

Five San Antonio City Council members, led by Sukh Kaur, Jalen McKee-Rodriguez, Ric Galvan, Marina Alderete Gavito and Marc Whyte, sent City Manager Erik Walsh a memo on October 22 laying out their own version of the city's $442 million 2027 bond program, setting up a direct contest with Mayor Gina Ortiz Jones. Their plan sets aside $124 million for citywide projects, including $35 million for infrastructure around the proposed downtown Spurs arena district, and sends $318 million to the 10 council districts using an existing formula based on roadway mileage and street condition. The mayor wants other money, such as state hotel-tax revenue, to pay for arena-area work and would steer more bond dollars to historically underserved districts. The dispute comes as the council revisits the package on Thursday, October 29, with voters getting the final say in May 2027. Council members have also stripped the mayor of the power to pick the bond process’s three tri-chairs, raising questions about delays.

Read more: TPR: council alternative bond plan TPR: tri-chair dispute

Business

San Antonio-based refiner Valero reported $6.8 billion in net income for the third quarter on about $46 billion in revenue, more than it earned in 2024 and 2025 combined. Refining operating income jumped to $8.8 billion from $1.6 billion a year earlier, as Middle East conflict, Strait of Hormuz disruptions and damaged refineries abroad tightened fuel supply and lifted diesel prices. Trinity University and other economists told the San Antonio Report the windfall should support job security at the company’s headquarters but is unlikely to add many local jobs, since Valero’s workforce has held near 9,800 even after record 2022 profits. They also cautioned that such margins are cyclical and likely to fall once supply recovers.
