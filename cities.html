Columbus, OH

Government

Columbus City Council leaders are preparing a policy package to address the rapid expansion of data centers in central Ohio. Council President Shannon Hardin and Councilmember Christopher Wyche scheduled two events for Wednesday night, beginning with a 5 p.m. news conference at City Hall. The centerpiece is an ordinance that would direct the city's water and power utilities to develop new rates, charges or fees for high-load, high-volume water users, including data centers. The proposal follows a council hearing that spotlighted the debate over how much strain the facilities place on local infrastructure. Statewide, Dr. Amy Acton and Conserve Ohio are urging the General Assembly to pass a data center moratorium when lawmakers return to Columbus next week, so the city's approach may shape a larger fight over who pays for the region's growth as a data center hub.

Ohio State University's total enrollment rose 1.4 percent over last year to 68,183 students, according to the Ohio Capital Journal's Sept. 28 news roundup. The figure reinforces Ohio State's place as one of the country's largest campuses and as a major driver of Columbus's housing demand, research economy and workforce pipeline. Growth at that scale keeps pressure on neighborhoods around campus even as the university supplies much of the talent behind the region's tech and health care employers.

Pittsburgh, PA

Government

The Allegheny County Board of Health voted unanimously on Monday to approve County Executive Sara Innamorato's paid parental leave mandate, sending it to County Council for a final vote. Under the revised proposal, employers with 15 or more eligible workers would have to provide up to 12 weeks of paid leave, down from 18 weeks in earlier drafts, and workers must have six months on the job and more than 600 hours of service to qualify. The Allegheny Conference on Community Development opposes the measure, arguing it puts a financial and administrative burden on local employers. If council passes it, Allegheny County would be the first county government in the country to adopt such a mandate, making the fight a closely watched test case well beyond Pittsburgh.

Read more: PublicSource Post-Gazette WPXI

Government

Pittsburgh's public housing agency voted 7-0 on September 24 to hire Ivory Mathews as its new executive director, replacing Caster Binion, who led the authority for 13 years. Mathews ran Home Forward, the housing authority in Portland, Oregon, from 2022 until April, and brings nearly three decades of affordable housing experience. The choice comes with scrutiny: she spent more than $100,000 in taxpayer-funded travel between 2023 and 2025, and she faces an Oregon state ethics investigation opened last month. How she handles those questions will shape trust in an agency that serves thousands of city residents.

Austin, TX

Government & Politics

A District 1 candidate for Austin City Council, Misael Ramos, was arrested Saturday, Sept. 26 on a Class B misdemeanor riot-participation charge stemming from a protest six days earlier against U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement, after an ICE officer shot and killed local delivery driver Wilber Garces Perez. Texas Department of Public Safety troopers said Ramos was captured on camera moving construction barricades and sandbags to obstruct traffic during the demonstration; he was booked at the Travis County Jail and released several hours later on $1,000 bail. Ramos, a democratic socialist and one of six candidates in the District 1 race, denies any criminal wrongdoing and says he intends to fight the charge as the campaign heads toward the Nov. 3 election. The arrest has become a flashpoint in the race, drawing statements of support from fellow candidates and renewed scrutiny of ICE's conduct in Austin. KUT's reporting and coverage from KXAN lay out the timeline in full.

Economics & Business

Two environmental and community advocacy groups, the Save Our Springs Alliance and PODER (People Organized in Defense of Earth and Her Resources), filed suit in Travis County District Court on Sept. 18 seeking to void the financing and development agreements behind the massive Dog's Head project on Austin's east side. The lawsuit argues the City Council's approval amounted to an "unconstitutional illegal gift" of more than $1 billion in public tax-increment financing to Endeavor Real Estate Group, and that officials rushed the deal through without the standard board reviews or public notice. At issue is a 2,600-acre development where Amazon is now expected to anchor a roughly 300-acre robotics facility — a detail, dubbed "Project Toaster," that KUT reports wasn't publicly revealed until just two days before the council locked in the tax-increment reinvestment zone. Attorney Bobby Levinski, representing the plaintiffs, said the deal hands Austin's regulatory authority over the zone to the developer for 45 years. The case is likely to shape debate over how the city handles large incentive packages heading into next year's budget cycle.

Richmond, VA

Government

Mayor Danny Avula's administration is racing to finalize a rooftop solar agreement covering 39 city-owned buildings, from fire stations and police precincts to the city's wastewater treatment plant, in partnership with private developer Secure Solar Futures. Under the proposed 25-year deal, the company would install the panels at no upfront cost to Richmond, which would then buy the electricity at a rate locked in below what Dominion Energy currently charges — a structure officials project could save the city roughly $19.4 million over the life of the contract. But as the City Council vets the fast-moving proposal, several members have raised concerns about steep early-termination penalties and contract language that could leave the city responsible for removing and reinstalling panels whenever a roof needs repair. The debate comes as electricity rates climb and federal clean-energy tax credits are set to wind down, adding pressure to move quickly even as some council members say the risks deserve more scrutiny before a vote.

Crime & Justice

A federal jury in Richmond convicted three people, including twin sisters who ran a Henrico-based mental health agency, in an $11 million Medicaid fraud and kickback scheme that unfolded over nearly four years. E'mon Ambers, chief executive of Divine Youth Counseling, and her sister Armone' Ambers, the program director, were found guilty alongside employee Traquan Brown of billing Medicaid for "crisis stabilization" and "mobile crisis" mental-health visits that were staffed by a single clinician while claiming two professionals were present. The scheme ran from January 2022 through October 2025, according to court records detailing the convictions, and included charges of wire and health care fraud conspiracy, aggravated identity theft, kickbacks and money laundering; E'mon Ambers alone faces up to 92 years in prison at sentencing. The case is among the largest health care fraud prosecutions to come out of the Richmond region in recent years and signals intensified federal scrutiny of Virginia's Medicaid-funded behavioral health providers.

San Antonio, TX

Government & Politics

San Antonio's City Council voted 7-4 on September 17 to adopt a $4.4 billion budget for fiscal year 2027 that raises the property tax rate by 3.9%, the city's first tax-rate increase in 34 years, over the objection of Mayor Gina Ortiz Jones. The increase, paired with new fees and targeted spending cuts, is meant to help close a roughly $158 million shortfall that city staff have warned would otherwise recur each of the next two budget cycles. The vote split largely along geographic and income lines, with council members from wealthier Northside districts joining the mayor in opposition while representatives of less affluent districts supplied the votes to pass it. Ortiz Jones argued the council had ducked harder structural choices, saying "we owe it to our neighbors to make the tough cuts, and unfortunately, this budget shows we punted." The approved plan avoids furloughs and layoffs for now, but officials are already flagging another difficult budget fight for fiscal 2028.

Crime & Justice

San Antonio's Public Safety Committee got its first detailed look on September 23 at a possible new ordinance aimed at crime concentrated at the city's budget motels, an effort that began with a council request from District 2's Jalen McKee-Rodriguez to cut down on repeat police calls and nuisance activity at chronically troubled properties. A staff analysis covering October 2023 through June 2026 examined 150 motels citywide and found that a subset of lower-priced, aging properties generate police calls, offenses and 311 complaints at disproportionately high rates compared with the rest of San Antonio. Rather than a single blanket rule, committee members floated a mix of carrots and enforcement, including facade grants and added street lighting for operators willing to make voluntary safety upgrades. Committee chair Sukh Kaur described the emerging approach as pairing resources for good-faith improvements with firmer standards for the worst offenders. No ordinance language has been drafted yet, and the committee is expected to keep refining the proposal before it reaches the full council.

Indianapolis, IN

Government & Politics

Indianapolis has seen a sharp rise in visible federal immigration enforcement since September 1, with agents making arrests in residential neighborhoods, at gas stations and during traffic stops across Marion County, often from unmarked vehicles and after foot chases. Video circulating online showed agents chasing a man across several lanes of traffic near Post Road and Interstate 70 on September 1, and local newsrooms logged numerous similar encounters in the days that followed, prompting several east-side businesses to close temporarily out of fear. U.S. Rep. André Carson responded by launching a formal congressional inquiry, sending a letter to Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin and acting ICE director David Venturella demanding the scope and legal basis for what he called the "apparent increased enforcement" in the city. Reaction has split sharply: immigrant aid groups warn the stops amount to racial profiling, while at least one U.S. senator has publicly welcomed the crackdown, and ICE has not confirmed a surge in activity or offered an official accounting of its Marion County operations. That leaves residents and city officials relying mostly on witness video and secondhand reports to gauge the scale of what is happening on their own streets.

Crime & Justice

Federal prosecutors filed wire fraud and tax charges this month against Sharon Jackson, 60, of Pendleton, who operated three Indianapolis childcare centers — Precious Hearts of Solid Rock, Greater St. Mark Five Star Ministry and Little Angels of Precious Hearts — at various points between 2017 and 2024. Investigators allege Jackson manipulated attendance records at Little Angels to fraudulently draw more than $359,000 from two government programs, the Child Care and Development Fund and the Child and Adult Care Food Program, which subsidize care and meals for low-income families. Court documents say she cut some parents in on the scheme, paying kickbacks through Cash App or by leaving cash hidden in gutters outside the facility in exchange for falsely reporting their children as present. The case was brought under the federal Task Force to Eliminate Fraud, and Jackson has agreed to plead guilty to both counts, though she had not yet formally entered a plea as of early September. The prosecution adds to a string of childcare-subsidy fraud cases in the city and renews scrutiny of oversight for programs that many low-income Indianapolis families depend on for daycare.

Nashville, TN

Government & Politics

Nashville's Metro Council is inching toward a scaled-back ban on right turns on red lights after months of wrangling over how far the restriction should reach. Councilmembers Jacob Kupin and Sandra Sepulveda originally proposed barring the turns at more than 700 signalized intersections across the Urban Services District, but after transportation officials called that version "overly broad," Sepulveda introduced a substitute narrowing the ban to 59 intersections concentrated downtown and near schools. The Traffic and Parking Commission voted 4-3 on September 15 to recommend the substitute, and the council postponed a final vote to its October 6 meeting after members held a public forum to gather feedback on the plan. Backers point to Nashville Department of Transportation and police data showing pedestrians have been struck by right-turning vehicles at the flagged intersections at least 18 times over the past five years, in a year when at least 20 pedestrians have been killed crossing Nashville streets overall, and a weekend follow-up from WSMV found residents still split on whether the narrower version will meaningfully improve safety or mostly just confuse drivers.

More than a year after 26-year-old Dante Jenoure was found shot multiple times in a breezeway at the Preakness Apartments on Bell Road, Metro Nashville police say they've caught the man responsible in Florida. The arrest closes out a lengthy investigation and gives Jenoure's family a criminal case to follow after more than a year of uncertainty.

Boston, MA

Government & Politics

Boston city councilors Liz Breadon and Gabriela Coletta Zapata introduced a measure on September 17 to extend the city's condominium-conversion tenant protections beyond large apartment buildings to triple-deckers and two-family homes, using authority the state legislature granted Boston in 2024. The current ordinance, which requires notice, relocation assistance and other safeguards before a landlord can convert to condos, applies only to buildings of four or more units built before 1983, leaving out much of the city's classic triple-decker housing stock. Breadon, who represents Allston-Brighton, argued that triple-deckers make up a large share of the city's "naturally affordable" housing and that tenants in neighborhoods such as Dorchester, Mattapan, Roxbury, Hyde Park, West Roxbury, Roslindale and East Boston face displacement risk without the same protections as apartment dwellers. The proposal now moves to the council's Committee on Government Operations, chaired by Coletta Zapata, for public hearings before any ordinance goes to Mayor Michelle Wu, and supporters say it could become one piece of a broader push to modernize outdated housing laws amid the city's ongoing affordability crunch.

A person was shot in the leg near the intersection of Dudley and Washington streets in Roxbury on the evening of September 16, and the ensuing chase ended with two suspects under arrest after their scooter collided with a marked Boston police cruiser on Madison Park Court. Officers responding to the initial report of shots fired spotted the scooter fleeing the wrong way down Shawmut Avenue, and after the crash both riders tried to escape on foot before being caught following a brief chase, according to Boston police. A firearm — a FN Five-Seven with an obliterated serial number and an empty large-capacity magazine — was found discarded near the cruiser, and a search also turned up individually wrapped bags of crack cocaine. Christopher Amill Jr., 20, of Dorchester, was arraigned in Roxbury District Court on firearm, assault and resisting-arrest charges, while a 17-year-old boy was also taken into custody, and the victim's wound was described as non-life-threatening.
