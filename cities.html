Columbus, OH

Government & Politics

Columbus voters will decide the future shape of their city government this November after City Council passed two charter amendments on August 24 and sent them to the ballot. The first is the citizen-driven Our City Our Say measure, which would convert all nine council seats from the current at-large system — in which every resident votes on every seat — to true district elections where voters choose only their own neighborhood representative. Council answered with its own competing proposal that would expand the body from nine members to 13, blending district seats with four at-large seats phased in across the 2027 and 2029 election cycles. The two sides had spent early August weighing a compromise before council opted to let voters choose between the approaches. The outcome matters well beyond procedure: Columbus is the largest U.S. city that still elects its entire council at large, a structure critics say leaves fast-growing neighborhoods without a dedicated advocate at City Hall.

Crime & Justice

A six-hour public hearing on August 10 turned into a referendum on police surveillance in Columbus, with 55 residents testifying and the feedback running overwhelmingly against the city's Flock license-plate camera network. The hearing followed Mayor Andrew Ginther's order halting statewide Flock data sharing after an audit found the Columbus camera network had been searched roughly 15,000 times in connection with immigration enforcement — a use residents say was never part of the bargain when the city signed its two-year, $228,000 contract in 2024. Councilmember Emmanuel Remy, who requested the audit, convened the session; a Flock company representative told council the firm welcomes regulation and is adding safeguards, but left the meeting before public comment began after being booed by members of the Safety Not Surveillance Coalition. Police leadership continues to defend the technology as a tool in missing-persons and violent-crime cases. No decision was made at the hearing, and the contract comes up for renewal in December, setting up a fall of negotiation over what data the city collects and who gets to see it.

Richmond, VA

Government & Housing Policy

Mayor Danny Avula unveiled the third draft of "Code Refresh" at City Hall on Aug. 17, the latest turn in Richmond's yearslong effort to rewrite a zoning code that has not been substantially overhauled since 1976. The headline change is a retreat: the administration dropped the proposal to allow duplexes by right in every residential district, a provision that drew sustained pushback from single-family neighborhoods in earlier drafts. In its place is a "1+1" framework permitting one primary dwelling plus one attached or detached accessory dwelling unit, with contextual standards governing height, setbacks and fit within the surrounding block. Duplexes remain by-right only in "residential attached" zones, which cover much of The Fan, Jackson Ward and Church Hill. The draft also adds contextual height limits and infrastructure protections addressing parking, narrow streets and aging utilities — concessions aimed at neighborhood groups, though housing advocates argue the softened density rules will blunt the code's impact on Richmond's supply crunch.

Schools & Public Facilities

Richmond Public Schools held a town hall this week after mold and air-quality problems forced the closure of Barack Obama Elementary on Fendall Avenue, displacing students days into the new academic year and reigniting a long-running fight over the condition of the district's aging buildings. The timing is pointed: the RPS Finance and Audit Committee received the first segment of a district-wide facilities maintenance audit examining how work orders for repair requests are processed and tracked. Prior reviews had already flagged tens of millions of dollars in deferred and "necessary" construction across the portfolio, and parents at the town hall pressed Superintendent Jason Kamras on why routine maintenance requests appear to stall before reaching crews. The audit findings land as City Council weighs capital allocations, making building conditions a live budget question rather than an abstract one.

San Antonio, TX

Government & Environment

The fight over San Antonio's data center boom came to a head at City Hall this week, with council members signaling they favor writing new rules into the city's Unified Development Code rather than imposing the outright building pause three of their colleagues had demanded. District 6 Councilman Ric Galvan, joined by Teri Castillo (D5) and Ivalis Meza Gonzalez (D8), had filed a three-signature memo in early August asking for a three- to six-month moratorium on new data center applications, permits and development plans while the city studied the industry's demands on water, electricity and air quality. Galvan also wants CPS Energy and the San Antonio Water System to require clean energy and recycled water for any new facilities. City staff instead recommended regulating through the development code, and at the August 25 session council appeared to line up behind that approach, leaving the moratorium question for a separate vote next month. The stakes are considerable for a city that has aggressively courted large-load tech tenants while simultaneously warning residents about aquifer levels and summer grid strain.

Government & Economics

San Antonio is heading toward its first property tax rate increase in more than three decades as the city tries to close a roughly $158 million shortfall spread over the next two budget years. The proposed $4.4 billion fiscal year 2027 spending plan would raise the rate to 56.288 cents per $100 of valuation — a 3.9% bump that staff estimate would cost the average homestead about $49 more a year, or under $3 a month, while generating roughly $29 million. That is paired with about $90 million in cuts and funding shifts, including the elimination of more than 100 civilian positions, department consolidations and defunding of several senior nutrition centers. The proposal has drawn sharp objections on the dais, with at least one council member calling the hike "unconscionable," though the increase is the largest allowed without triggering a public vote and survived an initial procedural test. Council is scheduled to adopt a final budget on September 17, and separately placed a $489 million city contribution to the downtown Spurs arena project on the November 3 ballot.

Indianapolis, IN

Government & Politics

The Indianapolis City-County Council handed Mayor Joe Hogsett a rare defeat this month, voting to override his veto of a new annual vehicle tax for Marion County. Councilors originally approved the wheel-tax measure in July as a dedicated funding stream for road repairs, arguing that Indianapolis’s deteriorating streets could not wait for state road-funding reform. Hogsett vetoed it, saying a flat per-vehicle charge would fall hardest on residents already squeezed by rising costs. Under the ordinance as enacted, owners of most cars, trucks and SUVs registered in Marion County and weighing under 11,000 pounds will pay a flat $100 a year, while heavier vehicles face a $240 annual fee. The override lands in the middle of a tense budget season in which councilors are working through department presentations against a backdrop of constrained revenue, following last year’s $1.7 billion 2026 budget that passed only after extended debate over taxes and service levels.

Business & Environment

Indianapolis has formally hit pause on new data centers. On August 19 the Metropolitan Development Commission voted 6-0 to adopt zoning proposal 2026-AO-001, giving final approval to a moratorium that halts new data center permitting through no later than December 31, 2027. The measure, which cleared the City-County Council earlier in the month and was backed by Mayor Hogsett, took effect immediately and follows months of neighborhood protest over the facilities’ water use, electricity demand and noise. City planners say the pause buys time to convene a committee of experts and residents to study campus size, building height, sound thresholds and environmental impacts before writing permanent rules. Indiana law shields projects already in the pipeline, so the three campuses that drew the loudest opposition — DC Blox in Warren Township, Metrobloks in Martindale-Brightwood and Sabey in Decatur Township — are exempt. Meanwhile, Indy Economic Development Inc. is building a scoring matrix to judge which future data center proposals, if any, deserve city incentives. Nearly a third of Indiana counties have now moved to restrict the industry in some form.

Nashville, TN

Government & Development

Nashville's Metro Council took the unusual step of pointing eminent domain at a data center, voting 27–5 in the early hours of Aug. 5 to authorize Metro to acquire the 23.5-acre South Nashville parcel that DC BLOX bought for roughly $23 million next to the Nashville Zoo. Mayor Freddie O'Connell's administration backed the measure, saying the site would serve public uses such as offices, warehouse space and training facilities, while the zoo and neighborhood groups had campaigned loudly against a 69,200-square-foot, 10-megawatt facility rising on their doorstep. The Nashville Banner reported the council first authorized negotiations with DC BLOX, with condemnation as the fallback if a deal can't be reached. The catch is price: WSMV notes Metro would likely owe at least $37 million, well above what the company paid. The vote is a notable marker in the national fight over where data centers get built, and Nashville has now planted a flag on the side of the neighbors.

Economics & Public Finance

A second big-money item moved at the council's Aug. 18 meeting: legislation to refinance Music City Center debt and route $300 million in excess tourism tax revenue toward East Bank infrastructure. The companion resolution passed 31–6 and the ordinance cleared second reading, with a final vote set for Sept. 1. The mechanics matter more than the headline number — refinancing releases Metro's backstop on the convention center debt, freeing borrowing capacity that city officials want to aim at an affordable housing bond program, and the package also clears the way for a Music City Center expansion that could run to $1 billion. The $300 million redirect traces back to state legislation moving the tourism surplus to the East Bank and lower Broadway, a running source of friction between Metro and Republican leaders on Capitol Hill. Separately, Council Member Sandra Sepulveda is pressing ahead with a bill to ban right turns on red across much of the city, citing 21 pedestrian deaths on Nashville roads so far in 2026 — double last year's pace at the same point.

Boston, MA

Crime & Public Safety

A string of violent incidents in and around Downtown Crossing has pushed public safety to the top of Boston's civic agenda this month. After assaults and a stabbing rattled merchants and commuters, civic leaders and the Wu administration convened a series of emergency meetings on downtown safety in early August, with retailers describing juvenile theft, vandalism, and assaults as increasingly untenable for street-level businesses. The Boston Police Department followed with a targeted shoplifting operation in Downtown Crossing on August 6 that produced multiple arrests and summonses, and the department has since expanded patrols across the district at the urging of the downtown neighborhood association. The pressure has not let up: police are now investigating a series of overnight commercial burglaries that began August 21, in which a suspect used a pry tool to force entry into businesses. For a district that anchors both the city's retail tax base and its tourism economy, the question heading into fall is whether a heavier police footprint alone can restore confidence, or whether the underlying issues around juvenile services and the downtown's post-pandemic foot-traffic mix require a broader response.

Business & Development

The Red Sox are leaving Fenway Park — at least as an office address. The club has leased the top three floors of a 13-story Boylston Street building steps from the ballpark and will vacate the cramped offices tucked inside Fenway, consolidating staff from the team, Fenway Sports Group, and Fenway Sports Management under one roof. The move is more than a real estate shuffle: the 250,000-square-foot building is the opening phase of Fenway Corners, the roughly $1.6 billion mixed-use project the team is developing with WS Development, which envisions eight new buildings ringing the ballpark. Landing an anchor tenant — even a related one — is a meaningful signal in a Boston office market still working through elevated vacancy and soft demand for older Class B space. For the Fenway neighborhood, it accelerates a decade-long transformation from game-day parking lots into a year-round district, though the pace of the remaining phases will depend on financing conditions and city permitting.
