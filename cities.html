Dallas, TX

Government

Downtown Dallas is trying to chart a future after AT&T's planned exit, and the fate of the city's own headquarters is part of that debate. City staff estimated that renovating City Hall could cost at least $152 million and as much as $345 million, driven by garage repairs, water infiltration and HVAC upgrades. The council adopted a resolution directing the city manager to explore relocation and redevelopment options, while also weighing repair scenarios. Developer Ray Washburne has pitched the site as a possible Mavericks arena and entertainment district, an idea that has split council members, some of whom worry the land would effectively be given away. How the city resolves the question will help shape whether the core of downtown rebounds after losing one of its largest employers.

Business

In the northern suburbs, Plano voters will decide in a Nov. 3 special election whether to approve a package of venue taxes to help pay for a proposed Dallas Stars arena at the site of The Shops at Willow Bend. The options include a hotel occupancy tax increase of up to 2%, a rental car tax of up to 5%, event parking fees and an admissions tax. City leaders estimate those taxes plus revenue from the Willow Bend tax increment zone could contribute up to $700 million to the project. Competing campaigns have formed, with one group backing the arena and another, Stop the Stars Arena, citing traffic, noise and suburban character. The outcome will signal how much appetite the region has for publicly supported entertainment districts.

Chicago, IL

Crime & Justice

Mayor Brandon Johnson's administration has proposed a $260.8 million settlement covering 17 men who were exonerated after being wrongfully convicted in cases tied to former police detective Reynaldo Guevara. The men spent between 18 and 30 years in prison, more than 383 years combined, after being coerced into confessions. If the City Council approves it, the deal could become the largest single police misconduct settlement in U.S. history, with payments spread over two years starting in 2027. Johnson's office argues that resolving the cases together will save taxpayers an estimated $300 million to $400 million compared with the risk of going to trial, while critics are already objecting to the size of the payout. The city's announcement frames the deal as closing one of the most costly chapters of the Chicago Police Department's history, and it now heads to the Finance Committee and the full Council.

Read more: Chicago Sun-Times CBS Chicago ABC7 Chicago

Government & Politics

Early voting for the November 3 general election opened Thursday at Chicago's new downtown supersite at 137 South State Street, which replaces the longtime Clark and Lake location. The space, a former CVS on the ground floor of a cooling plant building at State and Adams, can handle 80 voters at a time and offers an audio ballot and materials in 12 languages. It is open Monday through Friday from 9 a.m. to 6 p.m., Saturday until 5 p.m. and Sunday from 10 a.m. to 4 p.m., with weekday hours stretching to 7 p.m. from October 26. Early-vote sites open in all 50 wards starting October 19, which the Board of Election Commissioners hopes will ease Election Day crowds. Details are on the Board of Elections site.

Los Angeles, CA

Government

The Los Angeles City Council in September approved roughly $13 million in new vehicles for the LAPD, a purchase tied to the department's buildup ahead of the 2028 Olympics, according to Hoodline's report on the vote. The package covers crew-cab trucks, transport vans, multi-use dually trucks, utility task vehicles, unmarked investigative vehicles and incident-response trucks. The spending lands on a council that has been under scrutiny, and the city remains in a crowded election cycle, with runoff contests set for November 3 following the June primary. For residents, the vote is an early look at how the city plans to pay for Olympic-era public safety readiness while budgets remain tight.

Crime & Justice

Councilmember Curren Price, who represents District 9, is headed toward trial on 12 felony public corruption counts: five of embezzlement of government funds, four of conflict of interest and three of perjury. Prosecutors allege a company tied to his wife's associate received more than $950,000 from developers, the Housing Authority and LA Metro before Price voted on related projects, and that he used $33,800 in city funds toward his wife's medical benefits. A second judge rejected his motion to set aside the charges in August, finding ample evidence for probable cause. Price has pleaded not guilty and is due back in court October 16 for a pretrial hearing and trial setting, a date that will shape how long the case hangs over the council.

New York, NY

Crime

New York is on pace to finish 2026 as its safest year in recorded history, according to NYPD data, with the fewest murders, shooting incidents, shooting victims and robberies since record-keeping began. Major felony crime is down 2% citywide, while murders have fallen 20%. NYPD Commissioner Jessica Tisch has said the city is also headed for record-low gun violence. The city's Mayor's Management Report shows shooting incidents dropped 16% from 804 in Fiscal 2025 to 673 in Fiscal 2026, a 61% decline from the peak of 1,741. The numbers give Mayor Zohran Mamdani's administration a strong talking point as it develops its public-safety agenda.

Read more: FOX 5 NY CBS New York

Government

City Council Speaker Julie Menin is increasingly acting as a counterweight to Mayor Mamdani's agenda. This fall the Council has highlighted proposed e-bike legislation ahead of a major hearing, unveiled proposals to protect New Yorkers from risks of artificial intelligence, and announced that major AI companies would be asked to testify publicly under oath about AI-related incidents. It also announced a $700,000 pilot providing free, unlimited OMNY transit cards to CUNY students. Meanwhile, the Mayor's Office has rolled out the city's first strategy to combat antisemitism and a $607 million modernization at Nostrand Houses, setting up a busy autumn of negotiation between City Hall and the Council.

Columbus, OH

Government

Columbus City Council leaders are preparing a policy package to address the rapid expansion of data centers in central Ohio. Council President Shannon Hardin and Councilmember Christopher Wyche scheduled two events for Wednesday night, beginning with a 5 p.m. news conference at City Hall. The centerpiece is an ordinance that would direct the city's water and power utilities to develop new rates, charges or fees for high-load, high-volume water users, including data centers. The proposal follows a council hearing that spotlighted the debate over how much strain the facilities place on local infrastructure. Statewide, Dr. Amy Acton and Conserve Ohio are urging the General Assembly to pass a data center moratorium when lawmakers return to Columbus next week, so the city's approach may shape a larger fight over who pays for the region's growth as a data center hub.

Growth at that scale keeps pressure on neighborhoods around campus even as the university supplies much of the talent behind the region's tech and health care employers.

Pittsburgh, PA

Government

The Allegheny County Board of Health voted unanimously on Monday to approve County Executive Sara Innamorato's paid parental leave mandate, sending it to County Council for a final vote. Under the revised proposal, employers with 15 or more eligible workers would have to provide up to 12 weeks of paid leave, down from 18 weeks in earlier drafts, and workers must have six months on the job and more than 600 hours of service to qualify. The Allegheny Conference on Community Development opposes the measure, arguing it puts a financial and administrative burden on local employers. If council passes it, Allegheny County would be the first county government in the country to adopt such a mandate, making the fight a closely watched test case well beyond Pittsburgh.

Read more: PublicSource Post-Gazette WPXI

Government

Pittsburgh's public housing agency voted 7-0 on September 24 to hire Ivory Mathews as its new executive director, replacing Caster Binion, who led the authority for 13 years. Mathews ran Home Forward, the housing authority in Portland, Oregon, from 2022 until April, and brings nearly three decades of affordable housing experience. The choice comes with scrutiny: she spent more than $100,000 in taxpayer-funded travel between 2023 and 2025, and she faces an Oregon state ethics investigation opened last month. How she handles those questions will shape trust in an agency that serves thousands of city residents.

Austin, TX

Government & Politics

A District 1 candidate for Austin City Council, Misael Ramos, was arrested Saturday, Sept. 26 on a Class B misdemeanor riot-participation charge stemming from a protest six days earlier against U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement, after an ICE officer shot and killed local delivery driver Wilber Garces Perez. Texas Department of Public Safety troopers said Ramos was captured on camera moving construction barricades and sandbags to obstruct traffic during the demonstration; he was booked at the Travis County Jail and released several hours later on $1,000 bail. Ramos, a democratic socialist and one of six candidates in the District 1 race, denies any criminal wrongdoing and says he intends to fight the charge as the campaign heads toward the Nov. 3 election. The arrest has become a flashpoint in the race, drawing statements of support from fellow candidates and renewed scrutiny of ICE's conduct in Austin. KUT's reporting and coverage from KXAN lay out the timeline in full.

Economics & Business

Two environmental and community advocacy groups, the Save Our Springs Alliance and PODER (People Organized in Defense of Earth and Her Resources), filed suit in Travis County District Court on Sept. 18 seeking to void the financing and development agreements behind the massive Dog's Head project on Austin's east side. The lawsuit argues the City Council's approval amounted to an "unconstitutional illegal gift" of more than $1 billion in public tax-increment financing to Endeavor Real Estate Group, and that officials rushed the deal through without the standard board reviews or public notice. At issue is a 2,600-acre development where Amazon is now expected to anchor a roughly 300-acre robotics facility — a detail, dubbed "Project Toaster," that KUT reports wasn't publicly revealed until just two days before the council locked in the tax-increment reinvestment zone. Attorney Bobby Levinski, representing the plaintiffs, said the deal hands Austin's regulatory authority over the zone to the developer for 45 years. The case is likely to shape debate over how the city handles large incentive packages heading into next year's budget cycle.

Richmond, VA

Government

Mayor Danny Avula's administration is racing to finalize a rooftop solar agreement covering 39 city-owned buildings, from fire stations and police precincts to the city's wastewater treatment plant, in partnership with private developer Secure Solar Futures. Under the proposed 25-year deal, the company would install the panels at no upfront cost to Richmond, which would then buy the electricity at a rate locked in below what Dominion Energy currently charges — a structure officials project could save the city roughly $19.4 million over the life of the contract. But as the City Council vets the fast-moving proposal, several members have raised concerns about steep early-termination penalties and contract language that could leave the city responsible for removing and reinstalling panels whenever a roof needs repair. The debate comes as electricity rates climb and federal clean-energy tax credits are set to wind down, adding pressure to move quickly even as some council members say the risks deserve more scrutiny before a vote.

Crime & Justice

A federal jury in Richmond convicted three people, including twin sisters who ran a Henrico-based mental health agency, in an $11 million Medicaid fraud and kickback scheme that unfolded over nearly four years. E'mon Ambers, chief executive of Divine Youth Counseling, and her sister Armone' Ambers, the program director, were found guilty alongside employee Traquan Brown of billing Medicaid for "crisis stabilization" and "mobile crisis" mental-health visits that were staffed by a single clinician while claiming two professionals were present. The scheme ran from January 2022 through October 2025, according to court records detailing the convictions, and included charges of wire and health care fraud conspiracy, aggravated identity theft, kickbacks and money laundering; E'mon Ambers alone faces up to 92 years in prison at sentencing. The case is among the largest health care fraud prosecutions to come out of the Richmond region in recent years and signals intensified federal scrutiny of Virginia's Medicaid-funded behavioral health providers.
