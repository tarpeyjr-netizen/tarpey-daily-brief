Austin, TX

Economics & Business

A Southeast Austin grocer is warning that the city's light rail plans could put it out of business. Austin Transit Partnership, the agency building the largest piece of Project Connect, may use eminent domain to take part of the Riverside property of JD's Supermarket near East Riverside and Montopolis drives. The current design would leave the building standing but take the land holding the gas pumps, which store representatives say bring in about 30% of sales and whose loss could force closure within one or two years. The store has employed about 90 people since opening in 2023 and serves roughly 2,250 EBT customers a month. ATP says the design does not displace the store and is not final, and JD's is scouting nearby sites that could keep the fuel business alive, but no timeline for any condemnation or final route decision has been set.

Government & Politics

Austin City Council is facing questions about how it approved about $4.1 million in extensions for 14 nonprofit social service contracts on Sept. 24 without the full picture. Records obtained by the Statesman show a staff memo flagging compliance concerns at five of the organizations and a performance shortfall at a sixth reached council nine minutes after the vote. Council Member Marc Duchen called it an unacceptable administrative mistake and is worried about the roughly $101 million the city plans to spend on social services amid budget shortfalls. A February presentation found Austin spends about 85% of its General Fund on social services, far above similar cities. The council is considering a directive for geographic data in future reporting, and the city expects a broader strategic reset of its social services portfolio in December.

Richmond, VA

Government

Mayor Danny Avula has unveiled a draft immigration "Trust Policy" that sets rules for how city government deals with federal immigration enforcement, following a City Council vote in August directing the administration to write it. The policy would bar city employees from requesting or disclosing personal information unless required by law or authorized, prohibit police from detaining or questioning people solely to determine immigration status, and forbid any agreement granting immigration-enforcement authority to city departments. It covers only agencies reporting to the mayor or chief administrative officer, so officials do not expect the Sheriff's Office, which runs the jail, to adopt it; the city will instead request annual data from the sheriff. The policy, which resembles those in Arlington and Fairfax counties, now goes to City Council, and Avula hopes to have it approved and fully implemented by year's end.

Business

State Corporation Commission staff have recommended rejecting the proposed $67 billion merger of Richmond-based Dominion Energy and Florida's NextEra Energy as currently structured. Staff witness Carol Myers concluded the deal does not meet Virginia's Utility Transfers Act, arguing it would expose Dominion's Virginia utility to the risks of NextEra's unregulated operations, and proposed 57 conditions, including a $100 million workforce fund and keeping Dominion Energy Virginia a separate legal entity. Gov. Abigail Spanberger and Clean Virginia have also filed opposing testimony. Public hearings, including an in-person session in Richmond on Nov. 5, come first, with an evidentiary hearing beginning Nov. 17 and a commission decision due by Jan. 11, 2027.

San Antonio, TX

Government

Five San Antonio City Council members, led by Sukh Kaur, Jalen McKee-Rodriguez, Ric Galvan, Marina Alderete Gavito and Marc Whyte, sent City Manager Erik Walsh a memo on October 22 laying out their own version of the city's $442 million 2027 bond program, setting up a direct contest with Mayor Gina Ortiz Jones. Their plan sets aside $124 million for citywide projects, including $35 million for infrastructure around the proposed downtown Spurs arena district, and sends $318 million to the 10 council districts using an existing formula based on roadway mileage and street condition. The mayor wants other money, such as state hotel-tax revenue, to pay for arena-area work and would steer more bond dollars to historically underserved districts. The dispute comes as the council revisits the package on Thursday, October 29, with voters getting the final say in May 2027. Council members have also stripped the mayor of the power to pick the bond process’s three tri-chairs, raising questions about delays.

Read more: TPR: council alternative bond plan TPR: tri-chair dispute

Business

San Antonio-based refiner Valero reported $6.8 billion in net income for the third quarter on about $46 billion in revenue, more than it earned in 2024 and 2025 combined. Refining operating income jumped to $8.8 billion from $1.6 billion a year earlier, as Middle East conflict, Strait of Hormuz disruptions and damaged refineries abroad tightened fuel supply and lifted diesel prices. Trinity University and other economists told the San Antonio Report the windfall should support job security at the company’s headquarters but is unlikely to add many local jobs, since Valero’s workforce has held near 9,800 even after record 2022 profits. They also cautioned that such margins are cyclical and likely to fall once supply recovers.

Indianapolis, IN

Crime & Justice

WFYI reported on October 22 that Marion County is winding down several problem-solving courts after the Indiana Department of Correction cut a grant of roughly $600,000 that supported drug, reentry and veterans courts. The combined reentry and drug court has already stopped accepting applicants, while the veterans court and mental health court have not yet cut services but could close by the end of 2027; the state's office of court services will fund the veterans court through 2027. Participants, who are tested twice a week and can have charges dismissed and expunged if they graduate, may be redirected to other services such as the county's Quality of Life Unit. Chief public defender Ray Casanova argues treatment courts are more cost-effective than incarceration, and some judges are hopeful reentry and drug court funding returns in 2028. The cut affects problem-solving courts statewide, since at least 60 of Indiana's 92 counties run one or more.

Read more: WFYI: Problem-solving courts being cut

Business

At an October 22 meeting in the Warren Township Schools administration building, California-based BRC Commercial Properties pitched a luxury resort redevelopment of the Washington Square mall on the Far Eastside, as Mirror Indy reported on October 23. CEO Britt Wagner described “The Washington Grand Hotel, Resort, and Conference Center,” with ideas including a hotel and spa, rooftop dining, an entertainment venue, senior living and a Ferris wheel, at an estimated cost of about $5 billion. Wagner would not name the investors, citing nondisclosure agreements, and BRC does not yet own the property: the mall has more than a dozen owners, and a representative of primary owner Durga Property Holdings called BRC's claim of a signed purchase agreement false. About two dozen residents attended; many agreed the site needs redevelopment, but some noted that no funding or investor had been identified. The mall remains open with about a dozen local businesses.

Nashville, TN

Government

A Thursday-night crash in which an SUV rear-ended a downtown pedal tavern, sending nearly a dozen people to the hospital, has put Nashville's pedal tavern rules under a spotlight. Metro police said the driver was distracted by his radio. The Transportation Licensing Commission oversees the vehicles through a 53-page rulebook that gives pedal taverns a single page, covering permit fees, background checks, downtown and Midtown operating zones, and rush-hour route limits, but nothing on seatbelts or other equipment to keep riders from falling off. Seven companies hold licenses for 30 pedal taverns, and the rules date to 2015, when Freddie O'Connell, now mayor, sponsored the original bill as a council member. O'Connell says the commission reviews safety concerns, but that new state tourism-development-zone legislation led the city to pause some downtown safety projects.

Read more: WSMV: pedal tavern rules

Business

Opry Mills' head of security, Richard Siegelman, told the Metro Council this week that the mall's license plate readers share data with repossession companies, which tow about three or four vehicles from the lot each week. Tennessee law bars government entities from keeping plate-reader data more than 90 days outside an investigation, but that limit does not apply to private operators such as the mall's owner, Simon Property Group. Mayor O'Connell called the technology a useful but imperfect safety tool and said private parking operators' readers have falsely flagged him, a problem he raised with the state Attorney General. An Electronic Frontier Foundation privacy expert warned that retention periods vary from days to years and that the data could be monetized. WSMV said Simon and Metro police had not answered questions about data sales and police access.

Government & Politics

The Boston City Council took the unusual step this month of formally summoning MBTA General Manager Phillip Eng over the controversial Blue Hill Avenue bus lane project, a move Mayor Michelle Wu's administration has criticized. St. Louis, MO

Crime & Justice

Federal officials closed out Operation Show Me Justice, an eight-week, ATF-led violent crime surge in St. Louis, at an October 15 press conference. The Justice Department reported 435 arrests and 166 firearms seized, along with about 81 kilograms of drugs, six machine gun conversion devices and more than $1.25 million in currency. Most of the arrests were tied to existing warrants, with 412 warrants cleared, and the operation targeted several local gangs alongside the U.S. Marshals Service and the Eastern District of Missouri U.S. Attorney's Office. The results land as the city's own numbers show homicides still being tracked closely: a police analysis dated October 9 counted 89 homicides so far this year. What happens once the surge teams leave, and whether local agencies can sustain the pace, is the open question for city leaders.

Economics & Business

BJC HealthCare and Washington University Medicine said they will leave the Medicare Advantage networks of both Aetna and Anthem on January 1, 2027, after failing to reach agreements before the current contracts expire December 31. The move could affect thousands of older adults in the region; one estimate puts the combined pool of Aetna and Anthem Medicare Advantage members at roughly 85,800, though not all use BJC facilities. The Post-Dispatch reported that WashU physicians are covered under a separate contract that is also ending. Medicare open enrollment runs through the end of the year, so seniors have a window to switch plans, but the dispute puts pressure on the region's largest health system and two major insurers to return to the table. Separately, developer TerraWatt has proposed a 200-megawatt data center on the shuttered Alton Steel site across the river, adding to the metro area's data center debate.

Baltimore, MD

Crime

Mayor Brandon Scott called police command staff into a closed-door meeting on Tuesday after five people were killed and as many as ten injured in shootings in under 48 hours. The incidents included two separate Sunday shootings in West and Northwest Baltimore, one on Muth Avenue and another on Belair Road on Monday night, with police saying multiple gunmen were involved in several of them and no arrests announced for the West Baltimore BP station attack. Scott said the city “cannot have another two weeks like the two weeks we’ve been having,” and Police Commissioner Richard Worley said the department will return to basics such as vehicle stops and approaching groups on corners, targeting the small groups driving retaliatory violence. Community partners such as Safe Streets sites are working to head off further retaliation. Despite the spike, homicides stood at 101 as of Tuesday morning, down from 105 at this point in 2025, while shootings are slightly up at 253 versus 247.

Crime

A Baltimore judge declared a mistrial on Tuesday in the case of James Barnett after jurors could not reach a unanimous verdict following nearly a week of deliberations; a juror told CBS News Baltimore the panel split 6 to 6. Barnett was charged over the January 24, 2022 Stricker Street rowhome fire in which Lt. Kelsey Sadler, Lt. Paul Butrim and firefighter-EMT Kenny Lacayo died when the building collapsed, and firefighter John McMaster was injured. The judge had dismissed the murder charges the week before, leaving second-degree arson, involuntary manslaughter and reckless endangerment. Prosecutors may retry the case, and the judge told them to schedule any retrial as soon as possible, leaving the families of the three firefighters and the fire department waiting nearly five years after the tragedy for a resolution.
