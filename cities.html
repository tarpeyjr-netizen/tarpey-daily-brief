San Diego, CA

Government

Mayor Todd Gloria on Thursday named Bethany Bezak, currently the city's chief performance and logistics officer, as San Diego's new chief operating officer, reversing his 2025 decision to fold the job into his own office. The appointment, which needs city council approval at a vote set for Monday, comes three weeks after more than 87,000 residents were incorrectly billed for waste services, a gaffe that led one councilmember to call for Gloria's resignation and another to demand the COO seat be filled. The role was eliminated in February 2025 when Gloria cut 31 City Hall positions to save about $5.3 million a year. Gloria said Bezak "demands accountability" and will build on the progress the city has made since he took direct oversight of operations.

Science & Environment

A wind-driven brush fire, named the Henderson Fire, erupted shortly before 2 p.m. Thursday in the western reaches of Camp Pendleton and burned more than 1,400 acres, with over 1,100 of them in the first two hours, according to Cal Fire. Authorities briefly closed Interstate 5 in both directions between Harbor Drive in Oceanside and Basilone Road during the evening commute, and an evacuation order for the base's Las Flores (41 Area) was lifted by 7:30 p.m. with no evacuations outside the base. The fire was at 0% containment late Thursday, though firefighters had slowed its progress considerably by 8 p.m. and the CHP warned the highway could close again if conditions required.

Minneapolis-St. Paul, MN

Government

Two progressive Minneapolis council members, Council President Elliott Payne and Robin Wonsley, have introduced an ordinance to eliminate the Office of Community Safety, the umbrella created in 2022 under Mayor Jacob Frey that oversees 911, fire, police and violence-prevention work. Under the proposal, department heads including police and fire would again report directly to the mayor, while the Department of Neighborhood Safety would survive. Wonsley argued the office added bureaucracy and has not shown a measurable benefit to the public over its three years. Frey called it a “wholesale elimination without a plan” and said responsibility for any lost services would rest with the council members. On Tuesday the council voted to hold a public hearing on the ordinance on Oct. 27, and at least one council member has already warned that the measure needs broader support to avoid a mayoral veto, as KARE 11 also reported.

Read more: MPR News KARE 11

Government

Minneapolis Public Schools has backed away from a plan to close as many as 16 schools, a vote the school board had expected to take in November. On Tuesday the board instead agreed to avoid any closures through the 2027-28 school year, following a recommendation from Superintendent Lisa Sayles-Adams, who said the community needed more time to process such a large change. The district still faces enrollment challenges, and Sayles-Adams warned that the coming budget process “might be difficult” with fewer options after significant cuts in recent years. The board committed to discussing enrollment growth, early childhood enrollment, school start times, school choice assignments and how needs-based funding is distributed. One board member told KARE 11 the district needs a more hands-on approach going forward.

Detroit, MI

Government

Detroit City Council has approved a three-year, $2.7 million contract to install surveillance cameras and lighting in four city parks: Chandler, Eliza-Howell, Maheras-Gentry and Rouge. The Detroit-based Bayview Electric Company LLC won the work, and the General Services Department says the parks have logged more than 700 security-related interactions since 2024. Council Member Gabriela Santiago-Romero cast the lone no vote, pointing to the city's earlier experience with Flock cameras, while Denzel McCampbell reluctantly backed the deal after the city answered his concerns about data stored on third-party servers. Footage will be kept up to 30 days, will feed the police Real Time Crime Center, and may not be used for immigration or traffic enforcement. The city must also send council quarterly reports on incidents and complaints, so the surveillance debate is likely to continue.

Government

With the Nov. 3 election weeks away, voters are being asked to fill four full-term seats and one partial term on the Detroit school board, with 31 candidates in the main race. The seven-member board sets district policy, approves the budget and contracts, and can hire or fire the superintendent. It also decides on school closures, a live issue after four schools were closed last year and Thurgood Marshall Elementary-Middle is set to close at the end of this school year. Candidates are split on how to handle the district's small, expensive buildings, according to a Chalkbeat Detroit guide. The next board will also write a new strategic plan as funding from the city's literacy lawsuit runs out.

San Francisco-Oakland, CA

Government

San Francisco's Board of Supervisors voted unanimously last week to pause construction of new data centers under a 45-day urgency ordinance that took effect immediately. The move makes San Francisco the first Bay Area county to enact such a moratorium. Coverage tags the issue to energy and water supply and to neighborhoods such as Bayview-Hunters Point and South of Market. The headline notes that permanent rules are being eyed, so the 45-day window is meant to give city officials time to craft longer-term standards for a fast-growing industry.

Read more: Local News Matters

Crime & Justice

The San Francisco Public Defender's Office alleged Thursday that the District Attorney's Office tried to cover up an assault by police officers in August 2025. The office says prosecutors charged the victim, 33-year-old Kevon Gibson, with crimes he did not commit, and it released body-worn camera footage showing officers pulling his hair; he was hospitalized for several days. Officers told the DA's Independent Investigations Bureau that Gibson had assaulted them, but video contradicted those accounts and a jury later found the officers' claims not credible. The article did not include a response from the DA's Office or the Police Department, so the dispute is likely to keep raising questions about police accountability in the city.

Seattle, WA

Crime

A security guard was killed and a 27-year-old woman seriously wounded early Sunday in a drive-by shooting outside Kittens Gentlemen’s Club in Seattle. The woman, who was inside the club, was shot in the leg. Seattle police later found a burned car in the Georgetown neighborhood that they say is tied to the shooting, and investigators believe the suspect torched it afterward. The club’s ownership had not responded to requests for comment as of Sunday.

Economics & Business

The Trump administration announced Thursday that it is suspending Microsoft, the Redmond-based employer, from the federal PERM program that H-1B workers use to apply for green cards. Adobe and six outsourcing firms were also named, and Vice President JD Vance said Microsoft laid off 6,000 American workers last year while benefiting from 6,300 H-1B visas and nearly 3,000 green cards. Microsoft pushed back, saying about 80% of its roughly 6,000 H-1B filings last fiscal year were extensions or status changes for existing employees rather than new hires. The dispute matters for the Seattle region, where the company is one of the largest private employers and many of its workers are on temporary visas.

Cleveland, OH

Collegiate / Education

Cleveland State University announced on Friday a new tuition program, the Cleveland State Commitment, that will cover tuition for eligible Ohio students starting in fall 2027. To qualify, students must graduate from an Ohio high school in spring 2027 with at least a 3.0 GPA, come from a family with adjusted gross income of $125,000 or less, and file the FAFSA; the benefit renews for up to eight semesters. President Laura Bloomberg said the program “rewards hard work by opening doors,” and the university is aiming at middle-income families who earn too much for most grant aid. The move follows similar offers at Akron, Cincinnati and Ohio State, and comes as first-time, full-time enrollment at CSU has fallen 36% since 2021, to 1,166 students. The university has not said how it will pay for the program.

Read more: Signal Cleveland

Economics & Business

A study released Wednesday by ACORN and the Vacant and Abandoned Properties Action Council found widespread vacancy and code problems in the Cleveland portfolio of Riparian Capital Partners, a Maryland-based investor that bought 172 Cuyahoga County properties in 2025, 153 of them in Cleveland. About 79% of the Cleveland homes appeared vacant, mostly on the East Side. The city has issued 101 code violation notices and 191 civil tickets and filed 19 cases in Housing Court. The homes were sold by LLCs tied to Voyager Pacific Capital Management, whose CEO pleaded guilty to wire fraud conspiracy; Riparian calls itself a fraud victim. Building and Housing Director Sally Martin O’Toole said a lawsuit against the company is “on the table,” while the study urges a public nuisance suit and interior inspections.

Phoenix-Mesa, AZ

Crime & Justice

The family of Christian Diaz, who was shot and killed by a Phoenix police officer in January, has filed a wrongful death lawsuit against Officer Jason Valenzuela and the City of Phoenix, and is demanding a jury trial. According to court records, Valenzuela shot Diaz in the head while Diaz was holding down a home-invasion suspect police had been called to arrest, and the suit alleges the officer fired within one second of issuing a command. The Maricopa County Attorney declined to charge Valenzuela and publicly called Diaz a hero for defending his family, and the police chief has apologized to the family. The suit follows a $25 million notice of claim filed a month after the shooting, and it will keep pressure on the city over use-of-force training and accountability.

Read more: KJZZ

Government & Politics

Phoenix is preparing to launch a city-run, on-demand rideshare pilot called PHX Link, with flat $2 rides in three parts of the city. Service zones cover parts of South Phoenix, North Phoenix and West Phoenix, and riders will book by app or phone and meet drivers at virtual stops. The three-year pilot is funded mostly by grants plus money from the Transportation 2050 plan, and it is meant to fill gaps in areas where fixed bus routes are thin. The city plans informational pop-up meetings October 20 to 22, and the program is slated to begin later this month.

Philadelphia, PA

Education & Government

The School District of Philadelphia said this week that it found 720 students were “inappropriately dropped” from school rosters because they lacked supporting documentation. The announcement came two days after an Inquirer report in which principals said students who had left or moved were added back to rolls before the October 1 enrollment reporting deadline to the Pennsylvania Department of Education, a count that feeds state funding. Superintendent Tony Watlington said the district takes enrollment accuracy seriously, and an attorney at the Public Interest Law Center told PhillyVoice the 720-student gap would likely have little short-term effect on state aid, which is based on multi-year averages. The district says its enrollment office will work with principals to prevent withdrawal errors; the district received about $2.5 billion from the state last year and served 198,405 students.

Read more: PhillyVoice – roster story

Justice & Higher Education

A federal appeals court has vacated a March order that required the University of Pennsylvania to give the EEOC names and contact details for Jewish studies faculty and for students and staff tied to Jewish campus groups. The EEOC had sought the information since July 2025 for an antisemitism probe and sued after Penn did not comply; five campus organizations and the ACLU of Pennsylvania intervened to block it. Because the Trump administration agreed to drop the suit while the appeal was pending, the ruling changes little for Penn in practice, but the court sent the case back with instructions to dismiss it, which means the district court’s earlier opinion can no longer serve as precedent.
