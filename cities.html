Boston, MA

Government & Politics

Boston city councilors Liz Breadon and Gabriela Coletta Zapata introduced a measure on September 17 to extend the city's condominium-conversion tenant protections beyond large apartment buildings to triple-deckers and two-family homes, using authority the state legislature granted Boston in 2024. The current ordinance, which requires notice, relocation assistance and other safeguards before a landlord can convert to condos, applies only to buildings of four or more units built before 1983, leaving out much of the city's classic triple-decker housing stock. Breadon, who represents Allston-Brighton, argued that triple-deckers make up a large share of the city's "naturally affordable" housing and that tenants in neighborhoods such as Dorchester, Mattapan, Roxbury, Hyde Park, West Roxbury, Roslindale and East Boston face displacement risk without the same protections as apartment dwellers. The proposal now moves to the council's Committee on Government Operations, chaired by Coletta Zapata, for public hearings before any ordinance goes to Mayor Michelle Wu, and supporters say it could become one piece of a broader push to modernize outdated housing laws amid the city's ongoing affordability crunch.

St. Louis, MO

Government & Politics

St. Louis' Board of Aldermen moved toward disciplining one of its longest-serving members after Alderwoman Sharon Tyus was caught on video saying she would "put a bullet in the head" of colleague Alderman Rasheen Aldridge, a remark made September 14 during a meeting of the Homer G. Phillips Resident Association about a property-lease dispute. Board President Megan Green and other members introduced a resolution demanding a public apology from Tyus and referring the matter to the Personnel and Administration Committee to consider formal censure, while St. Louis police said they were reviewing the video. Tyus made a rare in-person appearance at the board's September 18 meeting, refused to apologize, described the comment as a wartime analogy she had used years earlier, and vowed to sue the board in federal court if it proceeds, arguing censure rules cover only conduct during meetings. The clash has exposed lingering friction on a board still navigating recovery from the May 2025 tornado and disputes over the future of the former Homer G. Phillips Hospital site.

Crime & Justice

St. Louis police are investigating a monthslong wave of thefts targeting the city's churches, confirming that more than 30 houses of worship across the 4th, 5th and 6th police districts have been hit since July 1 for air-conditioning units, copper wiring, water meters and other equipment with scrap value, causing an estimated $150,000 in damage. Police Chief Robert Tracy said the department has added patrol officers and mobile trailer cameras to affected neighborhoods and is drawing on federal resources to pursue the case, though detectives have not tied the incidents to a single suspect or crew. Two people, Jerome Eichelberger, 42, and Markisha Payne, 33, were charged in September with second-degree burglary and possession of burglary tools after allegedly breaking into a north St. Louis County church, but most of the pattern remains unsolved. Several targeted congregations are still recovering from the May 2025 tornado, compounding the cost of replacing stolen equipment for churches already stretched thin.

Baltimore, MD

Government & Politics

Baltimore's decade-old municipal ID program finally has a launch date: city officials told the Baltimore Sun on September 19 that a pilot will roll out in fall 2027, with citywide implementation targeted for early 2028. The City Council first approved the free identification cards back in 2016 under a bill from then-Councilman Brandon Scott, but the effort sat largely unfunded for years until Councilwoman Odette Ramos revived it in 2022 and pushed the city to build out an actual rollout plan. The fiscal 2026 budget set aside about $1.35 million for implementation, and the city says a dedicated team is now reviewing card-production vendors and coordinating with agencies on how the IDs will be accepted. The cards are aimed at residents who often struggle to obtain traditional government identification, including immigrants, homeless and transgender residents, seniors and formerly incarcerated people, though they cannot serve as a driver's license, proof of immigration status or ID for air travel and are valid only within the city.

Crime & Justice

Baltimore's historic drop in violence kept pace through the summer: Mayor Brandon Scott announced on September 1 that the city recorded 72 homicides through August, down 18.2% from the 88 tallied over the same stretch in 2025, alongside a 5.6% decline in non-fatal shootings to 201. Robberies, burglaries and carjackings all fell too, with carjackings down 44% from a year earlier, as police credited a rise in gun seizures and gun arrests for the trend. Much of the strategy behind the numbers is the Group Violence Reduction Strategy, a partnership among the police department, the mayor's office, prosecutors and community violence interrupters that targets the small social networks researchers say drive most of the city's shootings; a University of Pennsylvania-led study published this summer credited the approach with a meaningful share of Baltimore's homicide decline. The city is on pace to extend a slide that already pushed 2025 homicides to their lowest rate in half a century, though officials and researchers caution the trend still hinges on sustained funding for the community-based side of the strategy.

Charlotte, NC

Government & Politics

The Charlotte City Council voted on September 14 to let UNC Charlotte's campus police force patrol beyond the university's official boundaries into the surrounding University City neighborhoods, giving campus officers full police powers when they respond to calls involving students, faculty or staff in the expanded zone. The agreement leans on the roughly 30,000-student campus force rather than new hiring to plug a persistent gap at the Charlotte-Mecklenburg Police Department, which has been operating roughly 200 officers below its authorized strength. UNC Charlotte officers will still concentrate routine patrols on campus itself but can now respond off-campus at CMPD's request, following a model already used between NC State and Raleigh and between UNC-Chapel Hill police and its host town. City leaders framed the deal as a practical fix for chronic staffing shortages rather than a permanent expansion of the university department's mission.

Crime & Justice

A CMPD officer shot and killed Curtis Massey on the afternoon of September 18 after Metro Division officers responding to a disturbance call in the 1300 block of LaSalle Street in the Lincoln Heights area noticed a separate group nearby they suspected was involved in an illegal transaction. Police say Massey walked away, ignored commands to stop, then ran and produced a firearm during the ensuing foot chase before an officer opened fire; officers recovered a gun at the scene and no officers were hurt. Massey's family and at least one witness dispute that account, saying he was shot in the back and never threatened officers, and have since held a vigil for him near where he died. The North Carolina State Bureau of Investigation has taken over as the lead agency on the shooting, CMPD's Internal Affairs unit is conducting a parallel review, and the officer who fired remains on paid administrative leave while the case unfolds.

Government & Politics

Denver, CO

Government & Politics

Denver Mayor Mike Johnston unveiled a $1.68 billion general fund budget proposal for 2027 on September 10, projecting roughly $1.684 billion in revenue, about 1% growth over 2026, after two straight years of painful cuts to close a prior shortfall. Unlike the "cut to the bone" budget he presented a year earlier, this plan includes no layoffs or furloughs and funds a 2% merit raise for city employees, which Johnston framed as evidence that disciplined budgeting and a strengthening local economy are paying off. It directs about $140 million to Denver Parks and Recreation, including reopening Civic Center Park and Skyline Park and finishing the Westwood Recreation Center, plus $51 million for affordable housing and $4.4 million for safer routes to school. City Council is set to hold budget hearings later this month before a vote on final adoption expected in November.

Tampa-St. Petersburg, FL

Government & Politics

St. Petersburg's mayoral race is headed to a November 3 runoff after former Florida governor Charlie Crist topped the August 18 primary with about 35% of the vote, nearly doubling incumbent Mayor Ken Welch's roughly 20%. Welch, first elected in 2021, has centered his re-election bid on the city's redevelopment plans for the Historic Gas Plant District and the future of the old Tropicana Field site, while Crist is leaning on decades of statewide name recognition and a fundraising edge; Welch has noted that nearly a third of Crist's $1.8 million campaign war chest has come from Republican donors and interests tied to investor-owned utilities. Both candidates have made hurricane preparedness and rising affordability and utility costs central themes as residents weigh who should steer the city's recovery and growth. The runoff will effectively decide who controls the next phase of one of St. Petersburg's largest ongoing redevelopment fights.

Crime & Justice

In Largo, the first-degree murder trial of Pinellas County plastic surgeon Tomasz Kosowski opened September 14 in the 2023 killing of attorney Steven Cozzi, whose body has never been found. Kosowski, who is representing himself, made the rare decision to waive his right to a jury for both the guilt and penalty phases, leaving Circuit Judge Joseph Bulone alone to decide his fate in a case where prosecutors are seeking the death penalty. Prosecutors allege Cozzi, who had represented the opposing side in a medical billing lawsuit against Kosowski, was stalked and killed after months of planning, and on September 16 showed the court what they described as a "murder kit" containing masks, syringes and weapons investigators say Kosowski assembled beforehand. The trial, expected to stretch over several weeks, has drawn heavy local coverage both for its unusual details, including testimony that Kosowski hid in an electrical closet at Cozzi's law firm days before the killing, and for the rarity of a capital case decided entirely by a judge rather than a jury.

San Diego, CA

Crime & Justice

San Diego County District Attorney Summer Stephan's office announced on September 4 that it will not file criminal charges against SDPD Officer Daniel Gold, who fatally shot 16-year-old Konoa Wilson twice in the back on the night of January 28 as the teen fled gunfire from someone else. The decision comes after the City Council quietly settled the family's wrongful-death lawsuit for $30 million in a December closed session, one of the largest police payouts in the city's history. Now a separate fight has opened over transparency: on August 19, First Amendment Coalition legal director David Loy sent a letter demanding the city release body-camera and other critical-incident recordings required under California public-records law, and the city responded that the footage remains exempt while the (now-closed) investigation and confidentiality concerns are cited. The coalition has signaled it may sue if the records are not released, meaning the case is likely to keep generating headlines well after the DA's decision closes the criminal side of the story.

Government & Public Safety

The city has launched a multi-department push to clean up downtown's Gaslamp Quarter after months of complaints about late-night disorder. Since August 28, "Operation Last Call" has added visible and undercover police patrols on Friday and Saturday nights, and the department credits the surge with 76 arrests, 102 citations and five firearms recovered so far. Public works crews have added a temporary Saturday-morning street-sweeping shift for six weeks and are repainting curbs and crosswalks and replacing damaged signage along 4th, 5th and 6th avenues between Broadway and L Street. The city also created dedicated rideshare pickup zones on 5th Avenue this summer to cut down on curbside congestion and pedestrian conflicts. Officials are coordinating the effort with the Downtown San Diego Partnership and Gaslamp businesses, who have pushed for a visible response as the district tries to protect its tourism and nightlife economy heading into the fall convention season.
