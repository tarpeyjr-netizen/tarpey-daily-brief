Austin, TX

Government & Politics

Austin could own Lions Municipal Golf Course, the West Austin course known as "Muny," for the first time in its more than 100-year history. The University of Texas has agreed to give the city the property in exchange for a large anonymous donation to the university, and the amount has not been disclosed. More than 100 people addressed the City Council on Tuesday, Nov. 17. Many asked that the course be preserved, and others were worried about the 82-acre Brackenridge Field Laboratory on the nearly 200-acre Brackenridge tract that UT would keep. Mayor Kirk Watson said the city will update the tract's development agreement with environmental rules such as setbacks from the water and density limits. Council Member Marc Duchen said the council "can't dictate to the university how it uses this land for university purposes." The council is expected to vote on Dec. 3, and the UT System Board of Regents must also approve. The deal would secure the city's hold on a course that in 1950 became the first public golf course in the South to desegregate.

Government & Politics

District 4 Council Member José "Chito" Vela has launched a campaign for Texas Senate District 14, the seat Sarah Eckhardt is leaving after she won the state comptroller race on Nov. 3. Vela said "corporations and special interests have called the shots at the Capitol for too long." He listed Medicaid expansion, housing affordability, access to women's health care and legalizing marijuana as his priorities. State law requires him to resign from the council to run, but he will keep serving until a special election is called for his seat, and KUT reports it is unclear when that will be. The field is already crowded, including Austin Community College trustee Stephanie Gharakhanian, Pflugerville Council Member Rudy Metayer and activist Katie Naranjo. For City Hall, the race opens another seat at a time when voters have just approved $295 million in parks and library bonds and three council districts head to Dec. 12 runoffs.

Richmond, VA

Business & Development

Richmond projects will receive roughly $33.5 million from this year's state Affordable and Special Needs Housing awards, announced Tuesday by Gov. Abigail Spanberger, with the Diamond District and Gilpin Court among the biggest winners. Developer Pennrose landed $9.9 million for two Diamond District projects totaling more than 160 units reserved for households earning 30% to 60% of area median income, while HRI Communities got $3 million for the "build first" phase of the Gilpin Court redevelopment, 56 units on a vacant Jackson Ward block. Other $5 million awards went to the rehab of the housing authority's 68-unit Grace Place senior apartments, SupportWorks Housing's 61-unit Gateway Park supportive housing in Highland Park, and Gateway North Apartments, being built with Virginia Union University on a former motel site at Lombardy Street and Brook Road. Southside projects near Walmsley Boulevard and Semmes Avenue also won awards. The ASNH program provides gap financing that often decides whether these deals pencil out, so the round lines up money behind several of the city's long-planned affordable housing efforts.

Crime & Justice

A Richmond judge tossed the evidence in a drug case after body-camera video showed a detective proposing a profanity-laced pretext warrant to justify holding a car. Officers in Mosby Court saw Robert Harris in a parked car and said they "maybe" saw a hand-to-hand transaction; they towed the car, and a later search warrant turned up more than 150 grams of fentanyl and cocaine. Judge Tracy Thorne-Begland ruled that police lacked probable cause to seize the vehicle before getting the warrant, suppressed the evidence, and prosecutors then dropped the charges, which they could refile. Richmond Police said they respect the ruling, maintain the officers acted in good faith, and have opened an internal investigation into possible misconduct or policy violations. Separately, the city's housing authority is replacing its private security contractors with a firm whose officers can make arrests, starting Dec. 17.

San Antonio, TX

Government

San Antonio and Bexar County are stepping up their resistance to expanded federal immigration enforcement, Texas Public Radio reports. A City Council committee advanced District 2 Councilman Jalen McKee-Rodriguez's proposal to broaden SAPD's reporting on its interactions with ICE, including joint operations, detainers and use of city resources; Mayor Gina Ortiz Jones backed it as a way to reassure residents who fear calling police, while Councilwoman Misty Spears opposed it. The full council takes it up Dec. 3, and SAPD plans to begin expanded monthly reporting Dec. 1. Meanwhile, the city's Oct. 13 lawsuit challenging plans to turn a nearly 640,000-square-foot East Side warehouse into a detention center for up to 1,500 people is due for a status update to a federal judge on Nov. 30. Bexar County Commissioners Court also widened its opposition to cover about $51 million in federal office leases at Brooks and a $382 million GardaWorld detainee-transport contract.

Business

The UT System Board of Regents has approved selling 13.59 acres at Hemisfair — the former Institute of Texan Cultures site — to the City of San Antonio for the Spurs' planned $1.3 billion downtown arena. The price was not disclosed, but City Manager Erik Walsh has said he expects roughly $60 million funded through the Midtown TIRZ, and the deed restricts the land to an arena that must be built within 10 years; closing is expected in early 2027. The arena push is reshaping the rest of Hemisfair, too: the city is taking over the ground lease for parcel D5 on South Alamo Street to explore affordable housing, a move that surprised the Hemisfair Park Area Redevelopment Corporation, whose CEO Melissa Robinson warned the lost lease income threatens its finances. Separately, the $43 million 550 Market Street project has slipped to a January 2027 start after the city declined to make expected utility upgrades.

Indianapolis, IN

Government

Indianapolis Public Schools is weighing another round of consolidation: Superintendent Aleesia Johnson presented a plan to the school board on Nov. 19 that would close six district buildings and move seven schools for the 2027-28 school year, affecting more than 3,000 students and 450 educators. The proposal is part of an effort to cut $20 million from the district budget amid declining enrollment and state property-tax changes, and IPS estimates the moves would save about $4 million before any building sales. Among the changes, Longfellow Middle School would merge into T.C. Howe Middle School, and Center for Inquiry School 2 students would combine with Charity Dye School 27. Parents told Mirror Indy the timeline leaves little room to weigh alternatives, since first-round enrollment applications are due Dec. 18 and the board isn't expected to vote until Dec. 8. The urgency is partly structural: the new Indianapolis Public Education Corp. is expected to take control of public school buildings in 2028, narrowing the window for IPS to act.

Nashville, TN

Government & Politics

The Tennessee Supreme Court has upheld the 2023 state law capping Nashville's Metro Council at 20 members, half its current 40 seats, ending a legal fight Metro had waged for more than three years. The court rejected Metro's argument under the state constitution's Exemption Clause and dismissed its Local Legislation Clause claim as moot after lawmakers amended the law, but it gave little direction on how or when the cut should happen. Mayor Freddie O'Connell said Nashville's council size “was set by our community” and that the charter should only be changed by its voters, while Vice Mayor Angie Henderson and Charter Revision Committee chair Clay Capp called it a “somber day for democracy” and said the council would convene special meetings to work through the ruling. At-large Councilmember Zulfat Suara called the process “chaotic,” with open questions about how district lines will be redrawn, whether at-large seats survive, and how much farther residents may be from their representatives. The answers will shape the 2027 Metro elections and how closely Nashvillians are represented in their local government.

Infrastructure & Public Safety

Crews from the Nashville Department of Transportation began installing retractable bollards on Lower Broadway on Thursday, November 19, the first step in a pilot meant to make the city's busiest entertainment strip safer for pedestrians. Nine bollards are going in across the roadway on the east side of the Broadway and Rep. John Lewis Way intersection, and Broadway is closed east of John Lewis Way to 4th Avenue through December 4, weather permitting. The project follows a crash a few months ago in which a vehicle drove into a crowd on Broadway, killing one person and injuring three. NDOT says traffic control and uniformed police officers will be on site during construction, and earlier reporting said the program could expand down Broadway toward First Avenue if the pilot succeeds — part of the city’s broader Lower Broadway pedestrian improvements effort.

Boston, MA

Business

The Fish Pier building in South Boston that housed the No Name Restaurant for 102 years will get a second life as a marine robotics and tech facility run by MassRobotics, Massport announced Thursday, November 19. The startup incubator, whose 12 Channel St. headquarters a ten-minute walk away already houses about 100 startups, was chosen from three interested organizations. Massport CEO Rich Davey framed the move as part of the authority's Blue Edge initiative to keep and attract “blue-tech” companies on the waterfront, and MassRobotics executive director Tom Ryden said direct harbor access is the draw, since getting on the water is otherwise hard for underwater-robotics firms. Massport is renovating the two-story building, which has sat empty since the family-run restaurant closed in bankruptcy in 2019, and the hub is expected to open in early 2028.

Collegiate Sports

The 141st edition of The Game came to Fenway Park on Saturday, and Yale beat Harvard 20–17 on a 2-yard Dante Reno touchdown pass to Graham Smith with 3:37 left, then sealed it with Billy Eastep’s interception of Harvard’s final throw. Harvard opened the scoring on Damien Henderson’s 28-yard interception return and got a 115-yard day from DJ Gordon, but Yale picked off Charlie Smith three times. The loss was bittersweet for Andy Aurich’s Crimson (8–2, 5–2 Ivy), who still won the Ivy League title outright thanks to results elsewhere and earned an automatic NCAA playoff berth. Yale took the Big Three title and its fifth straight win in the rivalry, its longest run against Harvard since 1907. Across town the same day, Boston College closed its home schedule with an ACC win over Syracuse.

St. Louis, MO

Government

The St. Louis Board of Aldermen voted 10-2 on Thursday to send Mayor Cara Spencer a bill that would permanently fund the city's Code Blue winter outreach with marijuana tax revenue. Spencer created Code Blue by executive order last year and paid for it with $3 million in Rams settlement money. Last winter the protocol was activated more than four dozen times, adding shelter beds, transportation and outreach whenever temperatures fell below 32 degrees. Lead sponsor Alderwoman Alisha Sonnier (7th Ward) said the goal is to give city departments "the predictability they need" to plan early. City officials expect the 3% tax on recreational marijuana sales to raise about $1.4 million this fiscal year, and the city will also spend $2 million in general revenue on Code Blue this winter.

Crime & Justice

St. Louis Police Chief Robert Tracy signed a new contract Wednesday that keeps his $295,000 salary and his $110,000 raise, even though Mayor Spencer and the police union have both criticized the raise. The Board of Police Commissioners approved the deal in a closed session. It is largely the same as the contract the board approved in October, but it runs about two years shorter and drops a $24,500 retirement contribution. The board says it had to raise Tracy's pay to counter outside job offers. Spencer says she doesn't believe that, and she argues the money comes "at the expense of being able to spend that on other police officers." The Missouri Attorney General is still investigating whether the hastily called October meetings violated the state's Sunshine Law.

Baltimore, MD

Business

Mayor Brandon Scott's administration has reached a community benefits deal with Harborplace developer MCB Real Estate that is tied to a tax break that could abate $366 million over 25 years. Under the deal, MCB would pledge $10 million to the Baltimore Civic Fund or a similar third party and set up a $5 million equity fund so residents can invest in the project. It also sets hiring goals: city residents would work 35% of construction hours and fill 55% of post-construction jobs, 10% of new apartments would carry affordable rents, and 50 YouthWorks participants would be hired each year for a decade. The Board of Estimates is expected to vote on the tax break, the ground lease and the profit-sharing terms at a special hearing on Dec. 2. The financing is still uncertain. Private construction costs have climbed from an estimated $500 million to $820 million, MCB has not yet secured financing, and demolition of the old pavilions has slipped to early 2027.

Read more: Baltimore Banner: Harborplace tax deal

Government

Scott announced Thursday that Baltimore's long-delayed municipal ID, the "410 Card," is expected to launch early next year. City officials authorized the program roughly a decade ago. The card will be free to all residents, and every city agency, including law enforcement, will accept it. Scott also signed a bill that expands the documents residents can use to apply, including school and social services records, and adds privacy protections for those documents. Council member Odette Ramos has pushed the issue since 2022. Supporters say the card will help people without other photo ID, such as residents experiencing homelessness and immigrants without passports, get services like library cards and rental assistance.
