History & Geography

American History · Pony Express 1860

Starting in April 1860, Pony Express riders carried mail roughly 1,900 miles between St. Joseph, Missouri, and Sacramento in about ten days, swapping horses at relay stations every ten to fifteen miles. The service lasted only about eighteen months: the transcontinental telegraph was completed on October 24, 1861, and the Express announced its end two days later. It lost its backers heavily, yet it became one of the most mythologized enterprises of the American West.

World History · The Rosetta Stone

French soldiers rebuilding a fort near Rashid (Rosetta) in 1799 uncovered a slab carrying a 196 BC priestly decree for Ptolemy V in three scripts: hieroglyphic, Demotic and Ancient Greek. It passed to the British under the 1801 Capitulation of Alexandria and has been in the British Museum since 1802. Thomas Young made early progress, but Jean-François Champollion’s 1822 breakthrough came from realizing that hieroglyphs were not purely symbolic: they mixed sound signs with picture signs.

Geography · The Zanclean Flood

About 5.96 million years ago the Mediterranean was cut off from the Atlantic and largely evaporated, leaving salt deposits more than a kilometer thick in places. Around 5.33 million years ago the Atlantic broke through at Gibraltar. A 2009 study in Nature estimated that most of the basin may have refilled in under two years, with sea level at the peak rising as much as ten meters a day.

Japan · Satsuma Rebellion 1877

Saigō Takamori, one of the leaders of the Meiji Restoration, led disaffected Satsuma samurai against the government he had helped create, after reforms ended samurai stipends and banned wearing swords in public. A conscript army of commoners with modern rifles and artillery crushed the uprising, and Saigō died at Shiroyama in September 1877. Within twelve years the state that defeated him had pardoned him posthumously, and his statue now stands in Tokyo’s Ueno Park.

Ireland · Newgrange Solstice Light

The passage tomb at Newgrange in the Boyne Valley was built around 3200 BC, older than Stonehenge’s stone circle and the pyramids of Giza. A “roof-box” above the entrance admits a beam of sunrise that travels the roughly 19-meter passage and lights the inner chamber for about seventeen minutes around the winter solstice. The alignment was confirmed only in 1967, when archaeologist Michael O’Kelly stood in the chamber at dawn on December 21 and watched it happen.

India · Rani ki Vav Stepwell

Queen Udayamati, wife of the Chaulukya king Bhima I, commissioned this stepwell at Patan, Gujarat, in the eleventh century, descending seven levels to the water and lined with more than 500 principal sculptures and over a thousand minor ones. Floods of the Saraswati River later silted it over completely; in the 1890s surveyors could see only the well shaft and a few pillars. That burial is why the carvings survived so well. Excavated from the 1940s and restored in the 1980s, it became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2014 and appears on the ₹100 note.

South America · Machu Picchu 1911

Built around 1450, generally attributed to the Inca emperor Pachacuti as a royal estate, Machu Picchu was abandoned around the time of the Spanish conquest and never found by the Spanish. Hiram Bingham reached it on July 24, 1911, guided by local farmer Melchor Arteaga, and found families already farming its terraces. The site was “lost” only to outsiders.

Durham NC · Duke Chapel

The cornerstone was laid on October 22, 1930. The chapel was first used for Commencement in 1932 while still under construction and formally dedicated on June 2, 1935. Its 210-foot tower and walls are built from stone quarried near Hillsborough, trimmed with Indiana limestone. The design came from Julian Abele, the Black chief designer at Philadelphia architect Horace Trumbauer’s firm, whose authorship of much of West Campus went largely uncredited for decades. Duke renamed its main quad Abele Quad in 2016.

Business & Society

Economics · Goodhart’s Law

Economist Charles Goodhart observed in 1975 that a statistical relationship tends to break down once policymakers start targeting it. Anthropologist Marilyn Strathern later gave it the familiar form: “When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.” A classic case comes from French colonial Hanoi in 1902, where officials paid a bounty per rat tail and residents responded by cutting off tails, releasing the rats to breed, and eventually farming them.

Finance · Le Maire’s Short 1609

Isaac Le Maire, a founding investor and former director of the Dutch East India Company, organized a syndicate in 1609 to sell VOC shares for future delivery, betting that the price would fall. The campaign prompted Amsterdam authorities in 1610 to ban selling shares one did not own, the first known short-selling ban. Le Maire’s complaints about the company’s governance are also often cited as the first recorded act of shareholder activism.

Personal Finance · The 4% Rule

In 1994 planner William Bengen tested U.S. market history and found that a retiree who withdrew 4% of a balanced portfolio in year one, then raised the dollar amount with inflation, never ran out of money in any 30-year period studied; the 1998 Trinity Study reached a similar result. The rule describes a worst case, not a typical one. In most historical periods retirees following it finished with more than they started, and Bengen himself has since argued a higher starting rate is supportable.

Management · The Hawthorne Effect

At Western Electric’s Hawthorne Works in Cicero, Illinois, researchers in the 1920s found that worker output rose under brighter lighting, and also in a control group, and sometimes even when lights were dimmed. Sociologist Henry Landsberger named the “Hawthorne effect” in 1958: people change behavior because they know they are being studied. A 2011 reanalysis of the surviving lighting data by Steven Levitt and John List found the original evidence considerably weaker than the legend.

Constitutional Law · Miranda v. Arizona

In a 5–4 decision in 1966, the Supreme Court under Chief Justice Earl Warren held that statements from custodial interrogation are inadmissible unless the suspect has first been warned of the right to remain silent and the right to a lawyer. Ernesto Miranda himself won the case but lost his freedom anyway: retried without the confession, he was convicted again in 1967.

Sports

Track and Field · Abebe Bikila 1960

The Ethiopian Imperial Guard soldier ran the Rome Olympic marathon barefoot and won in 2:15:16, a world best, becoming the first Black African Olympic champion; the race finished at night beneath the Arch of Constantine. Four years later in Tokyo, about six weeks after an appendectomy, he won again in shoes, the first person to win the Olympic marathon twice.

Softball · Pitching Distance Physics

In women’s fastpitch the pitching rubber is 43 feet from home plate, against 60 feet 6 inches in Major League Baseball, and pitchers release the ball several feet in front of it. A 70 mph softball pitch therefore gives the hitter about as little time to react as a baseball fastball in the high 90s, and the underhand delivery lets the ball rise through the zone instead of dropping, which baseball hitters find unfamiliar.

Mayo GAA · The 1951 Curse Legend

Mayo won back-to-back All-Ireland football titles in 1950 and 1951. A popular story holds that the team, riding home in triumph, failed to show respect to a passing funeral, and a priest cursed them never to win again while any of that team lived; there is no contemporary record of the incident. Final defeats in 1989, 1996, 1997, 2004, 2006, 2012, 2013, 2016, 2017, 2020 and 2021 have kept it one of the best-known stories in Gaelic games.

Athletics · The Quiet Eye

Joan Vickers at the University of Calgary found that elite performers hold a final, steady gaze on their target for longer than near-elite ones just before they move: a putter on the back of the ball, a free-throw shooter on the rim. This “quiet eye” period can be trained, and studies have shown quiet-eye training improving free-throw and putting accuracy under pressure. Better eyesight doesn’t produce it; the difference is in how long and how calmly the athlete fixes on one point.

Religion & Philosophy

Catholicism · Gregorian Calendar 1582

To keep Easter aligned with the spring equinox, Pope Gregory XIII’s bull Inter gravissimas dropped ten days: in Catholic states, Thursday, October 4, 1582, was followed by Friday, October 15. A new rule made century years leap years only when divisible by 400. Protestant Britain and its colonies waited until 1752, by which time eleven days had to go, and Russia until 1918, which is why the October Revolution is commemorated in November.

World Religion · Ise Shrine Rebuilding

Every twenty years, the main sanctuaries of Ise Grand Shrine, Shinto’s holiest site, are rebuilt from fresh hinoki cypress on an adjacent plot and the deity is ritually transferred, a ceremony called Shikinen Sengū. The tradition is traced to the late seventh century, with a long interruption during Japan’s civil wars; the 62nd rebuilding took place in 2013 and the next is due in 2033. The buildings are never more than twenty years old, yet the design is roughly 1,300 years old.
