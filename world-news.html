Monday, September 28, 2026 · Asia

South Korea

Asia · population ~52M

Politics & Government

President Lee Jae Myung's approval rating rebounded for a second straight week, climbing to 37.9 percent after a bruising stretch, even as his government juggles a widening set of controversies. Seoul's presidential office demanded an apology from Kyiv after accusing Ukraine of leaking details of a North Korean POW transfer to the South, a claim Ukrainian officials have flatly denied. At home, confirmation hearings for justice minister nominee Kim Seung-won turned into a shouting match of accusations and insults over lobbying allegations, deepening opposition pressure on the cabinet. Lee also returns to a packed agenda that includes a Supreme Court appointment dispute alongside the DMZ fallout described below.

Economy & Business

South Korea's exports surged 78.3 percent in the first 20 days of September to a record $71.4 billion, an AI-driven boom that pushed semiconductor shipments to an all-time high of $34.1 billion, nearly 48 percent of total exports, with shipments to China and the United States both jumping sharply. Riding that momentum, President Lee is set to chair a meeting with Samsung and SK executives this week to check progress on the government's "three megaprojects" drive spanning semiconductors, physical AI and AI data centers, a plan that envisions roughly 4,755 trillion won in combined investment according to Seoul Economic Daily.

Military & Security

Three South Korean soldiers, including a battalion commander, were wounded on September 21 when a suspected landmine detonated inside the DMZ during a route-clearing operation, with investigators treating a North Korean-planted mine as the leading theory. The response has become a political flashpoint: opposition lawmakers accused the Lee administration of a "deliberately delayed" on-site probe that risked letting rain destroy key evidence, while military officials countered that residual blast risk made it unsafe to send inspection teams in sooner, with South Korean and UN Command personnel finally entering the site five days after the blast.

Society & Culture

South Koreans marked the three-day Chuseok harvest holiday from September 24 to 26, with families packing highways and KTX trains to reach ancestral hometowns while hospitals and pharmacies ran reduced holiday schedules searchable via the national E-Gen emergency service. In the workplace, unionized staff at Naver's metaverse affiliate Naver Z staged a full-day walkout on September 23, with roughly 70 percent of members joining to protest a wage freeze, per Digital Today, reflecting broader labor friction in Korea's tech sector.

Tourism

South Korea welcomed a record 15.05 million foreign visitors in the first eight months of 2026, up 21.6 percent year-on-year, with the tourism ministry eyeing October as the peak arrivals month. The autumn festival calendar is now in full swing, headlined by the Andong International Mask Dance Festival running September 25 to October 4 and the sprawling 56-day Seoul Autumn Festival spanning more than 200 venues across the capital.

Site to visit: Hahoe Folk Village, a UNESCO World Heritage-listed riverside settlement near Andong, preserves centuries-old tile-roofed and thatched hanok houses still inhabited by descendants of the Ryu clan and is the ancestral home of Korea's iconic hahoe talchum mask dance. It pairs naturally with the ongoing Andong Mask Dance Festival; entry costs about 5,000 won including a shuttle bus, and it's best reached by a roughly hour-long bus ride from Andong, itself about three hours from Seoul, with early morning or autumn weekday visits recommended to avoid festival crowds.

Science & Technology

Seoul is hosting AI4Sci Korea 2026, the country's first international "AI for Science" academic conference, running September 27 to October 1 at Yongsan-gu's Dragon City Hotel with roughly 600 researchers from 19 countries presenting about 220 papers, according to Digital Today. The gathering coincides with a broader government push into strategic technologies, including small modular reactors and fusion, and comes ahead of the planned October 7 launch of Korea's fifth Nuri rocket carrying a record 15 satellites, among them the country's first orbital satellite constellation.

🔥 Trending
HANRORO
Indie music · Seoul (Korea University scene)

HANRORO, a 26-year-old singer-songwriter and Korean literature major who writes and releases her own material outside the K-pop system, has become one of Korea's most talked-about grassroots music stories of the year. She built her following through self-penned tracks like "Landing in Love," which climbed to No. 2 on Melon's weekly chart, and by simultaneously dominating streaming charts and bestseller lists as an unsigned indie act, a rare feat that saw her three EPs sweep the top of Yes24's album sales chart. Momentum has only accelerated this month: she sold out her first overseas headline show in Tokyo on September 8 and, per Sports Khan, is now in the middle of a packed autumn university festival circuit across Korean campuses, culminating in an Inha University appearance on the 17th.

Sunday, September 27, 2026 · Europe

Germany

Europe · population ~84M

Politics & Government

Germany's governing coalition took a fresh political hit this week after twin state elections on September 20 dealt Chancellor Friedrich Merz's CDU its worst-ever results in Mecklenburg-Vorpommern and Berlin, with the far-right Alternative for Germany (AfD) taking roughly 38% of the vote to shut the CDU out of that state's parliament entirely for the first time in the party's history, while Berlin's vote swung sharply toward the Left party. Merz called the results a "disaster" but vowed his government would press ahead with its economic and pension reform agenda, rejecting internal party calls to change course, according to PBS NewsHour. The defeats followed the AfD's landslide win two weeks earlier in Saxony-Anhalt, where it captured 43.8% of the vote, deepening questions inside the CDU/CSU-SPD coalition about Merz's authority barely a year and a half into his chancellorship.

Economy & Business

Germany's leading economic institutes sharply raised their joint 2026 growth forecast to 1.3%, up from just 0.6% predicted in spring, citing resilient exports and demand tied to the global AI data-center boom, per Reuters. That upgrade was overshadowed by turmoil in the auto industry: Volkswagen issued its second profit warning of the year, slashing 2026 margin guidance to as little as 1% and confirming plans to cut roughly 100,000 jobs, which sent VW and Porsche shares sliding. In response, tens of thousands of autoworkers from VW, BMW, Mercedes-Benz, Audi and major suppliers staged protests at more than 280 events nationwide on September 21, with the IG Metall union demanding the Merz government cut energy costs and shield domestic manufacturing from low-cost Asian competition.

Military & Security

The Bundeswehr ran its largest homeland-defence drill of the year, Red Storm Charlie, in Hamburg from September 24-26, deploying around 500 soldiers alongside some 60 civilian and municipal partners — including police, the fire brigade, the Hamburg Port Authority and Airbus — to rehearse rushing troops and equipment to NATO's eastern flank under a simulated Baltic crisis with Russia, complete with drills for drone attacks and sabotage on military convoys. The exercise drew a counter-demonstration from the "No NATO Harbor" alliance and unfolded amid warnings from Bundeswehr chief Carsten Breuer that Russia is testing NATO's responses daily through hybrid attacks and that Germany must be fully war-ready by 2029. It also follows Berlin's move to unveil a nationwide anti-sabotage "shield" of rapid-response counter-drone units for cities and critical infrastructure after blaming Russia for a foiled drone-bomb plot at Leipzig/Halle Airport.

Society & Culture

Germany saw two major protest waves in the past week. On September 26, roughly 175,000 people marched through 15 cities including Berlin, Frankfurt and Nuremberg under union banners, with the DGB and Verdi warning Chancellor Merz's coalition against cutting early retirement and social benefits as part of its reform push, a mobilization backed by European trade federations. A day earlier, more than 45,000 students staged strikes in roughly 100 German cities against the possible return of compulsory military service, waving banners reading "We won't take part in your wars" at rallies including in Hamburg. Together the demonstrations reflect rising public anxiety over the government's simultaneous push to rearm and to trim the welfare state.

Tourism

Germany's inbound tourism sector kept growing through the first half of 2026, logging 36.4 million international overnight stays (up 0.5% year-on-year) led by Munich, Berlin and Frankfurt, with Gulf visitors alone spending roughly €3 billion in the country. This month the German National Tourist Board formalized a digital booking partnership with GetYourGuide to make attractions and local experiences easier to discover online. Meanwhile Munich's Oktoberfest is fueling Europe's biggest September travel surge, drawing crowds from Italy, the US and India, even as regions like Kaiserslautern roll out autumn wine festivals, cycling routes and hiking events.

Site to visit: Neuschwanstein Castle, King Ludwig II's 19th-century fairy-tale retreat in the Bavarian Alps near Füssen, was inscribed in 2025 as part of a new UNESCO World Heritage ensemble covering the king's palaces and remains Germany's most-photographed landmark, drawing about 1.4 million visitors a year. Adult entry costs €14.50 plus a small online booking fee and a timed-entry reservation is essential; the castle is best reached by train to Füssen followed by a short bus ride or uphill walk, with spring and early autumn offering noticeably thinner crowds than peak summer.

Science & Technology

Planetary scientists led by Frank Postberg at Freie Universität Berlin published two studies in Science Advances on September 25 showing that Saturn's icy moon Enceladus may be even more hospitable to microbial life than previously thought, with new analysis of Cassini spacecraft data suggesting the chemistry of its hidden subsurface ocean is easier to characterize than assumed and could support methane-producing microorganisms even under extreme carbon-dioxide limitation. The findings, also covered by EurekAlert, build on a newly launched DFG-funded Collaborative Research Center on planetary habitability that Postberg co-leads, reinforcing Berlin's role in the international search for extraterrestrial life.

Music (indie urban pop/rap) · Bonn, North Rhine-Westphalia

Bonn-born singer Kauta (Kaouthar Boulfazat) has become one of Germany's fastest-rising grassroots music stories, going from a fourth-place finish on the talent show Rap La Rue in 2024 to a homegrown streaming phenomenon built almost entirely on organic TikTok and Instagram momentum rather than label marketing. Singing in German, French and Arabic in a fusion of urban pop with Moroccan-influenced sounds that she describes as personal identity work, Kauta launched her first headline tour, "Tagebuch," in early 2026 and continues selling out shows, as detailed in Eventim's music magazine. Critics increasingly cite her rise as emblematic of a broader wave of bilingual, diaspora-rooted artists reshaping German pop from the ground up.

Saturday, September 26, 2026 · Asia

Iraq

Asia · population ~43M

Politics & Government

Five months into Prime Minister Ali al-Zaidi's term, Iraq's government still lacks a full cabinet, with several ministerial posts left vacant by disputes inside the ruling Shia coalition even as questions mount over how the new administration will handle the country's overlapping crises. Al-Zaidi, a 41-year-old businessman who took office in May as the youngest prime minister in Iraq's history, has framed his government's mission as making Iraq "a balanced country regionally and internationally," a balancing act tested almost daily by Washington's insistence that he rein in Iran-aligned armed factions. That pressure has already forced one retreat: the government's own deadline to disarm or absorb the Popular Mobilisation Forces into the regular military, originally due this month, has slipped to June 2027, according to analysts tracking the country's key political fault lines this year.

Economy & Business

Baghdad enters the autumn with its public finances under real strain: the IMF is now projecting a 6.8% contraction in real GDP for 2026, and the government still has not managed to pass a budget for either 2025 or 2026 amid disputes over how to forecast oil prices and exports. Facing that squeeze, the Cabinet moved on 1 September to lift fuel subsidies for government institutions and industry, while keeping cheaper rates in place for the petrol, gas oil, kerosene and LPG sold directly to citizens. Prime Minister al-Zaidi took a different message to New York during UN General Assembly week, unveiling an "Invest with Iraq" pitch for the country's economic future and meeting separately with executives from ExxonMobil and HKN Energy to press them to speed up stalled oilfield work.

Military & Security

The clearest test of Iraqi sovereignty this year lands at the end of the month: al-Zaidi has repeatedly called 30 September a "fixed and final" deadline for the US-led anti-ISIL coalition to complete its withdrawal, rejecting a Washington proposal to keep some troops on beyond that date. The United States is signalling it is treating that date as real: American officials signed a deal on 25 September to hand over a diplomatic support hub at Baghdad International Airport to the Iraqi government. Analysts warn the pullout carries real risk, since it strips away Patriot and C-RAM air-defence coverage that has helped intercept Iranian missile and drone traffic passing through Iraqi airspace toward Israel, Jordan and the Gulf. The stakes were underlined on 12 September when a drone strike forced the closure of Saudi Arabia's East-West oil pipeline; Riyadh blamed Iran-aligned Popular Mobilisation Forces factions, and Iraqi security forces responded by seizing a drone-launching platform from one such group.

Society & Culture

Iraq is leaning into a softer cultural profile even as security dominates the headlines: Baghdad has been named the Capital of Islamic Culture for 2026, with the culture ministry coordinating across government to ready the capital for a year of official events. Away from that official calendar, a grassroots cultural scene is finding its own voice: Inter Press Service profiled Sulaymaniyah visual artist Tara Abdullah this month on how public art has become a way to confront gender-based violence and censorship in Iraqi Kurdistan. The government also imposed a run of internet shutdowns from 1 to 12 September to prevent cheating during the second round of national school exams, a now-routine measure that digital-rights monitors continue to flag as disruptive to ordinary life.

Tourism

Iraq's tourism ambitions keep climbing even as much of the world still associates the country chiefly with conflict: the Tourism Authority has set out a ten-year strategy aiming to draw 10 million visitors by 2035, backed by an e-visa system that now grants visa-on-arrival to dozens of nationalities, according to a current guide to travelling in the country. The push is starting to show up in outbound numbers too: Iraqi travellers overtook seven other Arab nations, including Saudi Arabia and Egypt, to lead Arab tourism to Türkiye in April, with more than 60,000 visits in a single month. Officials say Britain and several European governments have eased Iraq's security travel classification this year, and the authority is piloting AI-based tools to manage visitor flows at heritage sites.

Site to visit: The ruined city of Babylon, once the capital of Nebuchadnezzar II's empire and the source of biblical legend as home to the Hanging Gardens and the Tower of Babel, is a UNESCO World Heritage Site that mixes genuine Neo-Babylonian brickwork with a controversial Saddam-era reconstruction. Entry costs roughly 25,000 Iraqi dinars (about $19) per person payable at the gate, and the ruins sit an easy day trip about 90 minutes south of Baghdad, best visited in the cooler months from November to March before summer heat sets in.

Science & Technology

Iraq is trying to turn worsening summer blackouts into a genuine energy pivot: the electricity ministry says it plans to add 10 gigawatts of solar generation to the grid, part of a wider push toward roughly 12 gigawatts of renewable capacity by 2030 that officials estimate could cover a fifth of projected demand, with contracts already signed involving TotalEnergies, QatarEnergy, ACWA Power and Masdar. On the digital side, Baghdad and Erbil have both broken into the world's top 1,000 startup ecosystem cities, and the Iraq International Telecommunications & Technology Exhibition opens at Baghdad's international fairground on 30 September, gathering regulators, operators and international vendors for its fifth telecommunications forum. Iranian and Iraqi officials also used a 25 September meeting to agree on deeper cooperation on joint tech research, part of Tehran's continuing push to keep scientific ties close even as Baghdad's Western partnerships expand elsewhere.

🔥 Trending
Lezzoo
Consumer tech / on-demand super app · Erbil, Kurdistan Region

Lezzoo is the clearest homegrown tech success story to come out of Iraqi Kurdistan, an on-demand delivery and services "super app" founded in Erbil in 2017 by local entrepreneur Yadgar Merani with little outside backing to speak of at the start, a story Kurdistan Chronicle has called a Kurdistani entrepreneurial success story. What began as food delivery for a handful of Erbil restaurants has organically grown into Kurdistan and Iraq's only Y Combinator-backed startup, now serving more than a million customers and 2,500-plus vendors and moving upwards of $200 million a year in orders across groceries, pharmacy and general goods. The app keeps finding new ways to keep users engaged without a paid-marketing budget behind it, most recently adding a built-in mini-game called Jezar that customers can play while waiting for deliveries to arrive, a small but telling sign of how much of Lezzoo's growth still runs on everyday word-of-mouth loyalty rather than advertising spend.

Friday, September 25, 2026 · Africa

Ethiopia

Africa · population ~128M

Politics & Government

Ethiopia's political calendar and its unravelling wartime peace collided this week. The newly elected House of Peoples' Representatives, where Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed's Prosperity Party holds a commanding 438 of 501 seats after June's election, is due to convene on 5 October to open a new five-year term and formally reappoint Abiy. But the diplomatic scaffolding around that mandate is fraying: African Union Commission chairperson Mahmoud Ali Youssouf called for "immediate de-escalation" after Tigrayan forces seized federal airports and effectively shattered the November 2022 Pretoria peace agreement, while US Ambassador Ervin Massinga said Washington was "disappointed" in the TPLF's "unwinnable" offensive and warned of further sanctions on any leaders who widen the war. Abiy adviser Getachew Reda has accused Tigrayan forces of launching an "all-out offensive," a charge Tigray's regional government rejects, saying its forces acted defensively after federal air and artillery strikes and accusing Addis Ababa of stalling on the return of displaced people and disputed territories promised under Pretoria. The dispute over blame underscores how little of the original peace deal's hardest provisions were ever actually implemented, four years on.

Economy & Business

Ethiopia's post-float economy keeps growing briskly but not cheaply: growth is still projected near 7.8% for 2025/26, among the fastest on the continent, even as annual inflation held near 15% in August, easing only slightly from July and still far above the central bank's comfort zone. The birr keeps sliding under the IMF-backed floating-exchange regime, and the National Bank of Ethiopia ran another $125 million special foreign-exchange auction on 23 September, its fourth such intervention since mid-August, as the official rate pushed past 161 to the dollar. The currency reform is nonetheless unlocking debt relief: official creditors approved a preliminary deal on Ethiopia's defaulted $1 billion Eurobond in August, following an earlier $8.4 billion restructuring with the Official Creditor Committee, moves the IMF credits with keeping its $3.4 billion reform programme on track.

Military & Security

Ethiopia's fragile Tigray peace effectively collapsed this week as full-scale fighting resumed for the first time since 2022. Tigray Defence Forces loyal to the TPLF seized the airports at Mekelle, Aksum and Shire from federal police overnight on 22–23 September, prompting Ethiopian Airlines to suspend flights to all three cities, while a newly declared "Alliance for Survival," uniting the TPLF with the Amhara Fano National Movement and several other armed groups, opened fighting on multiple fronts, including the Kobo-Tumuga line on the Tigray-Amhara border and the Tonsa line toward Afar. Each side blames the other for the escalation, with Tigray's regional government describing a "defensive war" against a federal ground offensive it says followed weeks of stalled talks. The UN Secretary-General voiced concern over the "rapidly deteriorating" situation, warning of a repeat of the 2020–22 war that killed an estimated 600,000 people and left roughly a million people still displaced.

Society & Culture

Even as war fears spread, Ethiopia is preparing for one of its most important religious festivals: Meskel, the UNESCO-listed commemoration of the finding of the True Cross, falls on 27 September, with the Demera bonfire lit in Addis Ababa's Meskel Square on the evening of the 26th traditionally drawing hundreds of thousands of worshippers in white netela robes. The timing is bittersweet this year — with fighting resuming along the Tigray-Amhara border barely a week before the holiday, communities in the contested north face celebrating, or being unable to celebrate, amid fresh displacement layered on top of roughly a million people still uprooted from the earlier war. Elsewhere, preparations are proceeding much as normal across the country's Orthodox Christian heartland, with priests, choirs and the ceremonial planting of yellow Meskel daisies (Adey Abeba) forming the backdrop to what UNESCO recognises as an element of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

Tourism

Ethiopia's tourism recovery kept outperforming the regional average this year, with international arrivals up 15% in 2025 according to UN Tourism data, nearly double the wider African market's 8% growth, helped by expanding Ethiopian Airlines routes. The government is now leaning into the shoulder season, promoting "breathtaking autumn" itineraries through the Simien Mountains and the northern historic circuit now that the June-September rains have left the highlands green, even as safety guidance continues to steer visitors toward well-trodden routes and licensed guides, away from the fighting that has resumed in the north.

Site to visit: The Rock-Hewn Churches of Lalibela, a UNESCO World Heritage Site of eleven monolithic churches carved downward into volcanic bedrock in the 12th and 13th centuries, remain Ethiopia's signature pilgrimage and tourist destination, often called a "New Jerusalem" built for Ethiopian Christians who could not reach the real one. A multi-day pass covering all the churches costs roughly $50, and the dry, cooler months from October to February, just beginning, are considered the best time to visit, reached by a roughly 1.5-hour flight north from Addis Ababa.

Science & Technology

Ethiopia's flagship infrastructure project kept outperforming expectations: the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam generated about 17% more electricity than its design forecast in its first year of commercial operation, the GERD International Collaborative Consortium said in a review timed to the dam's first anniversary this month, with the mega-dam supplying just over half of the record 35,671 gigawatt-hours Ethiopia generated in the 2025/26 fiscal year. The consortium is now pushing for faster transmission-grid investment so the extra output reaches Ethiopian households and factories rather than only export markets, even as the dam remains a flashpoint with downstream Egypt over Nile water-sharing rules. On the research side, the state-run Ethiopian Agricultural Research Institute has distributed more than 50 newly bred, higher-yielding and disease-resistant coffee varieties to farmers, part of a push behind the record $3 billion the country earned from coffee exports in the fiscal year just closed.

🔥 Trending
Addis AI
Consumer tech / voice-first AI assistant · Addis Ababa

Addis AI is a homegrown voice assistant built to let Ethiopians speak with a chatbot in Amharic and Afaan Oromo rather than type on a keyboard poorly suited to Ethiopic script, the brainchild of founder Biniyam Daniel after he noticed skilled software candidates who could code fluently but struggled to explain their work in English during interviews. Shega reported that the app crossed 10,000 users within two weeks of launch, far outpacing the team's own five-thousand-user target, and has since grown past 100,000 users generating 6,000 to 10,000 daily voice interactions. None of that growth came through advertising: word of mouth among Amharic- and Afaan Oromo-speaking users, frustrated by how little of the digital world speaks back to them in their own language, has carried the app from a small Addis Ababa team's side project to one of the country's most-discussed consumer tech launches this year.

Thursday, September 24, 2026 · South America

Chile

South America · population ~19M

Politics & Government

President José Antonio Kast used his first address to the UN General Assembly on 22 September to declare "Chile is back," touting an 86% drop in irregular border crossings, an 11.5% fall in homicides and a corporate tax cut to 23% while pushing for a UN Security Council overhaul and predicting that Latin America's conservative wave could make "the next decade… ours." The speech landed awkwardly alongside fallout at home: former president Michelle Bachelet withdrew her bid to lead the United Nations days earlier after a Security Council straw poll left her with just one supportive vote, even as Kast joined Washington's new "Shield of the Americas" security coalition as an observer alongside a newly admitted Peru. Six months into his term, Kast's own numbers tell a harder story than his UN remarks suggested: Cadem's tracking poll put his approval at just 34% against 61% disapproval, with 62% of respondents saying his government has performed worse than they expected. His government's one clear legislative win, a narrowly passed economic "mega-reform," stands in contrast to a hardline public-security bill that remains stalled in Congress amid criticism it curtails civil liberties.

Economy & Business

Chile's economy is struggling to match the copper boom underpinning it: GDP contracted 0.2% year-on-year in the second quarter, dragged down by a 6.4% slump in mining output as copper production fell 10.5%, even as the government raised its price forecast for the metal to a record $5.90 a pound. The pain is showing up in jobs: unemployment reached 9.5% in the May-July period, its highest level in five years, with informal employment at 26.5%, while the monthly Imacec activity index shrank 1.5% in July, its worst reading in more than three years. The central bank has responded by cutting its 2026 growth forecast to a range of just 0.25–0.75%, a sharp comedown from Kast's campaign pledge of 4% annual growth, and has warned the weakness could persist into next year. One bright spot: lithium exports reached $3.2 billion in the first half of the year, almost triple 2025's pace, as Chile's status as the world's second-largest lithium producer keeps drawing investment despite softer global prices this month.

Military & Security

With its own defence budget squeezed by austerity, Chile is watching nervously as its neighbours rearm: a UPI dispatch on 18 September detailed Santiago's unease over rising military investment in Peru and Argentina, just as Finance Minister Jorge Quiroz caps 2027 spending growth at 1% and President Kast has warned the coming budget, due by month's end, will be "austere." The fiscal squeeze leaves little room for the kind of hardware purchases its neighbours are making, even as Chile keeps running its "Escudo Fronterizo" border-security build-out along the 1,000-kilometre frontier with Peru and Bolivia, layering trenches, fencing and surveillance technology to curb irregular crossings. Kast has leaned on that border crackdown and a homicide rate down 11.5% year-to-date as his clearest security wins, even as the broader public-security reform he campaigned on remains stalled in Congress.

Society & Culture

Chile marked 216 years of independence over the three-day Fiestas Patrias holiday from 18–20 September, with fondas and ramadas serving empanadas and asado from the Atacama Desert to the southern fjords and families dancing the cueca in city parks nationwide. President Kast led his first Great Military Parade at Parque O'Higgins, with the Army fielding 10,206 troops, its largest lineup in 17 years, and Gendarmería de Chile marching for the first time in its history with a 94-strong contingent. The festive mood sits alongside more contested memory politics: civil-society groups have petitioned the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights over what they call the government's quiet dismantling of transitional-justice and reparations institutions tied to the Pinochet dictatorship, a tension that resurfaced around this year's 11 September coup anniversary.

Tourism

Chile is on pace to extend its tourism momentum from 2025, when arrivals hit a post-2017 high of just over 6 million; officials now project international arrivals to again top 6 million in 2026, powered less by long-haul travel than by record regional demand from Argentina, Brazil, Bolivia and Peru. The sector is also getting new analytical infrastructure, with the tourism ministry unveiling a National Tourism Activity Index meant to sharpen investment decisions, visitor forecasting and destination planning across the industry. A visa-free travel policy now covering 94 countries, including the UAE, US and Japan, is helping widen the visitor base beyond Chile's traditional South American core.

Site to visit: Rapa Nui National Park, a UNESCO World Heritage Site covering most of Easter Island some 3,500km off mainland Chile, protects hundreds of monumental moai statues carved by the Rapa Nui people between roughly the 13th and 16th centuries. Multi-day park entry runs around €90 and requires an accredited local guide at every site, and the only way in is a roughly 5.5-hour direct flight from Santiago; the milder shoulder months of March–May and September–November are considered the best time to visit.

Science & Technology

Chile's astronomy infrastructure kept expanding its reach this month: ESO's Paranal Observatory and the ALMA array agreed a tenfold increase in data-transmission capacity with national research network REUNA, positioning both facilities to feed data to regional centres on three continents as ALMA upgrades toward 2030. The outreach side kept pace too, with thirty students from Toconao and San Pedro de Atacama joining a special Atacama Makers session at ALMA in early September as part of Chile's 2026 Astronomy Day programming. The push for data infrastructure dovetails with a resource boom next door: Chile's lithium output is forecast to rise 4.9% to 67,300 tonnes in 2026, with demand increasingly driven by the same AI models and data centres that astronomers now use to process their own growing flood of sky data.

🔥 Trending
Medio Oriente
Music / DIY punk-electronic collective · Santiago

Santiago's most talked-about musical export right now isn't a chart hit but Medio Oriente, a tight-knit record label and collective that The FADER has called "Chile's wildest post-genre electronic collective" after years spent soundtracking raves and two-stepping sessions in basements and abandoned lots around the capital. Founded in 2016 by producer José Pablo Peña, better known by his stage name Segunda Mordida, the label has grown into a scene unto itself, releasing digital-punk, breakbeat and ambient records from acts like C678924, DJ Daddy Yonqui, Arboretum and Monitos Chinos that channel the unresolved frustration of Chile's post-dictatorship generation. None of it has come through major labels or radio play: a second FADER feature in August and a Bandcamp Daily scene report have instead carried word of the movement out of Santiago's DIY clubs and onto the international music press almost entirely through word of mouth and Bandcamp streams.

Wednesday, September 23, 2026 · South America

Colombia

South America · population ~52M

Politics & Government

President Abelardo de la Espriella, the far-right former lawyer who took office on 7 August after narrowly beating Iván Cepeda, hardened his line on the ELN this week, rejecting the guerrilla group's proposed "hostage exchange" for the police officers and a civilian it still holds and demanding their immediate, unconditional release while ordering the armed forces to intensify search operations. His young government is also facing its first transparency fight: a Colombian judge this week ordered the Ministry of Defence to disclose full records of the president's flights after his early trips became the subject of public-spending questions. The scrutiny has not dented his standing so far — a CB Global Data survey put his positive image at 53.6% in mid-September, placing him third among Latin American leaders behind Mexico's Claudia Sheinbaum and El Salvador's Nayib Bukele, just over a month into his term.

Economy & Business

Vice President José Manuel Restrepo took Colombia's investment pitch to Wall Street this week, presenting a portfolio of 176 projects worth more than US$150 billion to over 60 investors in New York during UN General Assembly week, part of the government's "Misión Crecer" push to lift growth toward 6% through energy, infrastructure, agribusiness and AI ventures. Markets have been rewarding the optimism at home: the COLCAP index gained a fourth straight session on 22 September, up 2.6% over the week, as buyers rotated out of Ecopetrol and into telecoms, gas distribution and infrastructure stocks, while the peso held steady near 3,209 to the dollar. The broader recovery remains modest rather than dramatic, with GDP growing 2.2% year-on-year in the first quarter against elevated inflation near 5.9% and unemployment around 9%.

Military & Security

Security forces killed José Luis Pérez Villanueva, alias "Cholo," the second-in-command of the Autodefensas Conquistadoras de la Sierra Nevada, in a raid near Santa Marta on 21 September, a paramilitary group whose leadership had been designated a "peace manager" under the previous government; the operation triggered a retaliatory 48-hour armed strike that saw trucks and buses burned and merchants threatened across the city and along the Tayrona road. Days earlier, Medellín police arrested three men, including an army major, accused of brokering ties between ELN guerrillas and a FARC dissident faction that Antioquia's governor said had allied against the rival Clan del Golfo for control of illegal income streams. The crackdown follows President de la Espriella's decision earlier this month to shut the country's last remaining peace negotiating table and strip armed groups of their political status, reactivating arrest warrants that had been suspended for talks — a hardening stance now playing out in near-daily clashes across the north and northeast.

Society & Culture

Migration pressure from Venezuela has eased markedly this year: Migración Colombia recorded roughly 56,000 irregular-transit detections through mid-July, down almost 39% from the same period in 2025, even as the country continues hosting some three million Venezuelan refugees, by far the largest such population in the region. Culturally, the town of Tocancipá just wrapped its 26th Festival of Colombian Culture, drawing more than 4,000 performers and delegations from five other Latin American countries to mark the town's 433rd anniversary, while Medellín's 20th International Book and Culture Festival closed on 20 September with a free, ten-day programme built around the theme "Journeys Through Reading."

Tourism

Colombia is riding a genuine tourism wave into its 2026 high season: Lonely Planet named the country's Coffee Region, Tayrona National Natural Park, La Guajira, Bogotá and the Amazon among its must-visit South American destinations for 2026, while Cartagena separately landed a top-25 spot on the outlet's global Best in Travel list. International arrivals are climbing to match the attention, with travel from the US, Mexico, Brazil, Spain and Panama helping push growth of roughly 16.7% this year and Bogotá alone on pace for more than 15 million total travellers, an aviation and hotel boom that is reshaping the capital's short-term rental market.

Site to visit: The Coffee Cultural Landscape, a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 2011 centred on the postcard towns of Salento and Filandia in the Zona Cafetera, preserves a century-old tradition of small-farm coffee cultivation on steep Andean slopes alongside the towering wax palms of the nearby Cocora Valley. A traditional finca tour costs as little as 20,000–53,000 COP (roughly $5–13) including a tasting, reached by hourly jeeps from Salento's main square; the driest, clearest months from December to March and July to August are the easiest time to hike the Cocora trail, and the region is best accessed by flying into Armenia's airport before a short bus or taxi ride into town.

Science & Technology

President de la Espriella named Nubank founder and OpenAI board member David Vélez as an unpaid advisor this week to lead what the president called "the revolution of artificial intelligence in Colombia," tasking him with helping build a smaller, more efficient state, overhaul education for an AI-driven economy, and get social programmes to reach citizens more directly through technology. The appointment, agreed after a three-hour meeting between the two men in Barranquilla, comes with an explicit ambition: making Colombia the first Latin American country to be "native" to artificial intelligence. It builds on an already sizeable base, with the country's roughly 2,295 active startups still led by fintech even as SaaS, agritech, logistics and healthtech ventures gain ground in Bogotá and Medellín.

🔥 Trending
Alejo Portela
Music / vallenato · Bogotá (performing in New York City)

Alejo Portela is a vallenato musician, arranger and photographer from Bogotá who has spent recent years busking with his accordion in the New York City subway, trying to carry Colombia's signature folk genre to audiences who had never heard it. His moment came when a passer-by kicked his microphone to the ground mid-performance; rather than react, Portela kept playing, and a bystander's video of the incident spread far beyond Colombia, eventually racking up tens of millions of views (From Colombia to New York: How Alejo Portela Is Taking Vallenato Beyond Borders). What could have been a forgettable subway indignity instead turned into an organic international introduction to vallenato, with messages of support pouring in from as far as Africa, Australia and India, and Portela has kept performing and posting since, now with a global audience discovering the genre through him rather than through any label or campaign.
