Thursday, September 3, 2026 · Europe

United Kingdom

Europe · population ~68M

Politics & Government

Westminster returned from summer recess on Tuesday 1 September, and the week has been dominated by Andy Burnham's first full sitting weeks as prime minister following his arrival in Downing Street on 20 July. Burnham made his debut at the despatch box for Prime Minister's Questions on Wednesday, and his government has framed its programme around "rewiring the state" and pushing significantly greater powers out to local and regional authorities. The Hansard Society's parliamentary bulletin notes the Commons opened the term with a debate on strategic lawsuits against public participation (SLAPPs), while the Armed Forces Bill entered clause-by-clause scrutiny in Committee of the Whole House, with a second sitting due on 8 September. Fiscal detail remains the outstanding question: a budget statement is promised for October alongside a ten-year plan, and until then the legislative agenda is being read closely for signals about how the devolution push will be funded.

Economy & Business

Inflation is moving in the wrong direction as the new government settles in. CPI inflation reached 2.9% in July, up from 2.6% in June, and the Bank of England's central projection has it peaking near 3.2% in the fourth quarter, driven largely by energy costs linked to Middle East conflict. A second increase to the Ofgem household price cap is expected in October, which would add further pressure. The Monetary Policy Committee held Bank Rate at 3.75% at its July meeting, but the vote split three ways against, with a minority arguing for an immediate rise to 4.0% on fears that energy costs are feeding into broader prices. Both the August CPI release on 16 September and the MPC's decision the following day now sit as the month's key economic markers.

Military & Security

Counter-drone capability and allied technology partnerships have driven the week's defence news. Saab UK has delivered Giraffe 1X radar systems to the Royal Air Force for detecting and tracking uncrewed aerial systems on deployed operations, while Serco has taken a five-year Ministry of Defence contract to keep the early warning radar at RAF Fylingdales in North Yorkshire running. On the international side, the UK has signed an AI cooperation agreement with Ukraine covering joint development of future military capabilities, and Defence Readiness and Industry Minister Luke Pollard has opened an Indo-Pacific visit centred on radar technology cooperation with Australia. Separately, high-level talks have begun between British and Irish officials on Ireland joining the ten-nation Joint Expeditionary Force, and a review of the Stratus long-range missile programme run with France and Italy is due to report this month.

Society & Culture

Industrial action across the health service has escalated through the start of September. Estates workers at South London and Maudsley NHS Trust resumed an on-call ban on 1 September, with picket lines at Denmark Hill running 2–4 September after 97% of members backed the action in a pay dispute. Health visitors in Tower Hamlets closed their own strike ballot on 2 September, arguing over the retention of full NHS Agenda for Change pay and conditions. Hundreds of health workers also rallied outside Parliament against what union leaders characterised as a return to austerity in NHS funding. Elsewhere in the system, NHS leaders have warned that autism and ADHD assessment pathways are close to collapse under spiralling costs.

Tourism

VisitBritain's most recent published outlook forecasts 45.5 million inbound visits in 2026 spending £35.7 billion, growth of roughly 4% in volume and 7% in nominal spend over 2025, which would put visitor numbers at 105% of 2019 levels though real-terms spending still trails at about 96%. Long-haul markets were projected to grow fastest at 5% by volume and 8% by value, with European markets close behind. That forecast was compiled in January and explicitly excludes the effects of the Iran conflict; flight bookings have been tracking down year-on-year since February with sharp declines from Middle Eastern origin markets and softening demand from India, and VisitBritain has flagged that its mid-year update is likely to revise the figure downward. The Great Britain Tourism Survey covering January to June is the next official data point on domestic travel patterns.

Site to visit: The Giant's Causeway on the north Antrim coast is a UNESCO World Heritage Site made up of roughly 40,000 interlocking basalt columns formed by volcanic activity around 60 million years ago, and it remains Northern Ireland's most visited natural attraction. The stones themselves are free to walk on at any time of year; the charge applies only to the National Trust Visitor Experience. Adult admission runs about £13.50 and includes reserved parking, hourly guided tours, an audio guide in eleven languages and access to the exhibition, with online pre-booking recommended year-round and essential in summer.

Science & Technology

UK Research and Innovation opened funding calls on 1 September for collaborative projects integrating quantum computing into high-performance computational workflows, aimed at broadening practical user access rather than pure research. This sits inside UKRI's newly published five-year strategy, which commits to supporting more than 20,000 researchers across AI, quantum and other frontier technologies and to deploying the government's £38.6 billion research and innovation settlement. On the health research side, UK-run large-scale clinical trials have reported results for a single blood test capable of detecting around 50 cancer types before symptoms appear, one of the more consequential diagnostic developments of the year. Between the quantum funding round and the diagnostics work, the through-line this month is translation — moving lab results into deployable systems.

WEDNESDAY, SEPTEMBER 2, 2026 · SOUTH AMERICA

Argentina

South America · population ~46M

Politics & Government

President Javier Milei's legislative machine kept grinding through late August, with the Chamber of Deputies approving a new charter for the Central Bank on August 26 by 144 votes to 102, barring the monetary authority from financing the fiscal deficit through money creation and restoring currency-value preservation as its sole mandate; the bill now awaits an unscheduled Senate vote. Deputies also cleared accession to the Patent Cooperation Treaty on August 27, though a reservation attached to Chapter Two sends it back to the upper house. The momentum traces to La Libertad Avanza's landslide in the October 2025 midterms, which followed February's contentious labour reform package that cleared the Senate. Analysts nonetheless note softening approval numbers and increasingly public disputes inside the governing coalition, with speculation already circulating about alternative candidates on the right.

Economy & Business

The disinflation story has stalled rather than reversed: annual inflation ticked up to 33.8% in July from 33.5% in June, a vast improvement on the 211% peak but well short of the government's targets, with monthly readings stuck near 2.9%. Growth remains the brighter side of the ledger — the economy expanded 4.4% in 2025 and forecasters cluster around 3–4% for 2026, powered by energy and agribusiness, while poverty fell to 28.2% in the second half of 2025, its lowest since 2018. The central bank hit its US$10.1 billion dollar-accumulation goal in the first half and has loosened the peso's trading band to keep building reserves. The overhang is roughly US$20 billion in maturities falling due this year against uneven sectoral growth and minimal job creation.

Military & Security

Argentina is rebuilding an air-defence capability it effectively lost when the Mirage fleet retired in 2015. The first six operational F-16s arrived in December 2025 with US support, and six more are due each December through 2028 for an eventual fleet of 24. Flight operations began at Área Material Río IV while Tandil Air Base — the jets' future home — finishes modernisation; a dedicated maintenance and instruction centre opened at Tandil in March, and the 2026 budget carries a 892.5-billion-peso comprehensive security and defence system across both bases. The air force is targeting initial operational capability by year's end. Defence analysts read the purchase as much as a geopolitical signal of alignment with Washington as a hardware upgrade.

Society & Culture

Austerity is colliding hardest with the public sector's classrooms and clinics. Argentina's public higher-education system went dark for a 24-hour shutdown on August 12, called by the FEDUN and FATUN unions over salaries, budget shortfalls and the disputed University Financing Law, with a 48-hour escalation threatened for August 27–28 absent a deal. Researchers have mounted a fresh wave of protests at the Obelisk over unrestored scholarships and salaries the government was required to update under a 2025 law. On the health side, unions have marched against hospital service cuts, medicine shortages and vaccine disruptions, and Argentina's withdrawal from the WHO has drawn sustained criticism from public-health scholars.

Tourism

Inbound travel is running well ahead of last year, with Argentina receiving 4.79 million non-resident visitors in the first half of 2026, up 8.8% year on year and six consecutive months of growth. Brazil remains the dominant source market at roughly 30% of arrivals, followed by Chile, with North American and European numbers climbing on better air connectivity. Buenos Aires posted hotel occupancy between 62% and 75% over the half, helped by a premium-travel push built around its Michelin-recognised dining scene, while Mendoza wine country, Córdoba and Salta all logged gains. Patagonia is the standout: Los Glaciares National Park recorded its highest visitor numbers in years. The one sour note is the outbound imbalance — favourable rates abroad still send more Argentines out than foreigners in.

Site to visit: The Perito Moreno Glacier, in Los Glaciares National Park near El Calafate in Santa Cruz province, is a 250 km² ice mass roughly 70 metres tall at its face and one of the few advancing glaciers on earth, famous for the thunderous calving that drops apartment-sized blocks into Lago Argentino. A network of steel walkways puts visitors within a few hundred metres of the ice, and boat trips and ice trekking are sold separately. As of August 2026 park entry runs about ARS 50,000 for foreign visitors and ARS 25,000 for Argentines, bookable online up to a month ahead, with a 50% discount on a second visit within 72 hours.

Science & Technology

Resource technology is where Argentina's capital is going. Neuquén province has opened its largest Vaca Muerta acreage offering since 2016, putting 15 exploration blocks on the market at once to draw foreign operator expertise, with the shale play forecast to generate roughly US$18 billion in annual exports and a US$12.5 billion energy trade surplus. Vaca Muerta now funds about a third of Neuquén's wage bill. On the lithium side, IDB Invest has committed up to US$700 million to the Sal de Oro project in Salta, targeting some 48,000 tonnes of lithium products a year and around 620 permanent jobs, while Catamarca's Sal de Vida, Fénix and HMW phases advance under a new ten-year national lithium plan. Sustainability remains the open question, with outside researchers pressing water and emissions strategies on the basin's operators.

The appeal is partly that she keeps breaking the genre's boys'-club default without softening the guitars.

Monday, August 24, 2026 · South America

Chile

South America · population ~19M

Politics & Government

President José Antonio Kast's administration, now roughly five months into its term, remains dominated by the aftermath of the catastrophic July storms and by a hard-line security agenda. Sixty deputies have formally petitioned the president to grant individual pardons to Carabineros and armed-forces personnel convicted over conduct during the October 2019 protests, a request that revives one of the most divisive fault lines in Chilean politics. Kast has meanwhile pushed his security doctrine outward, arguing at regional forums that Latin American states must adopt life sentences for gang leaders and total isolation regimes in prisons to stop criminal organisations operating from behind bars. On the international stage, former president Michelle Bachelet placed fourth in the UN Security Council's first informal straw poll for the succession to Secretary-General António Guterres, keeping her candidacy alive but well behind the front-runners. Analysts continue to characterise 2026 as a pivot year for Chilean governance, with the balance between security, austerity and social spending the defining question of the term.

Economy & Business

Chile's Congress approved Kast's flagship economic package on 4 August, a sweeping bill of corporate tax cuts and investment incentives intended to revive growth, though Finance Minister Jorge Quiroz has signalled the rollout will be delayed while the government vetoes provisions added in debate and other measures go to constitutional review. Copper remains the fiscal engine: Cochilco has lifted its 2026 price forecast to US$5.95 a pound from US$5.55 in May, even as it cut the output projection to 5.27 million tonnes, 2.6% below 2025, on a weak first half at El Teniente and Quebrada Blanca. In a first for the state miner, the government on 10 August allowed Codelco to retain and capitalise the full US$2.42 billion of its 2025 profits rather than remit them to the treasury, easing pressure on a balance sheet carrying a US$21.65 billion project pipeline. Lithium is the other bright spot, with SQM posting record quarterly sales above 84,000 tonnes and Salar de Atacama expansion expected to lift national output to about 67,300 tonnes this year.

Military & Security

The centrepiece of Kast's security programme is the "Border Shield Plan", an effort to seal the roughly 1,000-kilometre northern frontier with Peru and Bolivia using physical barriers and surveillance technology; the government reports about US$18.5 million invested and an 89% increase in military and police presence, alongside a claimed 95% fall in clandestine entries in the first half of 2026. Chile has also deepened regional counter-crime architecture, leading the Santiago Regional Commitment signed in May with Argentina, Bolivia, Ecuador and Peru on intelligence sharing and joint judicial action, and sealing a bilateral "strategic security alliance" with Javier Milei's Argentina in April. In the south, the military continues to provide logistical and security support to civilian authorities in Araucanía and Biobío, where a state of emergency first declared in 2022 is still renewed on a rolling 30-day basis amid politically motivated violence. Foreign analysts note the country retains a modest force of roughly 77,000 troops on a US$5 billion budget, making the border build-out a significant reallocation of scarce capability.

Society & Culture

Chile's social temperature has stayed high since Kast's first State of the Nation address, when unions and student federations clashed with police in Valparaíso accusing the government of dismantling public healthcare, education and social rights. Student marches in Santiago drew thousands and ended with 35 arrests and injuries from water cannon and tear gas, with parallel rallies in Antofagasta and Concepción. The flashpoint was Kast's declaration that "no one who burns a bus, no one who destroys public property deserves free education", a line critics read as making schooling revocable as punishment, and his National Reconstruction bill of spending cuts now working through the Senate. Overlaying all of it is the humanitarian toll of July's storms, which killed at least 13 people, destroyed or badly damaged more than 1,100 homes and left around 150,000 without power across ten of Chile's sixteen regions.

Tourism

Tourism is one of the few unambiguously good news stories in Chile right now. Sernatur and the Undersecretariat of Tourism report more than 3.1 million international visitors in the first six months of 2026, putting the country on track to clear six million arrivals for the year after roughly 5.8 million in 2025. Long-haul demand is growing fastest: Australian arrivals jumped 80.1% year-on-year in the first half, driven almost entirely by Patagonia itineraries. Chile also took the World Travel Awards' Leading Nature Destination and Leading Adventure Tourism Destination titles for 2026, and authorities have launched a new National Tourism Activity Index to give operators and investors standardised demand-forecasting data. August falls in the southern winter low season, when the Atacama and the central ski resorts carry most of the traffic and Patagonian lodges run reduced operations.

Site to visit: Torres del Paine National Park in Magallanes is Chile's signature wilderness destination — a UNESCO Biosphere Reserve of granite spires, turquoise glacial lakes and the Southern Patagonian Ice Field, best known for the multi-day "W" and "O" circuit treks. Entry must be booked in advance through CONAF's pasesparques.cl portal rather than paid at the gate, and international visitors pay roughly US$35 in high season (1 November–31 March) and about US$18 in low season. Right now is deep low season with short daylight and many refugios closed, so the shoulder months of November and March generally offer the best balance of access and crowd levels.

Science & Technology

Chile's Atacama skies continue to anchor roughly 70% of global astronomy investment, and 2026 has been a heavy build year. The Fred Young Submillimeter Telescope now stands near the summit of Cerro Chajnantor after a 34-year development effort led from Cornell, and is expected to become the world's fastest mapping instrument at submillimetre wavelengths. At Paranal, a Chilean-led construction consortium has begun foundation work on the southern array of the Cherenkov Telescope Array Observatory, with the first gamma-ray telescopes due for deployment before the end of the year. On the energy-technology side, the government has walked back its earlier green hydrogen ambitions, opening public consultation on a revised 2026–2030 strategy that lowers production targets and reorients toward domestic demand and derivative products rather than bulk export.

Friday, August 21, 2026 · Asia / Europe

Saudi Arabia

Asia · population ~37M

Politics & Government

Riyadh's foreign policy has pivoted sharply toward hard security this month, capped by a mutual defence pact signed in Mecca with Turkey and Pakistan that treats an attack on one signatory as an attack on all — an unusually explicit commitment for a kingdom that has historically preferred quiet hedging. The agreement followed a run of Iranian-aligned strikes on Saudi territory and drew two of the Muslim world's largest militaries into the Kingdom's defence perimeter. The Saudi foreign ministry has publicly condemned what it calls Iran's destabilising behaviour, while continuing to keep diplomatic channels with Tehran nominally open. Domestically, decision-making remains tightly centralised around Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman, whose authority the Bertelsmann Transformation Index's 2026 country report describes as essentially uncontested.

Economy & Business

The Kingdom posted its first quarterly contraction since 2023, with real GDP down 4.8 percent year-on-year in the second quarter as oil activity fell roughly 25 percent. The driver is regional rather than domestic: Aramco has cut production following disruption to the Strait of Hormuz, its principal export route, though the resulting spike in global crude prices has partly cushioned export and fiscal revenues. The non-oil economy is holding up considerably better, with growth in the 4.5–5.0 percent range on strong domestic demand and rising labour force participation, and the OECD's latest outlook still projects a recovery into 2027. With oil supplying roughly 60–70 percent of government revenue, the fiscal picture remains acutely sensitive to how long the Hormuz disruption lasts.

Military & Security

Saudi Arabia spent early August absorbing the widening Iran conflict directly, with Houthi forces claiming missile and drone attacks on Najran airport and Saudi positions in eastern Yemen, part of a broader escalation against Saudi military, infrastructure and shipping targets. Houthi spokesman Yahya Saree described the strikes as targeting troop concentrations, warehouses and vehicles; Saudi air defences have intercepted multiple ballistic missiles over the period. Intelligence assessments cited in regional reporting suggest further attacks on energy infrastructure, ports and airports may be planned under Iranian Revolutionary Guard supervision, involving both the Houthis and Iraqi armed groups. The Turkey–Pakistan defence pact is the Kingdom's most concrete institutional response so far.

Society & Culture

August's cultural calendar leans heavily into heritage-with-spectacle, with the International Falcon Breeders Auction running August 5–25 and drawing elite local and international breeders to live auctions, pavilions and interactive falconry exhibits, free with registration. The Ministry of Culture is simultaneously running open-air showcases of Saudi music, contemporary art and design, part of a deliberate push to give homegrown artists institutional footing rather than importing headline acts. That institutional layer keeps thickening — new galleries and performance spaces continue to open, and Sotheby's now holds sales in Riyadh, a meaningful signal for a domestic art market that barely existed a decade ago. The Kingdom's 13 regions retain distinct dialects and traditions, a diversity the culture ministry has begun foregrounding rather than flattening.

Tourism

Saudi Arabia has already blown past its original Vision 2030 target, recording roughly 30 million inbound international tourists and over 122 million total visitors in 2025, and has reset its ambition to 150 million by 2030. The growth story has shifted from religious travel toward leisure, pulled by the Red Sea coast, AlUla and a year-round events calendar that now includes the Saudi Grand Prix and Riyadh Season. AlUla in particular is being repositioned as an open-air museum where archaeology, land art and contemporary culture overlap, with the 2026 AlUla Arts Festival expanding its programme of installations. The obvious headwind is regional: with Houthi strikes hitting airports and shipping, several origin markets are watching travel advisories closely.

Site to visit: Hegra, in the AlUla region, is Saudi Arabia's first UNESCO World Heritage Site — a Nabataean city of more than 100 monumental rock-cut tombs, essentially Petra's southern sibling, but with a fraction of the crowds. Self-driving is not permitted and daily tour numbers are capped, so pre-booking is essential; guided tours start around SAR 95 and run about two hours, with sunrise slots the best bet for both light and heat.

Science & Technology

The Kingdom has formally declared 2026 its Year of Artificial Intelligence, backed by a roughly $9.1 billion national strategy and the launch of state-backed AI champion HUMAIN. The most concrete piece is at NEOM, where construction is due to start within months on a 1.5-gigawatt net-zero AI computing campus at Oxagon, a $5 billion first phase targeting 2028 operations that could become one of the world's largest AI data centre complexes. Microsoft's Azure region for the Kingdom is slated to go live in Q4, supported by three Eastern Province data centres and over $2 billion in infrastructure spending. On the research side, the Shaheen III supercomputer at KAUST anchors national HPC capacity, while Riyadh hosts the UNESCO-sponsored International Center for AI Research and Ethics ahead of the fourth Global AI Summit in September.

Russia

Europe · population ~144M (transcontinental)

Politics & Government

With State Duma elections set for 18–20 September, the Kremlin has been clearing the field. Yabloko figure Lev Shlosberg was sentenced to 11 years and one month in prison, a term upheld at the Supreme Court, in what observers read as punishment for the party's continued campaigning under an explicit "For Peace!" slogan. The KPRF, LDPR and Just Russia continue to occupy their managed role as loyal opposition — permitted seats, no genuine challenge to United Russia. But the Carnegie Endowment reports that Kremlin strategists privately worry that voters treat parliamentary elections as one of the few legitimate outlets for frustration, and that even Putin supporters may cast protest votes against the ruling party amid economic strain and war fatigue.

Economy & Business

The Bank of Russia cut its key rate a further 25 basis points to 14.00 percent on 18 August, continuing a cautious easing cycle down from the two-decade high of 21 percent, but the accompanying guidance was bleak. Policymakers downgraded 2026 GDP growth to a range of 0 to 1 percent, down from 0.5–1.5 percent, while simultaneously raising its inflation forecast — an uncomfortable combination that points toward stagflation rather than a soft landing. A significant part of the inflation risk is now physical: repeated Ukrainian long-range drone strikes on oil refineries and commercial infrastructure have created localised fuel shortages and supply bottlenecks that monetary policy cannot fix.

Military & Security

The battlefield picture has inverted from earlier in the war. Over the four weeks to 18 August, DeepState OSINT data indicates Russian forces suffered a net loss of about 12 square miles of Ukrainian territory, with the advance that characterised 2024–25 having stalled. Ukraine has meanwhile pushed the war deep into Russian territory, launching hundreds of drones in a single night — 201 downed over the Moscow region alone by official count — killing at least six people and striking a Wildberries logistics hub. A senior Ukrainian negotiator argues Russia is running out of options, while US-mediated talks have stalled since early this year, with territorial concessions and security guarantees still the unbridged gaps. Speculation about a fresh partial mobilisation — politically toxic ahead of September's vote — continues to circulate in Moscow.

Society & Culture

Polling ahead of the Duma vote paints a society out of step with its government: Russians broadly want the war to end, are anxious about the economy and oppose censorship, yet no party permitted on the ballot can offer a change of course. The prosecution of Yabloko's leadership has made the boundaries explicit, and Freedom House's 2026 assessment records continued contraction of independent media and civil society space. Cultural life continues in the gaps: the recent discovery of relics attributed to the medieval warrior-prince Dmitry Donskoy in the Kremlin was given heavy state media play, fitting neatly into an official historical narrative that emphasises martial continuity and defensive struggle.

Tourism

Russia remains open to tourists and reporting from the ground suggests a modest uptick in Western visitors to Moscow and St Petersburg in 2026, though the practical friction is substantial. The 30-day e-visa now covers all EU and Schengen states plus much of Asia and the Gulf — roughly $52, processed in about four days — but US, Canadian, British and Australian passport holders remain excluded and must obtain a traditional embassy visa with a letter of invitation. Once inside, foreign Visa, Mastercard and Amex cards do not work, so visitors need cash or a locally issued MIR card, and mandatory medical insurance applies for the full stay. Most Western social platforms are blocked, and Western governments including the US State Department maintain strong advisories against travel.

Site to visit: The State Hermitage Museum in St Petersburg occupies the Winter Palace and four adjoining buildings along the Neva, holding roughly three million objects — one of the largest collections on earth, assembled from Catherine the Great's purchases onward. The scale defeats a single visit, so most visitors pick two or three galleries rather than attempting the whole thing; going on a weekday morning shortly after opening is the reliable way to see the Rembrandt and Italian rooms before tour groups arrive.

Science & Technology

Roscosmos is pushing an Arctic satellite constellation of nine spacecraft — four for broadband internet, three for radar surveillance and two for hydrometeorology — including Arktika-M weather satellites and Kondor-FKA and Obzor-R radar platforms capable of imaging through cloud and darkness. The Arctic focus is not incidental: it combines civilian navigation and weather needs along the Northern Sea Route with clear military surveillance utility. A revised national space programme worth roughly 4.4 trillion rubles targets a domestic satellite communications group and a return to the top three globally by launch count, with commercial remote-sensing data services due to begin this autumn. Against that, Russian space science faces a widening multi-year gap, and timelines for the post-ISS national station have already slipped toward 2028 or later.

Ay Yola and the Bashkir folk-electronic wave
Music · Ufa, Bashkortostan

Ay Yola is a Bashkir-language trio formed in Ufa in 2024 — vocalist and dombra player Adel Shayhitdinova, veteran electronic producer Ruslan Shayhitdinov, and quray player Rinat Ramazanov of the ethno-group Argymak — whose debut single "Homay" became an unlikely international hit, charting across Kazakhstan, Turkey, Uzbekistan, Kyrgyzstan and Turkmenistan. The band's name roughly translates as "code of the universe." Notably, the wave has not been uncomplicated at home — some Bashkir listeners have questioned the group's handling of the source material, a debate about who gets to modernise a minority tradition that mirrors arguments happening far beyond Russia.

Thursday, August 20, 2026 · Europe / Asia

Ukraine

Europe · population ~44M (pre-war estimate)

Politics & Government

Domestic politics this week has been dominated by an anti-corruption investigation reaching into the Presidential Office, with searches conducted on August 19 involving a deputy head of the office and a group linked to presidential officials — the latest test of the independence of Ukraine's anti-graft agencies. The reshuffle at the top of the security establishment continued as a new defence minister took office while his ousted predecessor publicly called for wartime elections to be held. That argument remains a minority position: polling reported by Ukrainian outlets suggests only about 15% of Ukrainians want a vote before the war ends, with roughly 57% preferring to wait. On the diplomatic track, the foreign ministers of Ukraine, Romania and Moldova have been coordinating after repeated Russian violations of neighbouring airspace, with joint countermeasures slated for discussion at the Odesa Triangle summit.

Economy & Business

The State Statistics Service reported that real GDP grew 0.6% year-on-year in the second quarter of 2026, a modest return to growth after a 0.5% contraction in Q1 caused largely by Russian strikes on energy infrastructure and the resulting winter electricity shortages. Forecasts for the full year diverge sharply: the EBRD expects 2.5% growth and judges macroeconomic stability to be holding, while the Cabinet has cut its own projection to 1.6% and the OECD sees just 1.0%. Business activity in July was constrained by heavier shelling of critical infrastructure, logistics bottlenecks, exchange-rate expectations and acute staff shortages, and Ukrzaliznytsia raised freight tariffs 30% from August 1 to offset falling volumes and rising operating costs. Defence manufacturing remains the one sector posting sustained expansion.

Military & Security

Ukrainian officials on August 12 published results of the Oleksandrivka-direction counteroffensive conducted between January and August 2026, which analysts read as evidence that Ukrainian forces can still generate meaningful tactical gains and reintroduce limited mechanised manoeuvre on localised stretches of the front. The long-range strike campaign continued in parallel, with the General Staff reporting hits on the Syvash railway bridge near Chonhar, a rail crossing near Vladyslavivka and a Black Sea Fleet signals-intelligence detachment south of Sevastopol. Russian forces struck the Nikopol district of Dnipropetrovsk Oblast more than 20 times on August 19 with drones and artillery, damaging an administrative building and an enterprise and injuring one person. A prisoner exchange this period returned 103 Ukrainian POWs.

Society & Culture

A cluster of administrative changes took effect this month covering pension legislation, tax reporting, employee retention rules and inclusive education, alongside the national "Serpneva 2026" education conference held on August 18. Veteran support was in focus as the Ukrainian Red Cross began transferring one-time grants of up to 95,000 hryvnias from August 1 to service members blinded in the war, earmarked for household appliances and assistive devices. On the cultural front, UNESCO announced more than €2.5 million in additional support, in cooperation with the EU and Flanders, to safeguard documentary heritage and sustain the research sector through remote laboratory access. A therapeutic arts camp and training programme is also running through August under the Healing Arts Ukraine initiative, feeding recommendations to three ministries on arts-based psychosocial care.

Tourism

Tourism remains a narrow but resilient sector, concentrated in the western oblasts and the Carpathians where infrastructure is functioning and safety ratings for 2026 are highest, though no region is entirely free of missile risk. Ukrainian airspace stays closed to commercial aviation, so all arrivals come overland from Poland, Slovakia, Hungary, Romania or Moldova. Lviv region this summer unveiled a guidebook highlighting 25 key destinations for 2026, aimed at both domestic travellers and the small but steady flow of foreign visitors — largely solidarity travellers, heritage tourists following church and Jewish heritage routes, and journalists. Mountain resorts, historic city centres and national parks are the main draws.

Site to visit: Kamianets-Podilskyi Fortress, perched on a rock island in a loop of the Smotrych River in Khmelnytskyi Oblast, is one of Eastern Europe's most dramatic defensive complexes, blending Gothic, Renaissance and Baroque construction across eleven towers. It draws more than 200,000 visitors a year, who can walk the curtain walls, climb the towers and tour the on-site history museum. It is reachable by bus from Lviv in roughly three to four hours, making it a feasible long day trip or overnight stop.

Science & Technology

Ukraine's drone sector continues to set the pace of its wartime innovation, with the defence ministry approving a record 413 new unmanned systems so far in 2026 — more than 30% above the same period in 2025 and 73% above 2024. Production volumes are equally striking, with output running at roughly half a million small FPV attack drones a month and a projected six to seven million for the year, alongside a newly developed guided munition for heavy drones announced in early August. That expertise has become an export asset in its own right: analysts describe Kyiv as a globally coveted hub for drone and counter-drone know-how, with Western militaries studying a production gap they have yet to close. Emerging design work points toward mesh-networked swarms and radar-evading bio-inspired airframes.

🔥 Trending
Ovul
Tech / Femtech · Lviv & Kyiv

Ovul is a Ukrainian femtech startup that has built a home fertility and hormonal-health tracker working essentially as a pocket digital microscope: users deposit a saliva sample, and an AI model reads the crystallisation patterns to detect estrogen shifts and identify the fertile window. It won first place in the general category of the Startup Competition at IT Arena in Lviv, taking a $15,000 grant, and was subsequently picked by Ukraine's Ministry of Digital Transformation to represent the country's innovation at CES 2026. Its traction says something about where Ukrainian consumer tech is heading — away from the war-adjacent categories that dominate headlines and toward health hardware built for a domestic market with limited clinic access. The wider ecosystem it emerged from is still centred on Kyiv, with Lviv steadily gaining ground as a second hub.

Turkey

Asia · population ~86M

Politics & Government

Turkish politics remains locked in a confrontation between the government and the main opposition Republican People's Party (CHP), whose leader Özgür Özel accuses the administration of using the judiciary to break his resistance to a widening crackdown on opposition-run municipalities. A court ruling in May effectively removed the CHP's elected leadership, a decision analysts at the Washington Institute called among the most consequential in modern Turkish political history, and one that triggered street protests met with tear gas and water cannon. The campaign follows the March 2025 jailing of Istanbul mayor Ekrem İmamoğlu, President Erdoğan's principal rival. With Erdoğan weighing a third term, observers describe the country as approaching an inflection point, with the state increasingly reshaping rather than merely repressing the opposition.

Economy & Business

The central bank raised its year-end inflation projection to 28% from 26% at a presentation by Governor Fatih Karahan in Istanbul on August 13, citing higher energy prices, revised lira import-price assumptions and stronger-than-expected underlying inflation. Annual CPI eased marginally to 31.75% in July from 32.11% in June, roughly in line with expectations but far above target. The Monetary Policy Committee has held the one-week repo rate at 37%, with overnight lending and borrowing at 40% and 35.5%, pledging to maintain a tight stance until price stability is restored. The lira meanwhile continues to grind lower, trading near 47.96 to the dollar on August 20 — down about 1.6% over the month and more than 17% over the past year.

Military & Security

The most significant security development in years took formal effect this week as Turkey's PKK peace process law was published in the Official Gazette. The 12-article Law on Strengthening National Solidarity and Social Integration passed parliament on August 10 by 467 votes to 87 with seven abstentions, and is the most concrete legal step yet in an initiative launched in late 2024 to end a four-decade conflict that has cost more than 40,000 lives. Its relief provisions apply only once authorities confirm that the group and its affiliates have ceased activity and surrendered all weapons. President Erdoğan called the vote a "historic consensus," crediting his far-right MHP ally Devlet Bahçeli, who helped initiate the process. The International Crisis Group notes implementation and verification remain the hard part.

Society & Culture

Civic space has narrowed further in 2026, with monitors reporting that authorities continued to suppress freedom of assembly through police force, blanket protest bans and forced closures of civil society organisations — particularly groups working on LGBTI+ and women's rights. The prosecutions of opposition figures sparked large, youth-led protests nationwide that were met with mass arbitrary detentions and excessive force, according to documentation of the 2025–2026 protest wave. Human Rights Watch's World Report 2026 catalogues continued democratic backsliding, including prosecutions of journalists, lawyers and human rights defenders. More than 1,500 foundations and associations have been shut down since 2016.

Tourism

Turkey ranks as the world's fourth most-visited country in 2026 with roughly 60.6 million international visitors, trailing only France, Spain and the United States, and the government is targeting $68 billion in tourism earnings. The headline figure masks a softening trend, however: foreign arrivals fell 9.44% year-on-year to 3.53 million in April and 4.02% to 5.54 million in June, with Antalya and Bodrum feeling the squeeze from rising costs and political tension. Istanbul has been the standout, drawing 1.47 million foreign visitors in April to overtake Antalya as the leading gateway; across the year Antalya and Istanbul still account for about 36% and 30% of arrivals respectively. The early clustering of Ramadan, Eid al-Fitr and Nowruz pulled demand forward into the first quarter this year.

Site to visit: Cappadocia, the UNESCO-listed volcanic landscape of Göreme in central Anatolia, is famous for its fairy-chimney rock formations, Byzantine cave churches and the sunrise balloon flights that have become the country's most photographed experience. Spring is generally considered the best window — mild 10–20°C mornings, wildflowers and cancellation rates of only 15–20% — while peak-season flights in May, June and September run roughly €250–450 per person versus €80–140 in winter. Book at least two months ahead for any date between April and November.

Science & Technology

Turkey's space programme is having a visible year. TÜBİTAK's Space Technologies Research Institute showcased its RASAT, GÖKTÜRK-2, İMECE and TÜRKSAT 6A satellite capabilities at SAHA EXPO 2026, while the National Space Program advances the Lunar Research Program (AYAP), which aims first to survey the Moon from orbit and make initial surface contact, then to soft-land a rover. Turkish Space Agency president Yusuf Kıraç was unanimously elected to lead the APSCO Council for 2026–2027, the first Turkish official to head the organisation. Antalya will host the International Astronautical Congress this year, which has drawn over 8,330 abstracts from 108 countries and expects more than 10,000 delegates. In industry, domestic EV maker TOGG continues to pull local suppliers and startups into its supply chain.

A remix of Istanbul rapper Blok3's "Napıyosun Mesela" by producer Taha OwlMan has become one of the most-used Turkish sounds on short-form video this year, spreading well beyond Turkey's borders through TikTok's Turkish-trending feeds. Wednesday, August 19, 2026 · Africa

Nigeria

Africa · population ~225M

Politics & Government

Nigeria formally opened presidential campaigning on Wednesday, setting up a January 16 vote in which President Bola Tinubu seeks re-election against 18 declared challengers. Analysts expect a three-man contest, with former Vice President Atiku Abubakar now running under the African Democratic Congress banner and Peter Obi — whose youth-driven 2023 run reshaped the last election — standing for the Nigeria Democratic Congress. Tinubu enters the race with a formidable structural advantage after a wave of defections left 31 of Nigeria's 36 state governors inside his All Progressives Congress, though critics argue the machine masks genuine unpopularity among young voters. The president's prompt assent to the Electoral Bill 2026 has meanwhile shaped the legal terrain the campaign will be fought on, and he has paired the campaign launch with a ₦58.18 trillion appropriation bill promising tighter budget discipline. Gubernatorial races follow on February 6.

Economy & Business

The macro picture is improving on paper but remains painful at household level: headline inflation eased to 15.91% in June, a dramatic retreat from the 30%-plus peaks of 2024, yet food prices accelerated for a fourth straight month and transport costs climbed as a March fuel-price shock continued to feed through. The naira has settled into a broad ₦1,410–1,519/USD band, supported by higher reserves and returning portfolio flows, while GDP growth is tracking around 3.8–4.6% on the back of policy consistency and steadier commodity prices. That divergence — stabilising aggregates alongside the worst cost-of-living squeeze in a generation — is the central economic fact of the campaign now underway. Analysts at CFG Advisory have cautioned that inflation could yet drift back toward 20% before the year closes.

Military & Security

Security has deteriorated sharply enough to become an election issue in its own right. On August 19, gunmen killed 32 worshippers at a mosque in Unguwan Mantau village, Malumfashi LGA, Katsina State — the latest in a pattern that produced 599 banditry incidents and 2,742 casualties nationwide in 2025, roughly double the prior year. An estimated 30,000 armed gang members now operate across largely unpoliced stretches of the north, burning villages and displacing farming communities. More alarming to defence analysts is convergence between criminal and jihadist networks: Borno-based Boko Haram (JAS) has reportedly supplied equipment, advisers and bombmakers to bandit allies in Kaduna, and several northwestern groups claim affiliations with JAS, ISWAP and al-Qaida-linked outfits in the Sahel. Human Rights Watch continues to document abuses on multiple sides of the conflict.

Society & Culture

The university sector is heading for another confrontation: ASUU has called nationwide campus protests for August 26, with the Federal University of Technology Akure branch describing federal neglect of universities as "wicked and heartless." The union's grievances are long-running — renegotiation of the 2009 ASUU–FGN agreement, sustainable university funding, and unpaid entitlements for retired academics — but the timing places them squarely inside the campaign season. On health, commentators marked mid-August with warnings that Nigeria's health system stands at a crossroads and needs urgent structural reform, while vaccination teams have been pushing polio campaigns deeper into northern communities where insecurity complicates access. Local grievances are also surfacing directly, with residents of Ofugo protesting over infrastructure neglect earlier this month.

Tourism

Nigeria is leaning hard into diaspora and carnival tourism. The 2026 season has produced a record volume of visa-on-arrival applications from the Americas and Europe, driven largely by Brazilian and Caribbean travellers tracing ancestral roots — a flow the April Lagos Fanti Carnival at Tafawa Balewa Square, built around Afro-Brazilian returnee heritage, drew some 40,000 participants to. Lagos will follow in September with the inaugural week-long Victoria Island Carnival, pairing parades and live music with fashion, food and art programming. Improved connectivity through Murtala Muhammed International Airport and the Lagos Blue Line rail have made the city measurably easier to move around, though the dry season from November to April remains the practical window for travel beyond Lagos.

Site to visit: The Osun-Osogbo Sacred Grove in Osun State is a dense riverside forest studded with shrines, sculptures and art installations dedicated to Osun, the Yoruba goddess of fertility — the last intact grove of its kind and a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 2005. Much of its modern sculptural landscape was created by Austrian-Nigerian artist Susanne Wenger and local collaborators from the 1950s onward, making it as much a living art project as a religious site. Entry is inexpensive (roughly ₦500–1,000, with a small extra camera fee) and includes a guide; the annual Osun-Osogbo festival in August draws the largest crowds.

South Africa

Africa · population ~61M

Politics & Government

President Cyril Ramaphosa has set 4 November 2026 as the date for local government elections, with roughly 29 million South Africans now registered and 2.1 million names added to the roll since the 2024 general election. The vote will be the first real electoral test of the Government of National Unity, the ANC–DA coalition that has held together longer than most analysts predicted when it formed. Ramaphosa also spent this week on the regional stage, accepting the SADC chairship on 17 August at the bloc's 46th summit in Durban. Municipal governance — water, electricity and refuse collection in failing metros — is expected to dominate the campaign rather than national coalition politics.

Economy & Business

South Africa's recovery remains real but shallow. Unemployment climbed to 33.6% in the second quarter, up from 32.7% in Q1 and the highest reading since mid-2022, with the number of jobless rising 4.2% to 8.48 million as employment edged down. Against that, GDP growth is projected at about 1.2% this year, supported by steadier electricity supply, firm mining output and slow-moving reforms in energy, logistics and water. The clearest good news came in June, when Fitch upgraded the sovereign to BB with a stable outlook, citing fiscal consolidation and an improved debt trajectory — the first ratings upgrade in years, and a signal that markets now see the GNU's fiscal stance as credible even if the labour market does not reflect it.

Military & Security

Police operations remain the government's most visible security instrument: Operation Shanela accounted for 16,208 arrests of undocumented foreign nationals last month alone, bringing the year-to-date figure to 59,947, alongside seizures of illegal firearms and drugs. NATJOINTS activated its election security plan for the 1–2 August voter registration weekend and reports the exercise passed without major incident, with a fuller operational plan now in place for November. The bigger story is institutional: the Madlanga Commission into political interference and corruption within the police and criminal justice system has recommended criminal and disciplinary action against suspended SAPS deputy national commissioner Shadrack Sibiya. That follows Ramaphosa's earlier decision to deploy the army in support of police against organised crime gangs, a move defence analysts have questioned on effectiveness grounds.

Society & Culture

Mental health has moved up the schools agenda: KwaZulu-Natal education MEC Sipho Hlomuka has urged schools to build mental health awareness into their culture, warning that mounting pressure on learners is feeding disengagement and dropout, and launching a Digital School Mental Health Pilot Programme with partner Cloud Therapist. In public health, a South African dermatologist has been appointed to lead a new International League of Dermatological Societies campaign against skin-lightening practices, which the body classes as a significant and under-regulated public health risk. Continentally, health regulators and educators have agreed a shared framework of Africa Health Professions Education Quality Standards. On lighter ground, Varsity Football returns this month with Friday-night men's fixtures and the women's championship opening its season.

Tourism

Tourism is the clearest bright spot in the economy: international arrivals passed 5.58 million in the first half of 2026, up 12.3% year on year, with April alone bringing close to a million visitors. Visitors are also staying far longer — average stays have stretched from 9.4 to 14.2 nights as "slow travel," remote work and study programmes reshape itineraries. Visa liberalisation has been the single biggest lever, with visa-free entry now extended to travellers from 46 countries, while infrastructure has caught up: cruise berth capacity at Cape Town's V&A Waterfront has doubled to 7,500 simultaneous disembarking passengers and Skukuza Airport's extended runway can now handle A330 long-haul services into Kruger. Lodges along the path of the 28 November total solar eclipse over the Eastern Cape report exceptionally strong advance bookings.

Site to visit: Robben Island, seven kilometres off Cape Town in Table Bay, held Nelson Mandela for 18 of his 27 years in prison and is now a UNESCO World Heritage Site whose prison tours are led by former political prisoners. The standard visit runs roughly 3.5–4 hours and bundles the return ferry, an island bus tour of the leper colony and lime quarry, and the maximum-security block walkthrough. Ferries leave the V&A Waterfront at 09:00, 11:00, 13:00 and 15:00 — book well ahead, as sailings are weather-dependent and frequently sell out; tickets run about R600 for non-South African adults and R400 for SA citizens.
